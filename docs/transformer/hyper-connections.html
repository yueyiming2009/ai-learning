Rewiring the Residual Stream

Hyper-connections, DeepSeek's Sinkhorn-constrained mHC, Kimi's Attention Residuals, and Qwen's Gated Residual: three frontier labs replaced 2017's uniform residual sum in the same half-year — two with the same underlying constraint in different clothes, the third by deleting the operator that needed constraining.

The residual stream is the transformer's least-questioned component: every sublayer computes something and adds it to a running vector, \(x \mathrel{+}= f(x)\), 61 or 93 times in a row. It is a gradient highway, it needs no parameters, and it has been untouched through every attention and FFN revolution — until 2026, when DeepSeek-V4 (mHC) and Kimi K3 (Block AttnRes) both shipped replacements — and Qwen3.8-Next followed with a third, Gated Residual, whose ablations explain the other two. This page covers what's wrong with the uniform sum, the three fixes, and the one mathematical idea they share.

1. What the Uniform Sum Gets Wrong (and Right)

After \(L\) sublayers the stream is \(x_L = x_0 + \sum_{\ell<L} f_\ell\) — every layer reads the same undifferentiated sum and writes with the same fixed weight 1. Two structural costs:

  • Dilution. Layer 20's output reaches layer 80 as one summand among eighty. If it matters a lot to layer 80 specifically, tough — its relative weight is fixed at \(1/L\)-ish, and the only remedy is for other layers to waste capacity re-amplifying it.
  • No routing across depth. The stream is one lane: a layer cannot address "what layer 20 computed" separately from "what layer 60 computed." Features must coexist superposed in one vector.
  • And the thing it gets right, which any replacement must preserve: the identity path. Gradients reach layer 1 unattenuated, and signal magnitude is stable — adding \(L\) roughly independent contributions grows the norm only like \(\sqrt{L}\), which the norms absorb.

Why "just learn the mixing weights" fails at 93 layers. Relative signal magnitude vs. depth. An unconstrained learned mixing whose effective gain sits just 5% above 1 amplifies 90× by layer 93; 5% below, it kills the stream to 1% — and nothing in SGD pins the gain to exactly 1. The uniform sum (dashed) grows a benign \(\sqrt{L}\). Both 2026 redesigns buy their flexibility under a convex-combination constraint (violet): mixing weights that are non-negative and sum to 1 can re-route mass but never create it, so the magnitude stays bounded at any depth.

2. Hyper-Connections — Widen and Learn the Wiring

Hyper-connections (ByteDance, 2024) generalize the stream: instead of one lane, keep \(n\) parallel lanes (expansion rate \(n = 2\)–4), and let each layer apply learned maps for how it reads from the lanes, how it writes back, and how the lanes mix with each other across the layer boundary. The uniform sum is the special case (one lane, identity mixing, weight-1 write). The original paper showed consistent pretraining gains — and the obvious hazard: the lane-mixing matrix is applied at every layer, so any systematic deviation of its gain from 1 compounds geometrically (the amber/teal curves above). At 60–90 layers, that hazard is the whole story.

3. mHC — Constrain the Mixing to the Birkhoff Polytope

DeepSeek-V4's manifold-constrained hyper-connections keep the \(n = 4\) lanes and the learned mixing \(B_\ell\), but project \(B_\ell\) onto the set of doubly stochastic matrices (non-negative entries; every row and every column sums to 1) before use — via 20 iterations of Sinkhorn–Knopp, which alternately normalizes rows and columns and converges to the projection.

Why doubly stochastic ⇒ no compounding

A doubly stochastic \(B\) has \(\lVert B \rVert_1 = 1\) (columns sum to 1) and \(\lVert B \rVert_\infty = 1\) (rows sum to 1), so by interpolation \(\lVert B \rVert_2 \le \sqrt{\lVert B \rVert_1 \lVert B \rVert_\infty} = 1\): the mixing can never amplify. And by Birkhoff–von Neumann, every such \(B\) is a convex combination of permutation matrices — the learned mixing is exactly a "soft shuffling" of lane contents. Mass is re-routed between lanes, never created; a product of 93 such matrices is still non-expansive. The identity path survives inside the constraint set (the identity is itself doubly stochastic), so the highway property is preserved, not sacrificed.

Sigmoid gates control each sublayer's read/write against the lanes. The mHC parameters are few but sensitive — V4 trains them with AdamW while the rest of the model runs Muon.

4. Attention Residuals — Read the Depth Axis Selectively

Kimi K3 keeps a single stream but replaces the uniform read with attention over depth: each layer forms a learned pseudo-query and takes a softmax-weighted combination of the (RMSNorm'd) outputs of preceding computation blocks. Layer 80 that needs layer 20's features specifically can put weight ~1 on them — dilution is gone, input-dependently, per token.

Full per-layer AttnRes at \(L = 93\) would add an \(O(L \cdot d)\) read per token per layer; K3's Block AttnRes partitions the 93 layers into 8 blocks of 12 and attends over block outputs (9 sources with the embedding), reducing the overhead to \(O(N \cdot d)\), \(N = 8\). The same structure then gets reused for free at serving time: K3's EAGLE-3 speculative drafter consumes exactly these low/mid/high block features (K3 page, §6).

4.5 Gated Residual — Spend the Expressiveness on the Read

The third replacement is Qwen's, and it is the most useful to study because the report shows its work: Qwen3.8-Next's Gated Residual (GR) starts from the same widened stream as HC/mHC and then asks, operator by operator, which pieces of the machinery pay for themselves (Qwen Team, "On the Design of Qwen3.8-Next Architecture", Aug 2026, §2.2). The answer reorganizes §2–§4: almost all of the value is in the read, and the operator every earlier design spent its effort constraining — the branch mixing — can simply be deleted.

Step 0: width alone

Start with an AltUp-style static widening that fits a pre-norm network. The residual state before block \(\ell\) is \(n_r = 4\) branches \(R^{(\ell)} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_r \times d}\); each block holds \(n_r\) learnable scalars \(h\), reads a weighted sum, and writes its output to a single branch chosen round-robin by depth: \[ x^{(\ell)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n_r} h_i R^{(\ell)}_i, \qquad R^{(\ell+1)}_i = R^{(\ell)}_i + \mathbb{1}[\,i = \ell \bmod n_r\,]\; y^{(\ell)}. \] That is \(n_r\) parameters per block, no matrix multiply, and the only cost is the memory traffic of carrying four vectors instead of one. It still lowers the training loss of a 25B-A3B MoE on 400B tokens by roughly 0.01. Width alone is a substantial win; everything below is about how much read/write machinery is worth adding on top of it.

HC as three operators

Hyper-connections generalize both equations into three learnable operators — a read \(H_{\text{mix}}\) that forms the block input, a write \(H_{\text{combine}}\) that distributes the output over branches, and a mixing \(H_{\text{res}}\) that exchanges information between branches (\(A_m, B, A_r\) in the HC paper's notation): \[ x^{(\ell)} = H_{\text{mix}}^{\top} R^{(\ell)}, \qquad y^{(\ell)} = F^{(\ell)}\big(\mathrm{Norm}(x^{(\ell)})\big), \qquad R^{(\ell+1)} = H_{\text{res}}\, R^{(\ell)} + H_{\text{combine}}\, y^{(\ell)\top}, \] with \(H_{\text{mix}}, H_{\text{combine}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_r}\) and \(H_{\text{res}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n_r \times n_r}\). Each operator is a static term plus a data-dependent term predicted from a normalized view \(\bar R = \mathrm{norm}(R^{(\ell)})\) of the branches: \[ H_\star = H^{s}_\star + \lambda_\star \odot \phi\big(\bar R\, W_\star\big), \qquad \star \in \{\text{mix}, \text{combine}, \text{res}\}, \] where \(H^{s}_\star\) is learnable and static, \(W_\star\) projects the normalized residual, \(\phi\) is an activation and \(\lambda_\star\) a learnable scale. HC uses \(\phi = \tanh\) with \(\lambda_\star\) initialized to 0.01; mHC uses a sigmoid and additionally pins \(H_{\text{res}}\) to the doubly stochastic manifold of §3. Two limits anchor the family: static terms \(e_{\ell \bmod n_r}, 1, I\) with \(W_\star = 0\) start the widened network exactly at the pre-norm one, and \(\lambda_\star = 0\) throughout keeps every operator static — which is the AltUp variant above, at no extra compute.

Five ablation findings

Starting from the static operators, Qwen kept expressiveness only where it paid for itself. The endpoints of that progression (25B-A3B MoE, 560B tokens; \(n_r = 4\) for every widened variant; "static" is \(\lambda_\star = 0\), "dynamic" its data-dependent counterpart):

ResidualLossBenchmark avg.What changed
Pre-norm1.61750.91single stream
mHC (static)1.59652.494 branches, static operators
mHC (dynamic)1.59454.47+ data-dependent read and write
GR1.59054.66elementwise read, no \(H_{\text{res}}\)
  1. Bounded positive gates. A sigmoid beats \(\tanh\) in both loss and training stability — the same finding as mHC, and the same one the report makes for its GDN and attention gates.
  2. Data dependence is cheap in loss, expensive in benchmarks. Making the read and write data-dependent buys 0.002 of loss over static (against 0.021 for static over pre-norm), but the benchmark gain runs the other way: 1.98 points static → dynamic against 1.58 pre-norm → static. Loss alone would have understated the change.
  3. Read granularity matters; write granularity doesn't. Refining \(H_{\text{mix}}\) from one scalar per branch to one weight per branch and channel helps. The same refinement of \(H_{\text{combine}}\) gives almost nothing, so the write stays a per-branch scalar.
  4. Predict from all branches. Better than the last branch alone or a pooled view; normalizing each branch separately — a group RMSNorm over the widened stream — adds a further gain.
  5. \(H_{\text{res}}\) adds nothing. Once read and write are expressive enough, the \(n_r \times n_r\) mixing brings no significant improvement.

The operator

The read the ablation converges on — elementwise, data-dependent, sigmoid-gated — is the same thing as a stabilizer Qwen had found in separate work (Qiu et al. 2026): a lightweight elementwise self-gate after RMSNorm, GatedNorm, \(\mathrm{GatedNorm}(u) = \mathrm{RMSNorm}(u) \odot \sigma\big(W_2\, \mathrm{SiLU}(W_1\, \mathrm{RMSNorm}(u))\big)\), with \(W_1, W_2\) a low-rank bottleneck. GR is that gate applied to the widened stream, merged with the read into one operator.

Gated Residual — read and write

Normalize each branch independently with its own gain \(\gamma_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\), predict elementwise gates for every branch and channel from all branches through a bottleneck of rank \(r = d/8\), and average the gated branches into the block input: \[ \begin{aligned} \hat R_i &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}(R_i;\, \gamma_i), \qquad i = 1, \dots, n_r, \\ G &= \mathrm{unvec}\,\sigma\!\Big(W_u\, \mathrm{SiLU}\big(\tfrac{1}{n_r} W_d\, \mathrm{vec}(\hat R)\big)\Big) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_r \times d}, \\ x &= \frac{1}{n_r} \sum_{i=1}^{n_r} G_i \odot \hat R_i, \end{aligned} \] with \(W_d \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times n_r d}\) and \(W_u \in \mathbb{R}^{n_r d \times r}\). The block output \(y = F(x)\) is written to every branch through one data-dependent scalar per branch: \[ s = 2\,\sigma\!\big(\tfrac{1}{n_r} W_w\, \mathrm{vec}(\hat R)\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{n_r}, \qquad R'_i = R_i + s_i\, y, \] with \(W_w \in \mathbb{R}^{n_r \times n_r d}\).

Read against the HC template: \(\phi = \sigma\), an elementwise \(H_{\text{mix}}\), a per-branch scalar \(H_{\text{combine}}\), \(\bar R\) the group RMSNorm of all branches — and no \(H_{\text{res}}\). The static term \(H^s_\star\) brings no improvement for GR, standard random initialization suffices, and there is a separate GR module for the attention block and the MLP block of every layer. Two consequences fall out. Because the read already normalizes and gates, GR replaces the block's pre-normalization rather than sitting in front of it — \(\mathrm{Norm}\) drops out of \(y = F(\mathrm{Norm}(x))\), and widening adds no normalization layer. And with no mixing, a branch is only ever written by blocks and read through the gate, so the branches stay separate — which is what makes the path analysis below exact.

Why dropping \(H_{\text{res}}\) is the inference story

A widened stream costs no FLOPs to speak of; it costs bytes. The inference cost of GR is dominated by the memory traffic of the residual state, and \(H_{\text{res}}\) is a full read of that state per block — the dominant inference cost of a widened stream. HC and mHC keep the read and write as per-branch scalars and spend their capacity on the mixing, which mHC then has to constrain; GR spends the capacity on the read, and the ablation says the mixing costs nothing to drop. Dropping it also removes the one operator that needed separate constraints to stay stable.

Two further cuts on the same bill. The gates in GR, gated attention and GDN all bound the magnitude of what is written into the stream, so residual values stay in a narrow range; Qwen stores the branches in FP8, halving the bytes moved relative to BF16 with almost no loss in quality. And the read (norm, gate, average) and the write are each fused into a single kernel, with the group RMSNorm folded into the read, so the widened stream is traversed once per block in each direction.

The rejected cut: sparse reads. In trained models the write at each GR layer is typically dominated by two branches, so Qwen tried letting each block read only the two branches with the highest gate values instead of all four — from scratch and mid-training. Pre-training loss and benchmarks were almost unaffected; quality degraded clearly after post-training, and varying the sparsity level across layers did not fix it. The report flags this as a case where pre-training metrics alone would have led to the wrong decision.

Qwen also ran K3's design in its own setting: a 28-layer model (\(L = 56\) sublayers), each variant with and without GatedNorm (GN). Full AttnRes attends over every preceding sublayer's output; Block AttnRes sums \(S\) sublayers into one representation and attends over those.

So the block summarization that buys K3 its \(O(Nd)\) memory (§4) is not free — and K3's blocks are 12 layers, far coarser than Qwen's \(S = 4\) sublayers. Note the GN column too: the gate lowers the loss at every setting, by 0.004–0.005 on AttnRes and only 0.002 on plain pre-norm. It helps more when the input a sublayer reads is more complex — and it is the same gate GR carries inside its read.

What the branches are actually used for

Because there is no mixing, branch \(c\) before block \(v\) is a plain accumulator, \(R^{(v)}_c = R^{(0)}_c + \sum_{u < v} s^{(u)}_c\, y^{(u)}\), and what block \(v\) reads decomposes exactly into contributions from every earlier block: \[ a_{u \to v} = \frac{1}{n_r} \sum_{c=1}^{n_r} G^{(v)}_c \odot \gamma_c \odot \frac{s^{(u)}_c\, y^{(u)}}{\mathrm{rms}\big(R^{(v)}_c\big)}, \qquad \pi_{uv} = \frac{\lVert a_{u \to v} \rVert}{\sum_{u' < v} \lVert a_{u' \to v} \rVert}, \] evaluated at the gate values the forward pass actually took; every reader's shares sum to one to within \(3 \times 10^{-8}\). Qwen compares a 20-layer hybrid MoE (softmax attention every fourth layer, GDN elsewhere) trained with GR against an otherwise identical reference without it, and reports \(\Delta_{uv} = \pi^{\text{GR}}_{uv} - \pi^{\text{ref}}_{uv}\), which subtracts out the pattern common to every residual network — nearby writers dominate. Fig. 7 of the report draws the 21 of 780 ordered pairs with \(\Delta_{uv} \ge 0.05\) over a skip of at least one layer.

  • One branch goes long, three stay local. Across five GR checkpoints, each has exactly one long-range branch, with a typical skip of 10.9 layers against 3.4–3.9 for the rest. Every connection on it originates at layer 0 and lands past layer 10: layer-0 GDN → layer-15 attention rises from a 0.020 share in the reference to 0.138 under GR, and holds at 0.072–0.138 for every reader from layer 10 to 19 with no downward trend. The branch is written most heavily at layer 0 and barely updated afterwards, so layer-0 information stays addressable at every depth.
  • The heavy readers are the softmax-attention layers. That is where the long-range paths land — global attention acts as the hub integrating the long-range context the GDN layers compress away. Short paths gain too (layer-10 GDN → layer-11 attention, \(\Delta_{uv} = 0.117\)), and the same layer-0 MLP output reaches layer 2 on a local branch (0.139 → 0.192) and layer 15 on the long-range one (0.008 → 0.058) — two decay rates for one writer, which a single stream with one decay rate per writer cannot express.
  • The net effect is redistribution. Grouping all 780 paths by skip and summing \(\Delta_{uv}\): adjacent paths (skip 1) gain 0.96 in share, long-range paths (skip > 12) gain 0.91, and mid-range paths (skip 2–12) lose 3.21. The weighted-average skip barely moves (3.97 vs 3.91), so the total cross-layer information is similar; GR selects a few paths and amplifies them at the expense of the middle.

5. One Idea, Three Costumes

The same constraint, spotted twice — then deleted
mHC (DeepSeek-V4)

Mixing weights constrained doubly stochastic via Sinkhorn projection. Static per layer (learned parameters), input-independent. Flexibility axis: width — 4 lanes.

Block AttnRes (Kimi K3)

Read weights constrained non-negative-sum-to-1 via softmax. Dynamic — input-dependent per token. Flexibility axis: depth — 8 block sources.

Gated Residual (Qwen3.8-Next)

Read weights bounded in \((0,1)\) via an elementwise sigmoid gate on RMS-normalized branches. Dynamic — per token, per channel. Flexibility axis: width — 4 branches, and no mixing operator at all.

Strip the branding and the first two designs make the identical move: replace uniform accumulation with a learned convex combination. Softmax outputs and doubly stochastic rows are both points in a probability simplex — non-negative weights summing to one — and that is precisely the constraint class that re-routes signal without amplifying it (§1's plot). Where they differ is which axis gets the flexibility (lanes vs. depth) and whether the weights see the data (static vs. per-token).

The third design takes the other exit. §1's compounding hazard attaches to one specific thing: an operator that multiplies the state at every layer. mHC keeps that operator and constrains it to be non-expansive; AttnRes never has one (single stream, softmax read); GR keeps the width but drops the operator entirely. Its branches are additive accumulators exactly like the 2017 stream — there are just four of them, and the block output enters each through a bounded scalar — so there is nothing to compound and nothing to project. The flexibility moves to the read, where it is bounded by construction: a per-channel gate in \((0,1)\) on RMS-normalized branches, averaged. Qwen's ablation is the empirical half of the argument — once the read is expressive, the mixing "brings no significant improvement" — and the stress tests in §6 make GR the most stable of the three under load. One detail carries over unchanged: like V4's mHC parameters, GR's two low-rank projections train on AdamW while the backbone runs Muon — the report attributes it to their very elongated shape.

It is the residual-stream version of a pattern this series keeps meeting: when you make a structural component learnable, you must simultaneously constrain it to conserve — or remove it — compare routing bias updates that preserve top-\(k\) counts, or the gates in Gated DeltaNet bounded in \((0,1)\).

6. Why This Happened Now

  • Depth got extreme. At 93 layers, \(1/L\) dilution and single-lane superposition are far more binding than at GPT-2's 12. The deeper the stack, the more an addressable depth axis is worth.
  • The rest of the block ran dry. Attention and FFN have absorbed a decade of optimization; the residual stream was the last untouched component with structural slack.
  • Both labs bundle it with efficiency claims. Neither ships it as decoration: K3 counts AttnRes among the sources of its ≈2.5× scaling-efficiency gain over K2; V4 lists mHC among its core architectural bets. Independent adoption in the same generation is the strongest signal in architecture research — the same signal that blessed the 3:1 hybrid ratio.
  • The residual became a stability lever. Qwen's stress test holds the learning rate constant at a multiple of its optimum on a 28-layer 25B-A3B MoE. At 4×, the Qwen3.5 structure under AdamW spikes 183 times per 10k steps and crosses the clipping threshold on 213 of 19,932 steps; Muon + GR never touches the clipper and records zero loss spikes. A single-variable pair isolates the gate: at 3×, with AdamW and the structure fixed, toggling GatedNorm on cuts the spike rate from 32.0 to 3.2 per 10k steps and threshold crossings from 256 to 20. The report's reading (Qiu et al. 2026): a network trained at a high learning rate needs a rescaling mechanism, and without an explicit gate it grows activation outliers to supply one, leaving it fragile; the multiplicative gate provides the rescaling directly. That is why the gated read stands in for explicit activation control — Qwen3.8-Next trains without qk-clip or SwiGLU-clip (report, §3.3).

7. Summary

The defect

Uniform accumulation: fixed weight-1 writes, undifferentiated reads, \(1/L\) dilution, one lane.

The hazard

Naively learned mixing compounds: gain \(1 \pm \epsilon\) per layer → \(e^{\pm \epsilon L}\) at depth \(L\).

mHC

4 lanes; mixing Sinkhorn-projected onto doubly stochastic matrices — soft permutations, \(\lVert B \rVert_2 \le 1\).

Block AttnRes

Softmax pseudo-query reads over 8 depth-blocks — input-dependent, \(O(Nd)\), reused by the EAGLE drafter.

Gated Residual

4 branches, no mixing; elementwise sigmoid read gate (rank \(d/8\)) on per-branch RMSNorm, scalar writes. Replaces pre-norm; FP8 state; zero spikes at 4× LR.

The shared idea

Learned convex combinations or bounded gates replace uniform sums: re-route mass, never create it — and if an operator can amplify, constrain it or drop it.

Status

Three independent frontier deployments in one generation — the block's next contested component.

Multi-head Latent Attention

Every other way of shrinking the KV cache removes heads and pays for it in quality. MLA instead caches one low-rank latent per token and folds the reconstruction into the weights it was already going to multiply — so the cache lands near the MQA floor while the model stays at full head count.

MLA was introduced in DeepSeek-V2 (2024) and carried forward unchanged in structure through V3 and R1. It answers the question the KV cache page ends on: the cache is what caps concurrency, GQA and MQA shrink it by deleting KV heads, and deleting KV heads costs accuracy. MLA gets the memory win without the deletion.

1 · The ladder that MLA steps off

MHA caches \(2 n_h d_h\) elements per layer per token. MQA drops that to \(2 d_h\) by sharing one KV head across all queries; GQA interpolates with \(g\) groups. Both work by making query heads share a key/value subspace — the expressivity you lose is exactly the per-head freedom you gave up.

How much does that cost? DeepSeek-V2's Appendix D.1 is the cleanest controlled answer published: three 7B dense models, identical except for the attention mechanism, parameter-matched to ~7B by adjusting layer count, all trained on 1.33T tokens.

The ordering is monotone and the gaps are large — 7.3 MMLU points between MQA and MHA. Cache reduction on this ladder is not free, it is bought. MLA's claim is that you can leave the ladder entirely: keep all \(n_h\) heads distinct, and compress along a different axis.

2 · Low-rank joint compression

The observation is that \(k_t\) and \(v_t\) are both linear functions of the same hidden state \(h_t\), and together they span far less than the \(2 n_h d_h\) dimensions they occupy. So project \(h_t\) once into a shared latent and reconstruct both from it:

KV joint compression
\[ c^{KV}_t = W^{DKV} h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}, \qquad k^C_t = W^{UK} c^{KV}_t, \qquad v^C_t = W^{UV} c^{KV}_t \]
\(W^{DKV} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c \times d}\) is the down-projection; \(W^{UK}, W^{UV} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_h n_h \times d_c}\) the up-projections. Only \(c^{KV}_t\) is cached.

Note what is not shared: after up-projection, \(k^C_t\) is sliced into \(n_h\) genuinely distinct head vectors, as in MHA. The heads are not tied — the rank is constrained. That is the difference between MLA and the GQA/MQA family in one sentence.

Queries get the same treatment, \(c^Q_t = W^{DQ}h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d'_c}\) with \(q^C_t = W^{UQ}c^Q_t\), but for an unrelated reason: queries are never cached, so this buys nothing at inference. It cuts activation memory during training, where every position's queries are live at once.

3 · Absorption — the up-projection never runs

Compression alone would be a poor trade: you would save memory and then pay to reconstruct \(2 n_h d_h\) numbers per cached token on every decode step. It is free instead, because the up-projections are constant matrices sitting next to other constant matrices.

Take the pre-softmax score between query \(t\) and cached position \(j\), for head \(i\):

Absorbing \(W^{UK}\) into \(W^{UQ}\)
\[ (q^C_{t,i})^{\!\top} k^C_{j,i} = \left(W^{UQ}_i c^Q_t\right)^{\!\top} W^{UK}_i c^{KV}_j = (c^Q_t)^{\!\top} \underbrace{\left(W^{UQ\top}_i W^{UK}_i\right)}_{\text{precompute once}} c^{KV}_j \]

The bracketed product is fixed after training. Fold it in, and the query side reads the cache directly in latent space — \(k^C_j\) is never formed. The same argument runs on the output side: the attention output is a convex combination of \(v^C_j = W^{UV}c^{KV}_j\), and linearity lets the combination happen first, so \(W^O W^{UV}\) collapses into a single output matrix and \(v^C_j\) is never formed either.

What a decode step actually touches

Read \(c^{KV}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\) for each cached position. Score against the absorbed query. Weight and sum the same vectors. Apply one output matrix. K and V are never materialized at any point.

In the paper's words: "since \(W^{UK}\) can be absorbed into \(W^Q\), and \(W^{UV}\) can be absorbed into \(W^O\), we even do not need to compute keys and values out for attention."

Absorption is a decode-time rewrite. During prefill and training you have many positions in flight at once, so it is cheaper to materialize \(K\) and \(V\) once per chunk and run an ordinary \(d_h = 128\) FlashAttention kernel over them. Real implementations carry both paths and switch on phase — DeepSeek-V2 notes MLA is "optimized based on an improved version of FlashAttention-2." See Prefill & Decode for why the two phases want opposite things.

4 · Why there is an extra 64-dim key — the RoPE obstruction

Everything above breaks the moment you add RoPE. RoPE multiplies queries and keys by a position-dependent rotation \(\mathcal{R}_m\). Apply it to \(k^C_j\) and the score becomes

The obstruction
\[ \left(\mathcal{R}_t W^{UQ}_i c^Q_t\right)^{\!\top}\!\left(\mathcal{R}_j W^{UK}_i c^{KV}_j\right) = (c^Q_t)^{\!\top}\, W^{UQ\top}_i \underbrace{\mathcal{R}_{j-t}}_{\text{depends on } j,\,t} W^{UK}_i\, c^{KV}_j \]

A position-dependent matrix now sits between \(W^{UQ}\) and \(W^{UK}\), and matrix multiplication does not commute — so there is no fixed product to precompute. The absorption dies, and with it the whole scheme: you would have to re-expand and re-rotate keys for every prefix token at every decode step, which is worse than not compressing at all.

DeepSeek's fix is to stop trying to make one channel do both jobs. Decoupled RoPE splits position onto a separate, small channel that carries no compressed content:

Decoupled RoPE
\[ q^R_t = \mathrm{RoPE}\!\left(W^{QR} c^Q_t\right), \qquad k^R_t = \mathrm{RoPE}\!\left(W^{KR} h_t\right) \] \[ q_{t,i} = [\,q^C_{t,i};\, q^R_{t,i}\,], \qquad k_{t,i} = [\,k^C_{t,i};\, k^R_t\,], \qquad o_{t,i} = \sum_{j \le t} \mathrm{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{q_{t,i}^{\!\top} k_{j,i}}{\sqrt{d_h + d^R_h}}\right) v^C_{j,i} \]

Three things to notice. The rotary queries are per head (\(q^R_{t,i} \in \mathbb{R}^{d^R_h}\)) but the rotary key \(k^R_t\) is shared across all heads — MQA-style, because it carries only position, and position is the same for every head. The compressed half stays absorbable because no rotation touches it. And the softmax scale becomes \(\sqrt{d_h + d^R_h}\), since each head's effective query is now the concatenation.

So the cache holds two things per token per layer: the content latent \(c^{KV}_t\) and the one shared rotary key \(k^R_t\) — \((d_c + d^R_h)\,l\) elements in total. That extra 64-wide strip is not an implementation detail; it is the price of keeping RoPE and absorption in the same model.

The general shape of the argument

A memory optimization was blocked by a positional encoding, and the fix was to give position its own narrow channel rather than to weaken either mechanism. The same pattern recurs: hybrid linear-attention models (see Gated DeltaNet) go further and drop positional encoding from their global layers entirely, letting the recurrent layers' decay carry position instead.

MLA wins 7 of 8 comparisons — the exception is C-Eval at the small scale (51.6 → 50.9) — on 14% of the cache at 16B and 4% at 250B. And the margin grows with scale, which is the opposite of what a lossy compression should do.

Why would constraining rank help? The honest answer is that the paper reports the result without a mechanism. Two plausible readings: the low-rank bottleneck is a regularizer on an attention layer that has more capacity than it uses, and the query-side compression changes what the optimizer sees. Either way, the practical claim is the weaker and safer one — the compression is not paid for in accuracy.

7 · The real trade-off

It is tempting to say MLA's cost is "the up-projection at attention time." §3 shows it isn't — absorbed, there is no up-projection. The cost shows up somewhere less obvious.

In the absorbed form, every head scores against the same cached vector of width \(d_c + d^R_h = 576\). Structurally that is MQA with one very wide KV head. So per cached token, per head, the dot products run over 576 dimensions instead of \(d_h = 128\):

Absorbed MLA vs MHA at decode, per layer per cached token
  • HBM traffic: 576 elements read, versus 32,768 — a 56.9× reduction.
  • Attention FLOPs: \(n_h \times 576\) MACs for scores instead of \(n_h \times 128\) — roughly 4.5× more.

That is not a bad deal; it is an excellent one, for a specific reason. Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound — the GPU is starved for bytes, not for math (see Prefill & Decode). Trading a 4.5× rise in FLOPs for a 56.9× fall in bytes moves attention decisively up the roofline: arithmetic intensity rises by more than an order of magnitude, into the regime where the hardware is actually being used.

This is why MLA's payoff is reported as throughput, not latency. Smaller cache means more concurrent sequences per GPU; higher arithmetic intensity means those sequences batch efficiently. DeepSeek-V2 reports >50K tokens/s generation on a single 8×H800 node — 5.76× the previous dense model — with FP8 weights and the latent itself quantized to ~6 bits on average, compression stacked on compression.

The one genuine cost is implementation complexity. A correct MLA stack needs the absorbed decode path, the unabsorbed prefill path, weight-absorption at load time, and a kernel that handles a 576-wide effective head with a 64-wide rotary tail. This is why MLA stayed a DeepSeek-family architecture for a long time while GQA — four lines of change — went everywhere.

V2-Lite is the one to implement against: same mechanism, no query compression, and small enough to train. Two stability details that are easy to miss and hard to debug — DeepSeek adds RMSNorm after the compressed latents, and extra scaling factors at the width bottlenecks, because low-rank projections distort a layer's output scale.

One more consequence of the decoupled design: when extending context 4K → 128K, YaRN is applied only to \(k^R_t\). It is the sole carrier of position, so it is the only thing that needs rescaling — the content latent is untouched. 1000 steps at 32K sequence length was enough to pass needle-in-a-haystack at 128K.

Where MLA stands in 2026: the mechanism outlived its inventor's flagship. DeepSeek itself retired MLA in V4 (sequence compression replaced channel compression), but Kimi K3 runs a gated, NoPE variant as its global attention — NoPE dissolving the decoupled-RoPE workaround of §4 entirely — GLM-5 reshaped it into "MLA-256" (head dim 192 → 256, a third fewer heads), and Mistral Small 4 adopted it outright (kv_lora_rank 256), Mistral's first MLA model.

9 · Summary

The move

Cache one shared low-rank latent \(c^{KV}_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_c}\) instead of K and V. Heads stay distinct; the rank is constrained, not the head count.

Why it's free

\(W^{UK}\) absorbs into the query projection, \(W^{UV}\) into \(W^O\). At decode, K and V are never materialized.

The RoPE obstruction

A rotation between \(W^{UQ}\) and \(W^{UK}\) kills absorption. Fix: carry position on a separate per-head query and one shared key.

Footprint

\((d_c + d^R_h)l\) = 576/layer/token — GQA with 2.25 groups, 56.9× under same-shape MHA.

Quality

Beats MHA on 7 of 8 matched comparisons at 4–14% of the cache, with the margin growing at scale.

The actual cost

~4.5× attention FLOPs for 56.9× less HBM traffic — a good trade precisely because decode is bandwidth-bound. Plus real kernel complexity.

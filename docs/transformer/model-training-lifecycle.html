Model Training, End to End

Follow the checkpoints, datasets, and decisions that turn raw material and a compute budget into a usable language model. Techniques make sense once you know where they fit.

Read the process here; follow one real model next.

This is a conceptual map for autoregressive language-model development, not one universal recipe or an executable launcher. The companion MAI-Thinking-1 walkthrough follows a reported recipe from base-model development through specialist training and final evaluation. The existing component pages remain the detailed references.

1. Follow the artifacts, not just the techniques

  1. 1. Design and dataOutput: a tested architecture, tokenizer, corpus plan, and compute budget.
  2. 2. Pre-trainingOutput: a base checkpoint that predicts language and code.
  3. 3. Mid-training, if neededOutput: a base adapted to a domain, data mix, or context length.
  4. 4. Post-trainingOutput: instruction-following, reasoning, or specialist policies.
  5. 5. Consolidation, if neededOutput: one candidate combining the capabilities you want to ship.
  6. 6. Evaluate and releaseOutput: a versioned model plus its inference contract and evidence.

The numbers are a reading order, not a mandatory six-job pipeline. You can start from an existing base, omit a distinct mid-training phase, branch into specialists, or iterate SFT and RL. Evaluation accompanies every stage; bad evidence sends you back to the data, objective, or implementation rather than automatically forward.

Three different axes that a topic index can accidentally flatten.
AxisExampleQuestion it answers
StagePre-training, mid-training, post-trainingWhat capability are we developing now, from which checkpoint?
Learning techniqueSFT, distillation, GRPO, DPOWhich data and objective change the weights?
Execution strategyFSDP, expert parallelism, asynchronous rolloutsHow do we execute that learning process efficiently and correctly?

Distillation is not necessarily the stage after RL. It can bootstrap a student, consolidate specialists, or carry progress to a new base before another RL run. Async RL is not another capability stage: it changes how rollout collection and parameter updates overlap, introducing freshness and probability-accounting obligations.

2. Design a model you can train and serve

Input
Target tasks, permitted data sources, training budget, hardware, context requirements, and serving cost/latency constraints.
Work and objective
Build the corpus and tokenizer; run smaller architecture/data ablations. Compare held-out quality at matched cost and test whether gains persist with scale. Choose an operating point, not just the lowest small-model loss.
Why it exists
The largest run is too expensive to use as the first experiment. Choices such as sparsity, tokenizer, context length, and data repetition affect both learning and the later inference workload.
Output
Versioned model configuration and tokenizer, source-mixture and filtering rules, split/decontamination policy, and a feasible training plan.
Evidence to advance
Representative scaling experiments, data-quality checks, and measured memory/throughput on the intended stack. A FLOPs advantage alone does not establish a wall-clock advantage.

Mechanics: scaling laws, MoE, MFU and utilization. Worked decision: MAI's architecture and scaling ladder.

3. Pre-training: build a broadly capable base

Input
For a from-scratch run: initialized weights, the chosen tokenizer, and the large curated corpus. Continuing an existing base is a different starting point and should be labeled as such.
Weight update
Usually next-token cross-entropy on packed token sequences: forward pass, token loss, backward pass, optimizer update. Data order, loss masks, learning-rate schedule, and precision are part of the recipe.
Why it exists
Learn representations and predictive competence across language, knowledge, and code. That does not by itself specify a reliable assistant interface or task-solving policy.
Output
A base-model checkpoint and resumable training state, tied to the tokenizer/configuration and data history.
Evidence to advance
Held-out loss and capability trends, broad-domain regressions, stable optimization, and data-exposure accounting. A lower average loss can conceal degraded coverage or excessive repetition.

The systems problem is executing a large forward/backward workload: model and optimizer sharding, communication overlap, memory, and recovery. Those tools support the stage; they do not define its learning objective.

Mechanics: the next-token training loop and training frameworks. Worked run: MAI's 30T-token base training.

4. Mid-training: prepare the base for its next job

Input
A pretrained base plus deliberately selected domain, quality, or long-document data.
Weight update
Often continued next-token training with a changed mixture, schedule, or context length. Context extension may also require positional/configuration changes, depending on the architecture.
Why it exists
Improve the starting point for downstream specialization without spending the entire pre-training run at the most expensive context length or on the final specialized mixture.
Output
A new base checkpoint with documented domain/context behavior. It is not automatically an instruction-tuned or reasoning-specialist model.
Evidence to advance
Improvement on target domains and long-context tasks without unacceptable short-context or general-quality regressions. Validate downstream post-training where feasible; NLL alone is not the final product objective.

“Mid-training” is a recipe label, not a unique optimizer or universally separate phase. Its boundary with continued pre-training varies by report. Long training contexts also do not prescribe the same rollout-length or serving limits in later stages.

Worked transition: MAI's 16K → 64K → 256K training contexts.

5. Post-training: turn competence into behavior

Start from the base that passed the previous checks. Now the data contract changes: demonstrations specify behavior to imitate; preference pairs specify relative choices; RL tasks provide feedback on actions the policy actually samples.

Input
Base or mid-trained weights, an exact chat/tool format, and demonstrations, preference pairs, or executable tasks with graders.
Weight update
SFT trains on selected target tokens. DPO-style methods use preference pairs. RL collects responses or tool trajectories, scores them, constructs advantages, and updates the policy. These are alternatives or ingredients, not mandatory consecutive steps.
Why it exists
Develop instruction following, useful reasoning, tool use, safety, and other deployment behaviors that the base objective did not explicitly require.
Output
One broadly tuned policy or several domain specialists, plus their trace datasets, reward/evaluation definitions, and run lineage.
Evidence to advance
Held-out task quality and regression checks, grader validity, behavior beyond reward score, and stable updates. For agents, inspect full trajectories and environment state, not just final text.
Two source-backed paths, not a universal ordering.

DeepSeek-R1 combines cold-start SFT, reasoning RL, rejection-sampling SFT, and final RL. In contrast, MAI starts its reasoning climb without prior reasoning traces and develops three specialists independently before merging them.

RL needs both a learner and an inference/environment workload. Async execution can keep those resources busier, but the trainer must know which policy produced each action and whether the sampled tokens, contexts, and sampling support were preserved.

Mechanics: RLHF, DPO, GRPO, reward design, async RL. Execution recipe: a bounded agentic-RL experiment.

6. Consolidation: decide what one model must retain

Input
Specialist teachers and their data, or one strong policy whose capabilities should transfer to another checkpoint or smaller model.
Weight update
For offline trace distillation, SFT a chosen student on teacher-generated examples. Other recipes query teachers on student-generated states. A later RL stage can refine the combined policy.
Why it exists
Independent specialists are not yet a single deployable generalist. Transfer can also recover progress after instability or make serving cheaper; reducing model size is only one use case.
Output
A candidate model with an explicit teacher/data lineage. Pooling traces is not averaging specialist weights or copying their optimizer states.
Evidence to advance
Compare the candidate with each relevant specialist and the earlier generalist at matched evaluation budgets. Check long-context behavior, tool formats, safety, and regressions hidden by aggregate scores.

Mixture weights need a denominator: a domain can be a modest share of examples but dominate tokens because its traces are long. Data sampling and loss normalization jointly determine training influence. Successful transfer must be measured, not inferred from a balanced-looking pie chart.

Mechanics: distillation and specialist/consolidation patterns. Worked transitions: MAI's self-distillation restarts and its final merge.

7. Evaluation and release: the model is more than its weights

Input
The candidate weights, tokenizer, rendering and tool protocol, plus a proposed inference configuration.
Work and objective
Measure capability, safety, robustness, latency, memory, and cost. Evaluation normally does not update weights; failures trigger an explicit new training or systems iteration.
Why it exists
A benchmark score under one harness and token budget is not a guarantee of application quality, safety, or serving efficiency.
Output
A versioned release package: model/config/tokenizer, supported interface and limits, evaluation settings/results, and an operational rollback/monitoring plan.
Evidence to advance
Task-specific acceptance criteria and independent held-out evidence under the intended inference stack. Re-evaluate after quantization, template changes, or harness changes; those can alter behavior without changing the original training run.
Evaluation is a continuous control loop. Keep development evaluations separate from final reporting. If a final test informs further tuning, it has become development feedback; preserve an untouched test or disclose that reuse. No universal benchmark threshold certifies that a model is ready to ship.

Worked evidence and its limits: MAI's final evaluations. Serving mechanics: inference references.

8. The handoff that a list of stages leaves out

At every arrow, distinguish continuing a run, starting a new stage from weights, and transferring behavior through data. They preserve different things.

A recommended handoff record, not a claim that every report discloses all fields.
Carry or explicitly replaceWhy it matters
Weights, model config, tokenizer, chat/tool formatWeights alone do not identify the inputs and outputs the next stage will see.
Optimizer, schedule, RNG and data cursorFaithful continuation needs training state. A deliberate stage reset may initialize new state; record that decision rather than call it an exact resume.
Data lineage, mixtures and masksSeparate original corpus, demonstrations, teacher traces, and sampled RL trajectories. Record what tokens receive loss and how examples are weighted.
RL behavior and environment versionsA rollout's policy, sampling support, tool context, and grader determine whether it is valid for an update. Old traces reused for SFT are a new supervised dataset, not automatically valid RL samples.
Evaluation contract and baselinePreserve prompts/splits, budgets, harness, scorer versions, and reference checkpoints so a stage's gain or regression is interpretable.
Why a restart can be part of the recipe, not just failure recovery

An ordinary checkpoint restore resumes weights and training state from an earlier time. MAI instead sometimes turns strong RL rollouts into supervised data and trains a mid-trained checkpoint on them before restarting RL. The discovered behavior survives through traces; the unstable RL checkpoint need not be the student's initialization. That distinction connects imitation, optimization stability, and the model's development history.

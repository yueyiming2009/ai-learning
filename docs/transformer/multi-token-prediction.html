Transformer & Pretraining

Multi-Token Prediction

Train each position to predict the next \(D\) tokens, not just the next one. The extra heads densify the training signal and force the hidden state to plan ahead — and at serving time they become a draft head that was trained as part of the model rather than distilled from it afterward.

Pretraining gives every position exactly one label: the token that actually came next. That is a remarkably productive signal — everything a base model knows comes out of it — but it is also a thin one, and it is thin in a specific way worth naming. Multi-token prediction (MTP) is the family of objectives that widens it, and it has an unusual property for a training-time change: the machinery you add for the objective is also, at inference time, exactly the machinery speculative decoding wants.

1 · Why one token per position is a thin signal

Under teacher forcing, position \(t\) receives gradient from one term: \(-\log p_\theta(x_{t+1}\mid x_{\le t})\). Nothing in the loss asks the hidden state \(h_t\) to encode anything beyond what is needed to name the immediate successor. Three consequences follow.

The three complaints against pure next-token prediction
  1. Local shortcuts dominate. A large fraction of next-token decisions are all but determined by the last few tokens — the completion of a word after its first subword, the closing bracket, the boilerplate continuation. Gradient mass concentrates on the easy majority; the model can score well while learning mostly local statistics.
  2. The representation is permitted to be myopic. \(h_t\) is only ever graded on \(x_{t+1}\). Any longer-range structure it carries is incidental — useful for later positions, but never directly supervised at \(t\).
  3. Consequential tokens are underweighted. Some positions are choice points — the token that picks which proof strategy, which algorithm, which branch of an argument. They constrain a long span downstream, but in the loss they count exactly as much as the next piece of a word already half-spelled.

Complaint 3 is the sharp one, and it is the one MTP most directly attacks. If the loss at \(t\) also has to predict \(x_{t+2},\dots,x_{t+D}\), then a token whose identity determines the next several tokens gets scored \(D\) times — once by each head that has to live with the choice. A token that merely finishes a word is easy for every head and contributes little extra gradient either way. The objective effectively reweights the loss toward positions that matter, without anyone having to label which ones those are.

2 · The objective

Fix a prediction depth \(D\). Instead of one head, the model carries \(D\) output heads; head \(k\) at position \(t\) predicts the token \(k\) steps ahead. The training loss sums over both positions and depths:

Multi-token prediction loss
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}}(\theta) \;=\; -\sum_{t} \sum_{k=1}^{D} \log p_\theta^{(k)}\!\left(x_{t+k} \,\middle|\, x_{\le t}\right) \]
\(D=1\) recovers ordinary next-token prediction exactly. Every extra depth is free supervision — the labels are already in the corpus, just shifted further.

The label construction is the same one-position shift as teacher forcing, applied \(D\) times. Depth \(k\)'s target row is the input shifted left by \(k\), and its last \(k\) positions have no target and are masked out:

input Thecatsatonthemat
target k=1 catsatonthemat—
target k=2 satonthemat——
target k=3 onthemat———

Position 1 must now name cat, sat, and on from the single word The — hopeless in the absolute sense, but that is fine. The heads are not expected to be accurate at depth; they are expected to be calibrated, and the gradient they send back into the trunk is the point.

This is a product of marginals, not a joint. \(\prod_k p^{(k)}_\theta(x_{t+k}\mid x_{\le t})\) is not \(p_\theta(x_{t+1},\dots,x_{t+D}\mid x_{\le t})\) — the true joint does not factor that way, because \(x_{t+2}\) depends on which \(x_{t+1}\) was actually drawn. Each head is a proper scoring rule for its own marginal, and that is all. It is exactly why you cannot simply sample \(D\) tokens from the \(D\) heads and call it generation: independently sampled marginals produce incoherent combinations. Any use of the extra heads at inference must either verify them (§7) or restore the conditioning (§4).

3 · Design one — parallel heads on a shared trunk

The direct implementation, and the one in Gloeckle et al. (2024): keep the transformer trunk exactly as it is, and attach \(D\) independent output heads to its final hidden state. Each head is typically one transformer layer followed by the (shared) unembedding matrix.

\[ h_t = \text{Trunk}(x_{\le t}), \qquad p^{(k)}_\theta(\cdot \mid x_{\le t}) = \text{softmax}\big(W_u \, f_k(h_t)\big),\quad k = 1,\dots,D \]

All \(D\) heads read the same \(h_t\) and run in parallel — no head waits on another, so the extra wall-clock cost is one head-layer's worth of compute per depth, on top of an unchanged trunk. This is the same structure as Medusa, which arrived at it from the inference side: Medusa's "draft heads" and MTP's "extra prediction heads" are the same object, trained for different stated reasons.

The cost of the design is precisely the independence noted above: \(f_2(h_t)\) never sees which token head 1 chose, so it must marginalize over all of them. Head accuracy therefore falls off steeply with \(k\), and the fall-off is structural rather than a training failure.

4 · Design two — sequential MTP modules (DeepSeek-V3)

DeepSeek-V3 keeps the depth-\(D\) objective but refuses the independence. Its \(D\) MTP modules form a chain: module \(k\) receives module \(k-1\)'s hidden state and the embedding of the token at \(t+k\), so the complete causal chain is preserved at every prediction depth. Concretely, for module \(k\) at position \(i\):

One MTP module
\[ h'^{k}_{i} = M_k \left[ \operatorname{RMSNorm}\!\big(h^{k-1}_{i}\big) \,;\, \operatorname{RMSNorm}\!\big(\text{Emb}(x_{i+k})\big) \right] \] \[ h^{k}_{1:T-k} = \text{TRM}_k\!\big(h'^{k}_{1:T-k}\big), \qquad P^{k}_{i+k+1} = \text{OutHead}\!\big(h^{k}_{i}\big) \]

\(M_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times 2d}\) is a projection that fuses the two \(d\)-dimensional inputs back to width \(d\); \(\text{TRM}_k\) is a single transformer block; \(h^{0}\) is the main model's output hidden state. The embedding layer and the output head are shared with the main model — only \(M_k\) and \(\text{TRM}_k\) are new parameters per depth.

Main model
h⁰ᵢ = Trunk(x≤ᵢ)
OutHead → x̂ᵢ₊₁
Full depth. This is the model you keep.
MTP module 1
in: h⁰ᵢ , Emb(xi+1)
1 block → h¹ᵢ
OutHead → x̂ᵢ₊₂
Conditions on the true xi+1 during training.
MTP module 2
in: h¹ᵢ , Emb(xi+2)
1 block → h²ᵢ
OutHead → x̂ᵢ₊₃
Sequential: waits on module 1.

The per-depth losses are cross-entropies over the valid range, averaged and scaled by a weight \(\lambda\):

\[ \mathcal{L}^{k}_{\text{MTP}} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=2+k}^{T+1} \log P^{k}_{i}\big[x_i\big], \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{MTP}} = \frac{\lambda}{D}\sum_{k=1}^{D}\mathcal{L}^{k}_{\text{MTP}} \]

added to the main next-token loss. DeepSeek-V3 uses \(D=1\) — a single MTP module — with \(\lambda=0.3\) for the first 10T tokens and \(\lambda=0.1\) for the remaining 4.8T, annealing the auxiliary objective down as pretraining proceeds.

Note what the chain buys and what it costs. It buys conditioning: module 2's prediction of \(x_{i+3}\) is made given \(x_{i+1}\) and \(x_{i+2}\), so the factorization is a genuine one and its samples are coherent. It costs parallelism: module \(k\) cannot start until module \(k-1\) finishes, so wall-clock grows linearly in \(D\) at both train and draft time. With \(D=1\) that cost is a single extra block — which is a large part of why \(D=1\) is a defensible default (see the next figure).

Why \(D=1\) captures most of the win

Suppose head \(k\)'s conditional acceptance — the probability it is right given every shallower head was right — decays geometrically, \(\alpha_k = \alpha\,\rho^{\,k-1}\) for some decay factor \(\rho<1\). A draft block is consumed in prefix order, so the expected number of tokens confirmed per verification step is the sum of prefix products:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\tau] \;=\; 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{D} \prod_{j=1}^{k} \alpha_j \;=\; 1 + \sum_{k=1}^{D} \alpha^{k}\,\rho^{\,k(k-1)/2} \]
D = 1 D = 2 D = 4 D = 8

Depth has sharply diminishing returns, and the decay rate decides how sharply. At \(\rho=0.85\) the \(D=4\) and \(D=8\) curves nearly coincide — doubling the head count again buys under 10% even at \(\alpha=1\) — because the prefix product \(\alpha^k\rho^{k(k-1)/2}\) has a \(k^2\) term in the exponent, so deep heads contribute almost nothing even when \(\alpha\) is near 1. Slide \(\rho\) toward 1 (heads that barely degrade with depth) and the curves fan apart; slide it to 0.6 and everything past \(D=2\) collapses onto the \(D=2\) line. Since cost is linear in \(D\) for sequential modules while benefit saturates, small \(D\) is the sound default.

5 · The memory problem: \(D\) copies of the logits

The parameter cost of MTP is mild — one block plus a projection per depth, against a trunk of many blocks. The activation cost is not, and it comes from one place: the logits tensor. For batch \(B\), sequence \(T\), vocabulary \(V\), a single logits tensor is \(BTV\) elements, and \(V \sim 128\text{k}\) makes it the largest activation in the model by a wide margin. Naively holding \(D\) of them, plus their gradients, before backward runs is what blows up.

The fix is to never materialize more than one at a time. Forward the trunk once, then for each head in turn: compute its logits, compute its loss, run backward through that head only accumulating into the trunk's output gradient, and free the logits before starting the next head.

# h: (B, T, d) — trunk output, computed once, retains grad
h = trunk(inputs)
h.retain_grad()
total = 0.0
for k in range(D):
    logits_k = heads[k](h)                      # (B, T, V) — the big one
    loss_k   = cross_entropy(logits_k[:, :-(k+1)], targets[k])
    loss_k.backward(retain_graph=True)                # grads land in h.grad
    total += loss_k.detach()
    del logits_k                                 # freed before the next head
h.backward(gradient=h.grad)                       # one pass back through the trunk

Peak activation memory for the head stack drops from \(\mathcal{O}(D\cdot BTV)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(BTV + D\cdot BTd)\) — one logits tensor, plus \(D\) hidden-sized tensors that are smaller by the factor \(V/d \approx 30\text{–}40\). The trunk is traversed backward exactly once regardless of \(D\). This is the same trade as activation checkpointing: order the graph so the expensive tensor has a short lifetime.

6 · What it actually buys — and where it does not

The empirical picture from Gloeckle et al. has one genuinely surprising feature, and reporting it honestly matters more than the headline gains.

RegimeEffect of MTP pretrainingReading
Small models Neutral to harmful on downstream benchmarks Capacity spent on depth-\(k\) marginals is capacity taken from the depth-1 task the model has not yet mastered.
Large models Improving, and increasingly so with scale The benefit is not a fixed regularizer bonus — it grows with capacity. Extrapolating from a small-scale ablation would have told you to abandon the method.
Generative / coding tasks Largest gains (HumanEval, MBPP-style) Tasks with long-range structural commitments — the choice points of §1 — are where lookahead supervision pays.
Multiple-choice / likelihood evals Roughly flat Scoring a fixed continuation never exercises the planning the extra heads induce.
Inference speed (heads retained) Up to ~3× with self-speculative decoding Orthogonal to quality; see §7.
The scale dependence is the trap. MTP is one of the clearer cases where a method's sign flips with model size. A 100M-parameter ablation showing MTP is worse is not evidence against it — it is the expected result. Treat any MTP decision made below the scale you intend to ship at as uninformative.

Two further honest caveats. First, MTP is an auxiliary objective: the main head still trains on next-token prediction, and the extra heads can be discarded at the end with the main model bit-identical in architecture to a normally-trained one. That makes MTP cheap to try and cheap to abandon. Second, \(\lambda\) is a real hyperparameter — too large and the depth losses compete with the objective you actually care about, which is why DeepSeek-V3 anneals it downward.

7 · The second payoff: the heads are a draft model

Everything above justified MTP as a training objective. But once trained, the extra heads propose tokens at \(t+2,\dots,t+D\) — which is precisely the input speculative decoding needs. Two deployment choices follow.

Discard the heads

Serve the main model alone. MTP was pure training-time regularization; inference is byte-for-byte a standard model with no extra latency, memory, or serving complexity.

Keep the heads

Use them as the drafter and verify with the main model's own forward pass — self-speculative decoding. No second model to train, host, or keep in sync. Output distribution is unchanged by the acceptance test.

The advantage over bolting on a drafter afterward is that the draft head was trained jointly with the target, on the target's own hidden states, against the true corpus — not distilled post-hoc to imitate the target. It never has to close a distribution gap because it was never on the other side of one. DeepSeek-V3 reports an acceptance rate of 85–90% for its second token and roughly 1.8× the tokens per second when its single MTP module is retained for speculative decoding.

The two designs of §3 and §4 map onto the two drafter families in speculative decoding. Parallel MTP heads are Medusa-style drafters: one pass, \(\gamma\)-independent cost, and suffix decay from exactly the independence-of-marginals problem flagged in §2. Sequential MTP modules are closer to EAGLE-style drafters: conditioned on real hidden states and on the previously drafted token, higher acceptance, but with per-depth latency. The training-time and inference-time literatures converged on the same two-point design space from opposite directions.

8 · Summary

The objective

\(-\sum_t\sum_{k\le D}\log p^{(k)}_\theta(x_{t+k}\mid x_{\le t})\). Labels are free — the corpus already contains them, shifted \(k\) places. \(D=1\) is ordinary pretraining.

Why it works

Positions whose choice constrains a long span get scored \(D\) times; trivially-predictable positions do not. An unsupervised reweighting toward choice points.

Parallel heads

\(D\) heads on one shared \(h_t\). Cheap and fully parallel, but the heads are independent marginals, so accuracy decays fast with depth. Structurally identical to Medusa.

Sequential modules

DeepSeek-V3: module \(k\) takes \(h^{k-1}\) plus \(\text{Emb}(x_{t+k})\), preserving the causal chain. Shares embedding and output head. \(D=1\), \(\lambda\) annealed 0.3 → 0.1.

Memory

The \(BTV\) logits tensor dominates. Forward-and-backward one head at a time to keep peak at one logits tensor instead of \(D\).

The scale caveat

Hurts small models, helps large ones, gains grow with scale. Small-scale ablations mislead. Largest effect on generative and coding tasks.

Free drafter

Retain the heads and self-speculate: no separate draft model, no distillation gap. DeepSeek-V3 reports 85–90% second-token acceptance, ~1.8× TPS.

Choosing \(D\)

Benefit saturates as \(\alpha^k\rho^{k(k-1)/2}\) while sequential cost grows linearly. \(D\) of 1–4 covers nearly all of the achievable gain.

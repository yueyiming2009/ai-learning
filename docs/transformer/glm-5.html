Model Architectures

GLM-5

The DeepSeek stack as an industry standard — adopted wholesale, then sharpened piece by piece: fatter MLA heads, sparse attention retrofitted in 20B tokens, one indexer shared across four layers, per-head Muon orthogonalization, and three MTP steps from a single weight set.

GLM-5 (Zhipu / Z.ai, February 2026, MIT license) is the clearest evidence that the DeepSeek architecture became the field's shared substrate. Every major component — MLA, DSA, aux-loss-free MoE routing, MTP — is adopted, and every one of them is modified, each change a targeted engineering fix rather than a research bet. Then the June revision, GLM-5.2, contributes this family's genuinely new idea: IndexShare, the depth-axis answer to sparse attention's indexer bottleneck.

One spec discrepancy worth recording: the tech report twice says the layer count was reduced "to 80", but the released config.json and checkpoint index say 78 backbone layers (+1 stored MTP module). This page follows the checkpoint.

1. MLA-256 — Fewer, Fatter Heads

GLM-5 keeps MLA's latent-compression machinery intact — 512-dim KV latent plus 64 decoupled RoPE dims, the familiar 576 cached elements per token — and reshapes the heads that read it: head dimension up from 192 to 256, head count down by a third (to 64). Total attention width barely moves; its partition changes. The report frames the win as hardware-agnostic efficiency: fewer, larger heads mean fewer but bigger matmuls (better tensor-core utilization on any accelerator, not just the H800s DeepSeek's original shapes were tuned for) and a lower projection-overhead-to-arithmetic ratio, at no cache cost — the cache depends on the latent, not the head geometry. The same fatter-heads instinct shows up independently in Qwen3.5 (head dim 256) and V4's 512-dim shared-KV head: 128-dim heads, a constant since GPT-2, are quietly on the way out.

2. DSA on Every Layer — Retrofitted in 20B Tokens

GLM-5 runs DSA — lightning-indexer top-\(k\) selection in front of core attention — on all 78 layers: a 32-head × 128-dim indexer per layer, top-2048 selection. Two things distinguish the adoption:

  • It was a retrofit, not a pretraining bet. The indexers were added by continued pretraining: a 1,000-step warm-up in which only the indexers train (distilling from the dense model's own attention distributions), then just ≈20B tokens of sparse adaptation. Against a 28.5T-token run that is a rounding error — strong evidence that learned selection is cheap to add to any trained dense model, the same lesson the hybrids-by-conversion line reached for linear attention.
  • Deterministic top-k. GLM-5 replaces the selection operator with deterministic torch.topk, so tie-breaking is bitwise stable. The motive is RL: if rollout and trainer disagree on which entries tie into the top-2048, the policy that generated a token and the policy being graded on it silently differ — the same train/inference-mismatch class of bug that routing replay repairs for MoE, and that V4 attacks with fully deterministic kernels. Sparse selection is a discrete decision, and discrete decisions inside RL loops must be reproducible.

3. IndexShare — One Indexer per Four Layers

After DSA, the indexer's linear scan over the history is the dominant long-context cost — the conclusion the sparse attention page's cost plot builds to. V4's answer was to compress what the indexer scans (4 tokens → 1 summary). GLM-5.2's answer, from the IndexCache work, is orthogonal: stop giving every layer its own indexer. Adjacent layers' attention concentrates on heavily overlapping token sets, so one layer's top-2048 selection is nearly right for its neighbors.

The mechanism

Layers are typed full (own indexer) or shared (reuse the nearest full layer's selection). GLM-5.2's released config: the 3 early dense-FFN layers plus every 4th layer are full — 21 full indexers serving 78 layers, a steady-state 1 : 4 ratio. The shared pattern is trained, not bolted on: in the sparse-adaptation phase, each surviving indexer distills against the average attention distribution of all the layers it serves, \(\mathcal{L} = \sum_j \tfrac{1}{m+1}\sum_t \mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_t^{(\ell+j)} \,\|\, q_t^{(\ell)}\bigr)\), so it learns to be a good consensus predictor rather than a layer-\(\ell\) specialist. The IndexCache paper's finding: with this training, fixed uniform interleaving matches a greedy-searched layer pattern — which is why the shipped config is a plain 1-per-4.

The sequel to the DSA cost curve: amortize the scan across depth. Per-token attention cost, averaged per layer (illustrative constants: MLA-256 core ≈65K MACs/entry over the top-2048; indexer ≈4K MACs/entry over all \(T\)), log–log. Both sparse schemes share the constant core-attention floor; what differs is the indexer slope, cut ≈3.7× when 21 indexers serve 78 layers. At the 200K native context the gap is modest; at GLM-5.2's 1M context the indexer dominates everything and the sharing is the difference — the model card's official figure is 2.9× lower per-token attention FLOPs at 1M. Compare V4's CSA, which flattens the same slope by scanning 4:1 summaries: two orthogonal divisors of the same term, and nothing stops a future model composing them.

4. Muon Split — Stability from the Optimizer Side

GLM-5 trains with Muon and introduces "Muon Split": MLA's up-projection matrices are split per attention head and each head's slice is orthogonalized independently, rather than orthogonalizing the fused matrix once. This is the same insight as Kimi K3's Per-Head Muon, reached independently in the same half-year — a fused projection is really many per-head operators, and Newton–Schulz should see them separately so no single loud head sets the update scale for all of them.

The claimed payoff is notable: attention logits stay stable through pretraining with no clipping strategy at all. Set that against the field's other fixes for the same pathology — Kimi clips QK weights (MuonClip), Qwen normalizes (QK-norm), gpt-oss adds sinks — and GLM's position is that exploding attention logits are an optimizer artifact: balance the per-head update scales and the pathology never develops. Three layers of the stack (weights, activations, optimizer), three working fixes for one failure mode.

5. Three MTP Steps, One Weight Set

DeepSeek-V3 shipped one sequential MTP module; adding more depth-\(k\) modules buys drafting accuracy but each costs a full extra layer of parameters and memory. GLM-5's move: unroll three MTP steps during training but make them share one set of weights — the released checkpoint stores exactly one extra layer (num_nextn_predict_layers: 1), applied recurrently. Memory cost of V3, training signal and drafting depth of three modules. As a self-speculative drafter at 4 draft steps it reaches 2.76 accepted tokens per verification vs. 2.55 for DeepSeek-V3.2's single module — and GLM-5.2's retuning (the shared indexer also serves MTP iterations, index_share_for_mtp_iteration) pushes acceptance another ≈20%. The MTP head's drift from training aid to serving asset — visible across Qwen3.5's multi-step training and K3's EAGLE conversion — is here too, as weight-tying.

6. The MoE — Deliberately Standard

The FFN side is DeepSeek-V3's recipe with the dials moved: 256 routed experts (expert dim 2048), top-8 plus one shared expert, sigmoid scoring with aux-loss-free bias balancing (noaux_tc, scaling factor 2.5), first 3 layers dense. The one stated design driver is systems-shaped: more experts across fewer, wider layers to cut expert-parallel communication rounds — each all-to-all is amortized over a bigger layer. Like Qwen3.5, GLM spent its architecture-risk budget elsewhere; unlike K3, no new router, no latent dispatch.

7. Training and Serving Notes

  • 28.5T tokens, with long-context mid-training staged 32K (1T tokens) → 128K (500B) → 200K (50B), using synthetic interleaved packing at the long stages.
  • INT4 QAT at the SFT stage — quantization enters before RL, so the policy that trains is the policy that serves (the same serve-what-you-train logic as K3's MXFP4 pipeline), with a W4A8 path targeting Chinese accelerators.
  • RL on slime — the async, decoupled generation/training framework (covered in the RL series), with reasoning RL at group size 32 and a final on-policy cross-stage distillation (cascade pattern).

8. GLM-5 Against Its Contemporaries

Among 2026's frontier open models, GLM-5 is the consolidator: it neither bets on recurrent state (Qwen, Kimi) nor redesigns attention wholesale (V4), but takes the proven MLA + DSA line and squeezes it — per-head optimizer treatment, shared indexers, shared MTP weights, INT4 QAT. The result is arguably the best cost-per-capability engineering of the generation, and the strongest signal of which techniques have become defaults.

9. Summary

Thesis

The DeepSeek stack, adopted wholesale and sharpened at every joint — the consolidator of the 2026 generation.

MLA-256

Head dim 192 → 256, ⅓ fewer heads, same 576-element cache — hardware-agnostic head geometry.

DSA retrofit

Indexers added to a dense model in 1K warm-up steps + 20B tokens; deterministic top-k for RL safety.

IndexShare (5.2)

1 indexer : 4 layers via consensus distillation — 2.9× per-token FLOPs at 1M context.

Muon Split

Per-head orthogonalization; attention logits stable with no clipping — the optimizer-side fix.

Shared MTP

3 recurrent draft steps, 1 weight set: acceptance 2.76 vs 2.55 at V3's memory cost.

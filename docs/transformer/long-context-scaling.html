Long-Context Scaling

Make a model use longer inputs, not merely accept them: choose a validated recipe or an adaptation route, build useful long-context data, and qualify both capability and execution cost.

Scope: this guide owns long-context adaptation choices. RoPE owns rotation/gradient derivations, attention owns Q/K/V and softmax mechanics, and the linked systems pages own execution. Data design and usable-context evaluation are core requirements. Among positional methods, YaRN receives the most depth because of documented deployment use; SSMax is a practical complementary idea, while LeRoPE is a focused emerging technique. This is an editorial priority, not a usage survey.

2. Choose a deployment route or a training route

First audit the checkpoint and target workload: native and previously validated lengths, input/output token budgets, rotary dimensions, frequency/gain settings, attention/cache layout, and document-boundary policy. A model that already meets the target may need no new training. A recipe validated on another checkpoint is a candidate, not an inherited guarantee.

Data design: length and domain are separate knobs

Data Engineering for Scaling Language Models to 128K Context separates the domain mixture from the length distribution. Globally selecting the longest documents can silently increase books or code while reducing other domains. Its LLaMA-2 experiments favor retaining the domain mixture while upsampling long documents within each source; book-heavy improvements did not reliably transfer to other domains.

Choose the intended domain mixture.
Within each domain, adjust the document-length distribution.
Track realized domain shares, lengths, and tokens after packing.
Evaluate both long-context behavior and short-context retention.

Declare whether mixture weights count documents, examples, or tokens: preserving one does not automatically preserve the others. Also distinguish long sequences from long dependencies. Packing unrelated short documents into a large tensor can improve utilization without creating a task that needs information from opposite ends of that tensor. Prefer relevant natural structure and tasks that actually require distant evidence; padding is not a substitute.

Evidence boundary: the paper studies LLaMA-2 7B/13B with a particular data mixture and RoPE-base adjustment, evaluates retrieval and book QA, and reports retrieval differences even at similar validation loss. Its token budgets and hardware results are not universal adaptation requirements or proof of arbitrary long-context reasoning.

Continued pretraining and long-input SFT teach different things

StageTraining signalWhat to verify
Continued pretrainingModel representative long text under the selected positional/attention recipe, retaining suitable short-context data.Loss by domain/position, retrieval and distant-evidence tasks, plus short-context regressions.
Long-input instruction tuningAnswer a request requiring the long input: QA, synthesis, code understanding, or summarization. Usually train on response tokens, not the prompt.Following the request, grounding the answer, combining evidence, and retaining ordinary short-input behavior.

LongAlign first extends its base models, then compares instruction-tuning data and training strategies. In those experiments, context extension alone did not supply the same long-input instruction-following performance as diverse long-instruction SFT mixed with short instructions. This motivates separate stage gates, not a claim that every already aligned long-context checkpoint requires another SFT run.

LongRoPE2's mixed-window training is more specific than simply replaying short text: short segments use original RoPE with cross-document attention blocked, while long segments use rescaled RoPE. Its inference policy selects the corresponding short/long schedule. If adopting that recipe, preserve the routing and cache contract; do not silently switch frequency schedules underneath already-rotated keys.

A progressive length curriculum is another candidate when direct extension is unstable or costly. Neither a fixed number of stages nor a fixed short/long ratio follows from these papers for a new model. Compare a staged recipe against the simplest viable baseline at declared data and compute budgets.

Packing is an execution choice, not a free objective change

LongAlign uses packing or length-sorted batches to reduce idle work from uneven lengths. Independent SFT examples in a pack use block-diagonal attention so one example cannot read another's answer. Continued-pretraining recipes can choose a different document-boundary policy; record the mask and position-reset semantics rather than treating all concatenation as equivalent.

Decide whether the objective averages over target tokens or over examples. LongAlign's equal-example objective is \(\mathcal L=M^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{M}L_i/N_i\), where \(L_i\) is summed loss over \(N_i>0\) target tokens. Averaging pack means can produce a different objective: if one pack holds example A and another holds equal-target-length examples B/C, equal pack weights give A one half of the total weight and B/C one quarter each, rather than one third each. Mask prompts, handle empty targets, and aggregate numerators/denominators consistently across ranks and accumulation steps.

Equal-example weighting is the paper's choice, not the only valid objective. The general rule is to avoid changing sample weights accidentally when changing packing or batching. Source: LongAlign §3.3; stage handoffs: mid-training and post-training.

3. Positional adaptation: YaRN and alternatives

Compare what is changed and how it is selected

MethodMain changeSelection and adaptation
Position InterpolationDivide positions, equivalently all rotary frequencies, by one extension factor.A prescribed uniform mapping; the original recipe adapts the pretrained weights with longer-context fine-tuning.
Base / NTK-aware scaling familyAdjust the RoPE base so different bands change by different amounts.Rules and fixed/dynamic variants differ. A larger base is not identical to YaRN or a general extrapolation guarantee.
YaRNBlend frequency-band interpolation and attention calibration.Prescribed schedule with checkpoint-specific settings; validated inference recipes and continued-training uses both exist.

LongRoPE2Search scaling and the frequency-band transition using needle-answer perplexity.Then adapt model weights with mixed short/original-RoPE and long/rescaled-RoPE training.

LongRoPE2 scores the reference needle-answer tokens of controlled long-dependency examples, rather than letting easy background tokens dominate whole-document perplexity. That changes the search objective, not the need for held-out task evaluation. Its experiments on Phi3-mini-3.8B and LLaMA3-8B support the combined recipe at those scales; “near-lossless” is not a universal guarantee. Unlike LeRoPE's gradient-learned frequencies, these are search-selected schedules followed by weight adaptation.

YaRN means Yet another RoPE extensioN method. It combines frequency-dependent interpolation with attention-temperature calibration. It is not simply a larger RoPE base or a uniform division of all frequencies.

Start with a 4K-to-32K example

RoPE rotates coordinate pair \(i\) at angle \(p\theta_i\), where \(p\) is token position. To extend \(L_0=4096\) to \(L_1=32768\), set \(s=L_1/L_0=8\). Uniform position interpolation uses \(p/s\), equivalently \(\theta_i/s\). Position 32768 then reaches the angle previously reached at 4096.

Original:       angle = position * frequency
Uniform PI:     angle = position * (frequency / 8)

No tokens are removed or merged.
Every band's positional difference between neighbors is reduced eightfold.

That uniform change also slows high-frequency bands that distinguish nearby positions. YaRN instead preserves those bands while interpolating the slowly rotating bands whose newly encountered phases can be out of distribution.

Use wavelengths to choose which bands to change

A band's wavelength is \(2\pi/\theta_i\). Its number of rotations over the original context is \(r_i=L_0\theta_i/(2\pi)\). The original paper's interpolation blends:

\[ \theta_i'=\gamma_i\theta_i+(1-\gamma_i)\theta_i/s,\qquad \gamma_i=\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{r_i-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha},0,1\right). \]
BandPolicyIntuition
Fast: many rotations during original training\(\gamma=1\): keep the frequency.Preserve fine local positional variation.
Slow: little rotation during original training\(\gamma=0\): divide by \(s\).Avoid moving too far beyond the previously encountered phase range.
IntermediateBlend the two policies.Avoid a hard discontinuity between bands.

The paper uses \(\alpha=1,\beta=32\) in its Llama experiments. These are counts of rotations used to define the transition, not token windows. A band has no hard “cannot attend beyond this distance” cutoff.

YaRN also calibrates attention sharpness

Frequency interpolation is only one part of YaRN. The original Llama-family recipe multiplies both rotary queries and keys by \(m=1+0.1\ln s\), producing an attention logit gain \(m^2\) in its full-RoPE formulation. At \(s=8\), \(m\approx1.208\) and the gain is approximately 1.46. This is an empirically fitted calibration, not a universal optimum.

scores = attention_gain * (Q_rotated @ K_rotated.T) / sqrt(key_dim)
probabilities = softmax(masked_scores)

Both Q and K must use the same frequency schedule. For partial RoPE or decoupled-RoPE MLA, scaling only rotary coordinates is not necessarily a uniform scale on the complete score. Match the model's implementation; do not blindly apply a second gain on top of an existing one.

Numerical reference, then an actual model recipe

This dependency-free Python function illustrates the paper's band blend and original gain rule. It does not replace a framework's model-specific YaRN implementation.

import math


def yarn_schedule(rotary_dim, base, original_length, factor,
                  slow_turns=1.0, fast_turns=32.0):
    if rotary_dim <= 0 or rotary_dim % 2:
        raise ValueError("rotary_dim must be positive and even")
    if base <= 1 or original_length <= 0 or factor < 1:
        raise ValueError("invalid base, original length, or extension factor")
    if not 0 <= slow_turns < fast_turns:
        raise ValueError("invalid transition")

    original = [base ** (-2 * i / rotary_dim)
                for i in range(rotary_dim // 2)]
    adapted = []
    for frequency in original:
        turns = original_length * frequency / (2 * math.pi)
        keep = min(1.0, max(0.0, (turns - slow_turns)
                           / (fast_turns - slow_turns)))
        adapted.append(frequency * (keep + (1 - keep) / factor))
    attention_gain = (1 + 0.1 * math.log(factor)) ** 2
    return adapted, attention_gain


frequencies, gain = yarn_schedule(64, 10000, 4096, 8)
assert len(frequencies) == 32
print(f"Illustrative full-RoPE logit gain: {gain:.3f}")

For deployment, prefer a verified checkpoint-specific recipe. For example, Qwen3-8B's model card documents a native 32,768-token window and validated use up to 131,072 with this configuration:

{
  "rope_scaling": {
    "rope_type": "yarn",
    "factor": 4.0,
    "original_max_position_embeddings": 32768
  }
}

That is not a universal recipe for other models. The same card cautions that static YaRN can degrade short-input performance and recommends enabling it only when needed. Input plus output must fit the serving budget. Library version, original-length semantics, partial rotary dimensions, and model-specific gain settings matter.

Do not silently change the rotation schedule underneath cached keys. If dynamic scaling changes frequencies as context grows, previously rotated keys and new queries must remain consistent. Use an implementation that manages this lifecycle; changing the factor without updating cache semantics is not sufficient.

4. SSMax: attention selection as the candidate set grows

Scalable-Softmax addresses a different issue. For one useful key with score advantage \(\Delta\) and \(n-1\) equally scored distractors, its ordinary-softmax probability is \(1/[1+(n-1)e^{-\Delta}]\). At fixed \(\Delta=5\), this falls from 22.5% for 512 keys to 0.45% for 32,768 keys. This fixed-score construction illustrates dilution; it is not a theorem that every trained head must flatten as context grows.

The basic proposal uses softmax(s * log(n) * scores). With positive scaling, score differences can grow with the number of competitors. This neither identifies the correct key nor resolves ties: sharpening can amplify an incorrect top-scoring distractor.

Ordinary softmax SSMax, no bias

More candidates change the normalization problem. X: log2(key count), from 512 to 131,072. Y: probability of one higher-scoring key. Every distractor has score zero; the selected key has the adjustable advantage. This is a toy probability calculation, not accuracy or a learned model's attention. The controls include regimes where scaling does not prevent dilution.

Separate the paper, the variant, and the deployment stage

The paper also studies a layer/head-specific affine gain a * log(n) + b. Mistral's released implementation applies that form to Q, using n = position_ids + 1. Scaling Q is equivalent to scaling the QK logits, so a conventional softmax kernel can remain in place. The ordinary 1 / sqrt(key_dim) factor remains: dimension scaling and length scaling solve different problems.

gain = a[head] * log(position + 1) + b[head]
Q_scaled = gain * Q
scores = Q_scaled @ K.T / sqrt(key_dim)
probabilities = softmax(masked_scores)

The bias is inside the multiplicative gain; adding an identical bias to all logits would cancel in softmax. For dense unpadded causal attention, position plus one matches prefix length. For windows, packed sequences, or sparse selection, it need not equal the number of attended keys. Neither the appropriate length definition nor a learned head's gain can be inferred from a maximum-context setting.

import math


def scaled_softmax(scores, n, a, b=0.0):
    # Numerical reference: pass only valid, unmasked scores.
    # Choose n explicitly; position-based variants may not use len(scores).
    if not scores or n < 1:
        raise ValueError("need valid scores and a positive length")
    gain = a * math.log(n) + b
    logits = [gain * score for score in scores]
    peak = max(logits)
    weights = [math.exp(logit - peak) for logit in logits]
    total = sum(weights)
    return [weight / total for weight in weights]


example = scaled_softmax([5.0] + [0.0] * 511, n=512, a=0.25)
assert math.isclose(sum(example), 1.0)
print(f"Toy selected-key probability: {example[0]:.2%}")

The original paper reports benefits from pretraining with SSMax, but this is not evidence of widespread use from step one in named frontier models. Meta explicitly describes Llama 4 Scout's cited SSMax-related temperature scaling as inference-time. That is important adoption evidence, not proof of an identical pretraining recipe.

5. LeRoPE: learn the frequency correction

LeRoPE learns a scalar for each rotary frequency: learned_frequency[i] = base_frequency[i] * exp(alpha[i]). Zero initialization preserves the starting schedule. The base can be ordinary RoPE or an adapted schedule such as YaRN; YaRN is not a prerequisite.

The same learned frequencies rotate Q and K, preserving RoPE's relative-offset structure. Increasing a frequency shortens its wavelength; adapting to longer context does not mean every band must rotate more slowly. Frequencies are learned model parameters, not predicted separately for each input.

The original paper shares one learned scalar per band across layers and heads: 32 scalars for its 64 rotary dimensions. Its from-scratch 52M-to-2.5B ladder reports that the largest RoPE baseline requires approximately 3.4% more compute to match LeRoPE's loss. The reported ladder uses single-seed runs; it is promising evidence, not a universal improvement guarantee.

Training versus frozen inference: training requires learnable parameters and a gradient path through frequency construction and sin/cos. The paper excludes these parameters from weight decay and clips their gradients separately. Mistral's published inference implementation instead stores the learned multipliers as a buffer and computes rotary tables under no_grad; copying it unchanged does not train the frequencies.

Its low parameter cost and Mistral's use justify tracking it, but broader named-model adoption is less established in the sources checked here. Start with a controlled comparison against a strong fixed schedule, not an assumption that learning more positional parameters must help. LeRoPE changes rotary geometry; SSMax changes selection sharpness and can also be used in NoPE layers.

For ordinary full attention, the attention part of prefill grows quadratically with length and persistent KV storage grows linearly per producer. YaRN/LeRoPE/SSMax do not change those counts. The prefill/decode and KV-memory pages own the execution models. Hardware, achieved kernel efficiency, communication overlap and concurrency determine real wall time; do not turn an asymptotic formula into a universal speedup claim.

LongLoRA: an adaptation-efficiency recipe, not just LoRA

LongLoRA combines low-rank adaptation with trainable embedding and normalization parameters and shifted sparse attention during fine-tuning. Half the heads shift local group boundaries to permit information exchange across groups; masking must prevent unintended future leakage. The reported inference path returns to ordinary full attention.

Its ablations distinguish sparse training attention from the parameter-update policy: increasing ordinary LoRA rank did not close the tested perplexity gap as effectively as allowing embeddings and norms to train. This supports the combined Llama2-era recipe, not a guarantee that every architecture can train sparsely and serve densely. Evaluate retrieval and synthesis as well as loss, and budget full-attention inference separately. No framework implementation or benchmark is reproduced here.

Retrieval and compression change the input contract

When only a small fraction of a corpus matters, selecting evidence can be preferable to sending the entire corpus. Measure whether the selector retained the needed facts separately from whether the reader used them. Include an oracle-evidence comparison where possible. Summaries can lose exact details or cross-document dependencies; a larger native window can help only if the task and model benefit from the extra material. Lost in the Middle's retriever-reader study shows why more retrieved documents need not produce proportional answer-quality gains.

7. Qualify usable context, not only accepted length

Lost in the Middle holds questions and answer-bearing evidence fixed while varying document position or adding distractors. Its studied models often performed worse with evidence in the middle. This is motivation for position-sensitive checks, not a claim that every current model has an identical U-shaped curve.

RULER expands beyond a single needle to harder retrieval, multi-hop variable tracking, aggregation, and QA with distractors. Models that pass simple retrieval can still fail those tasks as length or complexity increases. RULER's published effective-length threshold is a benchmark-specific convention, not a universal service acceptance criterion.

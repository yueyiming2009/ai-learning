Behavior vs Target Policy

Every RL algorithm has two policies hiding inside it: the one that generated the data and the one you are trying to learn about. Whether they are the same policy is the on-policy / off-policy distinction — and it decides what you can reuse, what you must correct, and how much variance you pay.

📘
Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd ed.)

§5.5–5.7 (off-policy prediction via importance sampling), §6.4–6.5 (SARSA vs Q-learning), §7.3 (n-step off-policy), §11 (the deadly triad).

When we write "the policy \(\pi\)" in an RL algorithm, we are usually being sloppy. There are two distinct roles a policy can play, and they only coincide by choice:

  • The behavior policy \(\mu\) (also written \(b\) or \(\pi_b\)) is the distribution the actions in your dataset were actually sampled from. It is a fact about the data.
  • The target policy \(\pi\) is the distribution you want to evaluate or improve. It is a fact about your objective.

If \(\mu = \pi\) the method is on-policy. If \(\mu e \pi\) it is off-policy, and every expectation in your update that is "under \(\pi\)" is now being estimated with samples from the wrong distribution — which must be either tolerated (Q-learning), corrected (importance sampling), or constrained (PPO's clip). This page is that distinction on its own, before it reappears as \(\pi_{ heta_{ ext{old}}}\) in PPO, as the replay buffer in DQN, and as a stale inference engine in async RL.

1. Two Roles, One Symbol

Take any quantity an RL algorithm estimates — a value, a return, a gradient. It is defined as an expectation under the target policy:

\[ V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi,\ s'\sim P}ig[r + \gamma V^\pi(s')ig], \qquad abla J( heta) = \mathbb{E}_{ au\sim\pi_ heta}\Big[ extstyle\sum_t abla\log\pi_ heta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\hat A_t\Big]. \]

But an algorithm never sees \(\pi\)'s expectation directly. It sees a finite set of transitions \((s,a,r,s')\), and those transitions were generated by whatever policy was running when they were logged. Call that \(\mu\). The central question of every sample-based RL method is therefore:

The question

Samples come from \(\mu\). The expectation is over \(\pi\). When does a Monte Carlo average over the samples still estimate the right thing?

The honest answer is "only when \(\mu=\pi\)" — that is the on-policy case, and it is why on-policy methods are simple and unbiased. Everything else on this page is about what to do when you do not have that luxury, and why you so often don't.

On-policy (\(\mu = \pi\))

  • Act with the policy you're learning; discard data when the policy changes.
  • Plain averages are unbiased. No correction terms.
  • Exploration must come from \(\pi\) itself (\( arepsilon\)-soft, entropy bonus, sampling temperature).
  • Sample-hungry: each batch is used once.
  • REINFORCE, SARSA, A2C, vanilla policy gradient, MC control.

Off-policy (\(\mu e \pi\))

  • Learn about \(\pi\) from data generated by something else: an older version, an exploratory policy, a human, a replay buffer.
  • Needs a correction (importance weights), a structural trick (the \(\max\) in Q-learning), or a trust region.
  • Exploration and exploitation can be separated: \(\mu\) explores, \(\pi\) is greedy.
  • Sample-efficient: data can be reused many times.
  • Q-learning, DQN, DDPG/SAC, IS-corrected MC, PPO (mildly), async LLM RL.

2. Why You Would Ever Want Them to Differ

If on-policy is unbiased and simple, why is off-policy learning the norm in practice? Because the behavior policy is frequently not under your control, or you want two things from it that a single policy can't provide.

Reason 1 — The exploration / exploitation split

The target of control is a greedy policy \(\pi(s)=rg\max_a Q(s,a)\). A greedy policy never tries anything new, so if you also act greedily you never discover better actions. Off-policy learning lets \(\mu\) be exploratory (\( arepsilon\)-greedy, Boltzmann) while \(\pi\) stays deterministic-greedy. This is exactly Q-learning's structure — see §4.

Reason 2 — Data reuse

Rollouts are expensive: environment steps, GPU hours of LLM generation, human demonstrations. If every gradient step forces you to throw away the batch and regenerate, you pay the rollout cost for one update. An off-policy method can take \(K\) gradient steps on one batch (PPO's epochs), or keep a replay buffer of a million transitions (DQN) — but after the first step, the data is from \( heta_{ ext{old}}\), not \( heta\), so it is off-policy by construction.

Reason 3 — You don't control the behavior policy at all

Offline / batch RL learns from a fixed logged dataset (medical records, driving logs, past deployments). Learning from demonstrations means \(\mu\) is a human. In harnessed agentic RL, \(\mu\) is the inference engine's sampling distribution — with its own temperature, top-p, and numerical kernels — and it only approximately matches the trainer's \(\pi_ heta\).

Reason 4 — Systems lag

In an asynchronous trainer, generation and optimization run concurrently. A trajectory generated by weights version \(k\) arrives after the trainer has moved to \(k+3\). It was on-policy when it was generated and is off-policy when it is consumed. The staleness is a behavior/target gap — see §8.

4. The Canonical Pair: SARSA vs Q-Learning

The two TD-control algorithms differ in a single term, and that term is exactly the behavior/target distinction.

\[ ext{SARSA:}\quad Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + lphaig[\,r + \gamma\,Q(s',\,{\color{#0d9488}a'}) - Q(s,a)ig],\qquad {\color{#0d9488}a'}\sim\mu(\cdot\mid s') \] \[ ext{Q-learning:}\quad Q(s,a) \leftarrow Q(s,a) + lphaig[\,r + \gamma\,{\color{#d97706}\max_{a'}}Q(s',a') - Q(s,a)ig] \]

SARSA bootstraps from \(Q(s',a')\) where \(a'\) is the action the agent actually took next. That action came from the behavior policy, so the target estimates \(Q^\mu\) — the value of the exploratory policy including its mistakes. SARSA is on-policy: it learns about the policy it runs.

Q-learning bootstraps from \(\max_{a'}Q(s',a')\) — the value of the greedy action, regardless of what \(\mu\) did next. The target estimates \(Q^{\pi_{ ext{greedy}}} = Q^*\). The behavior policy only decides which \((s,a)\) pairs get updated; it has no influence on what they get updated toward. That is why Q-learning is off-policy without any importance weights: it is a one-step method, the one action \(a\) whose value is being updated was taken by \(\mu\) but is evaluated as a fixed \((s,a)\) pair, and the "under \(\pi\)" part — the next action — is handled analytically by the \(\max\) rather than by sampling.

The classic illustration is Sutton & Barto's cliff walk. Q-learning learns the optimal path along the cliff edge (and, with \( arepsilon\)-greedy behavior, keeps falling off it during training). SARSA learns the safer path one row up, because it is evaluating the policy that does occasionally take random actions, and a random action next to a cliff is expensive. Both are correct — they are answering different questions.

The general principle behind "why doesn't Q-learning need weights" is worth stating separately, because it governs every algorithm in the table:

When you need a correction

Importance weights are required wherever an expectation over \(\pi\) is estimated by sampling an action from \(\mu\). If the expectation over \(\pi\) can instead be computed analytically — a \(\max\), or a sum over a small action set — no weights are needed. Multi-step returns sample many actions in sequence, so they need many weights.

5. Importance Sampling: The General Correction

For any expectation that genuinely must be estimated by sampling under \(\mu\), the fix is the identity from importance sampling:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[f(a)] = \sum_a \pi(a) f(a) = \sum_a \mu(a)\, rac{\pi(a)}{\mu(a)}\,f(a) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\mu}\!\left[ rac{\pi(a)}{\mu(a)}\,f(a) ight], \]

valid whenever \(\mu(a)>0\) wherever \(\pi(a)>0\) (the coverage or absolute-continuity condition — the behavior policy must be able to produce every action the target would). For a full trajectory, the transition dynamics \(P(s'\mid s,a)\) are the same under both policies and cancel, leaving a product over action probabilities only:

\[ ho_{t:T} \;=\; rac{\Pr_\pi( au_{t:T})}{\Pr_\mu( au_{t:T})} \;=\; rac{\prod_k \pi(a_k\mid s_k)\,P(s_{k+1}\mid s_k,a_k)}{\prod_k \mu(a_k\mid s_k)\,P(s_{k+1}\mid s_k,a_k)} \;=\; \prod_{k=t}^{T} rac{\pi(a_k\mid s_k)}{\mu(a_k\mid s_k)}. \]

Off-policy Monte Carlo prediction then estimates \(V^\pi\) from returns generated under \(\mu\):

Ordinary importance-sampled MC
\[ V^\pi(s) pprox rac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} ho^{(i)}_{t:T}\, G^{(i)}_t \]

This is unbiased. It is also, for any nontrivial horizon, useless in practice — the next section shows why. The usual mitigations are:

  • Weighted (self-normalized) IS: divide by \(\sum_i ho^{(i)}\) instead of \(N\). Biased for finite \(N\), consistent, and dramatically lower variance.
  • Per-decision IS: reward \(r_k\) only depends on actions up to \(k\), so it only needs \( ho_{t:k}\), not the full \( ho_{t:T}\). Shorter products, less variance, still unbiased.
  • Truncation / clipping: cap each \( ho_k\) at \(ar c\) (Retrace, V-trace in IMPALA, PPO's clip). Introduces bias in exchange for bounded variance — the trade that every practical algorithm makes.
  • Keep \(\mu\) close to \(\pi\): the only mitigation that attacks the root. PPO's whole design is this.

6. The Variance Problem

The importance weight has mean one under \(\mu\), but its variance is governed by how different the policies are, compounded over the horizon. Take the simplest analytic case — Gaussian action distributions with unit variance whose means differ by \(\Delta\), \(\mu=\mathcal N(0,1)\), \(\pi=\mathcal N(\Delta,1)\). Then \(\mathbb{E}_\mu[ ho]=1\) and

\[ \mathbb{E}_\mu[ ho^2] = e^{\Delta^2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}_\mu[ ho] = e^{\Delta^2}-1 . \]

Across a horizon of \(T\) independent decisions each with the same gap, the trajectory ratio has \(\mathbb{E}[ ho_{1:T}^2]=e^{T\Delta^2}\). A standard way to read this is the effective sample size — the number of equally-weighted samples your \(N\) weighted samples are worth:

\[ rac{N_{ ext{eff}}}{N} pprox rac{1}{\mathbb{E}_\mu[ ho^2]} = e^{-T\Delta^2}. \]

That exponent is the whole story. A per-step gap that is negligible at \(T=1\) is catastrophic at \(T=100\) — and an LLM response is a trajectory of hundreds to thousands of token-level decisions.

Per-step gap \(\Delta\) on the x-axis; \(\mu=\mathcal N(0,1)\), \(\pi=\mathcal N(\Delta,1)\).
\(N_{ ext{eff}}/N = e^{-T\Delta^2}\) (trajectory-level weight) \(N_{ ext{eff}}/N = e^{-\Delta^2}\) (single step, \(T=1\)) 10% of samples remain

Trajectory-level importance weighting collapses exponentially in horizon. Drag \(T\) up and watch the teal curve fold into the y-axis: at \(T=64\) a per-step gap of just \(\Delta=0.2\) already leaves only \(e^{-2.56}pprox 8\%\) of your samples effectively contributing. The single-step curve (violet) is what per-token PPO / GRPO ratios see — one factor, not a product — which is why LLM RL applies the ratio per token and clips it, rather than importance-weighting whole responses.

Note that variance is not the only failure. When \(\mu\) has zero probability where \(\pi\) does not, the estimator is biased no matter how many samples you draw — you simply never see those actions. This is the coverage condition failing, and it is the central obstacle in offline RL: the dataset does not contain the actions the target policy wants to take, so their values are extrapolated rather than estimated, and a \(\max\) over Q will happily pick the most over-estimated one.

7. On-Policy Is a Point; Off-Policy Is a Spectrum

It helps to stop thinking of on/off-policy as a binary and think of it as a distance \(D(\mu\,\|\,\pi)\). Algorithms are organised by how far from zero they allow that distance to go, and what they pay for it:

\(\mu = \pi\)REINFORCE, SARSA, A2C
unbiased, one-shot data
\(\mu pprox \pi\)PPO, GRPO, V-trace, async RL
clipped ratio, few epochs
\(\mu\) arbitraryQ-learning, DQN, SAC, offline RL
needs \(\max\) structure or coverage

Read from left to right:

  • Exactly on-policy: no correction, every batch used once. Simple and stable; pays in samples.
  • Proximal / trust-region: the batch is from \( heta_{ ext{old}}\) and the update is under \( heta\), but a clipped ratio (PPO) or a KL penalty (TRPO) keeps \(D\) small enough that the single-step ratio is well-behaved. After a few epochs the ratio drifts and the batch is discarded. This is the regime all modern LLM RL lives in. The PPO page derives the clip; the variance figure above is the reason it exists.
  • Fully off-policy value methods: replay buffers holding data from hundreds of past policies. Made possible by computing the "under \(\pi\)" expectation analytically through the Bellman optimality operator, not by weighting. The price is the deadly triad — off-policy data + bootstrapping + function approximation can diverge — and the over-estimation bias that Double DQN exists to fix.

8. Where This Shows Up in LLM RL

Large-scale LLM post-training is almost never exactly on-policy, even when it calls itself that. Three gaps between behavior and target are always present, and each one is a \(\mu e\pi\) in disguise:

Gap 1 — Multiple gradient steps per batch

PPO and GRPO generate a batch with \(\pi_{ heta_{ ext{old}}}\), then take several optimizer steps. From the second step on, \( heta e heta_{ ext{old}}\). The per-token ratio \( ho_t=\pi_ heta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{ heta_{ ext{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\) is the single-step importance weight; clipping bounds its contribution. Because it is per-token rather than per-sequence, the horizon exponent from §6 never appears — this is a deliberate design choice, not an accident.

Gap 2 — Inference engine vs trainer

Rollouts come from vLLM / SGLang; gradients from FSDP / Megatron. Even with identical weights, the two compute different log-probabilities: different kernels, different precision, different MoE routing decisions, sampling under temperature and top-p. The behavior policy is what the engine actually sampled from. If the trainer recomputes \(\log\pi_{ heta_{ ext{old}}}\) itself and uses that as the denominator, it is silently using the wrong \(\mu\). The fix — and the reason frameworks increasingly insist on the engine's own logprobs — is spelled out in Harnessed Agentic RL §7.

Gap 3 — Asynchrony and staleness

When generation and training are decoupled, a sample generated at weight version \(k\) is consumed at version \(k+d\). The behavior policy is \(\pi_{ heta_{k}}\), the target is \(\pi_{ heta_{k+d}}\), and \(d\) (the staleness) is a knob that trades throughput for off-policy distance. Async RL covers the corrections — ratio against the true behavior logprobs, decoupled PPO's separate proximal and behavior policies, and hard bounds on \(d\).

A practical diagnostic: log \(\mathbb{E}[ ho_t]\) and the clip fraction per step. On-policy, \( ho_t\equiv1\) and nothing clips. A mean drifting from one, or a clip fraction rising across epochs, is a direct measurement of how far the behavior policy has fallen from the target. Async RL Monitoring builds a dashboard around this.

Summary

Behavior policy \(\mu\)

Where the logged actions came from. A fact about the data.

Target policy \(\pi\)

What the expectation in your update is defined over. A fact about the objective.

On vs off

\(\mu=\pi\) → on-policy, plain averages are unbiased. \(\mu e\pi\) → off-policy, something must correct the gap.

Importance ratio

\( ho_t = \pi(a_t\mid s_t)/\mu(a_t\mid s_t)\); dynamics cancel, only action probabilities remain.

Variance

\(N_{ ext{eff}}/N pprox e^{-T\Delta^2}\) — the gap compounds over the horizon. Weight per decision, not per trajectory.

No-weights shortcut

If the expectation over \(\pi\) is computed analytically (\(\max_{a'}\)), no IS needed. That's Q-learning.

Coverage

\(\mu\) must give nonzero probability to every action \(\pi\) takes, or the estimator is biased regardless of \(N\).

In LLM RL

Three gaps: multi-epoch updates, engine-vs-trainer logprobs, async staleness. All are \(\mu e\pi\); all are handled with a clipped per-token ratio.

Imitation Learning

Two different problems: represent the expert's valid actions, then learn what to do in states the learner actually reaches.

Based on Chelsea Finn's CS224R Lecture 2 and official slides (April 4, 2025). Start with the MDP vocabulary: a policy chooses actions, and those actions influence its future observations. Here the training target comes from an expert, rather than an explicitly specified reward.

1. Behavior cloning: supervised learning of actions

Collect expert demonstrations, form state–action pairs, and fit a policy by maximum likelihood. Write \(s\) for the policy input; with partial observability, this may need to be an observation history rather than a single observation.

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mathcal{D}_E}\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s). \]

For discrete actions this is cross-entropy. For a Gaussian policy with fixed variance, minimizing negative log-likelihood is equivalent to squared-error regression of its mean. This offline baseline is simple and does not require learner rollouts during training. Low held-out action loss, however, does not by itself establish good closed-loop behavior.

2. Network capacity is not distribution expressivity

Suppose equally valid demonstrations go left or right around an obstacle: actions \(a=-1\) and \(a=+1\). The squared-error optimum is their conditional mean, \(a=0\), which in this example heads toward the obstacle. Enlarging the mean-predicting network does not turn its output into a bimodal distribution.

The lecture's robotics examples illustrate why diverse demonstrators can require a richer distribution family. Diffusion Policy is a concrete action-sequence approach, not evidence that diffusion is universally better than autoregression or that a robotics result transfers to LLMs.

3. Why small errors compound

Let \(d_\pi\) denote the state distribution induced by running policy \(\pi\), averaged over the task horizon. Demonstrations sample \(d_{\pi_E}\); deployment samples \(d_{\pi_\theta}\). A slightly wrong action can move the learner to a state absent from the demonstrations, where its next prediction is worse, causing further deviations.

Two independent failure modes

Expressive policies address which actions can be represented at a given input. Corrective data addresses which inputs receive expert supervision. A diffusion policy can still suffer covariate shift; DAgger cannot make an inadequate action distribution expressive.

This is a closed-loop distribution problem, not just memorization or ordinary random train/test splitting. Errors may be recoverable in some environments and catastrophic in others; no universal failure rate follows from a per-step prediction error alone.

4. DAgger: label the states the learner visits

Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) iteratively changes the supervised dataset rather than replacing expert labels with rewards:

  1. Initialize: train a learner on an initial expert demonstration dataset.
  2. Collect: roll out the current learner. The original algorithm can mix expert and learner control, reducing expert mixing over iterations.
  3. Label and aggregate: query the expert's action at visited states and append those labeled pairs to the accumulated dataset. The target is the expert's action, not the learner's possibly mistaken action.
  4. Retrain and repeat: fit on the aggregate, collect again with the updated policy, and evaluate candidate policies on held-out rollouts.

Unlike collecting more expert-only demonstrations, this acquires labels where the learner needs recovery behavior. But expert queries and online execution can be expensive or unsafe. The paper's no-regret reduction has assumptions; finite data, imperfect experts, and limited policy classes do not yield an unconditional success guarantee.

Online is not synonymous with exactly on-policy: DAgger collects fresh learner-influenced trajectories but trains on an aggregate of historical state distributions, possibly including expert-mixed rollouts.

5. Human-gated DAgger: who controls the system?

Asking a human to label actions while the learner controls the system is awkward: the human's commands may not produce the expected response. HG-DAgger instead lets the human decide when to take control and records corrective actions during those interventions. The original work also studies an uncertainty-based risk metric; human gating itself is not a proof of safety.

This improves the correction interface, but depends on timely detection, takeover, and recovery. Intervention data is selectively collected and is not the same as labeling every learner-visited state. Track who controlled each segment and do not treat uncorrected learner actions as expert-approved labels.

6. The bridge to LLM distillation

For an autoregressive LLM, a generated prefix is a policy input. Teacher-trace SFT trains on teacher prefixes; student-rollout distillation asks the teacher for supervision on prefixes the student produces. This shares DAgger's coverage motivation, but token-distribution KL supervision is not the same algorithm as aggregating expert action labels.

Keep data collection (fixed demonstrations, learner rollouts, or a mixture) separate from the learning signal (expert actions/distributions or rewards). Imitation can be interactive; RL can use offline data. Reward optimization and imitation can also be combined in one objective.

Learner-state supervision reduces the expert/learner coverage gap, not every possible distribution mismatch. Policy updates, stale samples, new prompts, tools, or environments can change the deployment distribution.

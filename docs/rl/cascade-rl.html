Alignment

Cascade RL

Sequential domain-specific RL stages with cross-stage distillation. The pattern that replaced single-phase RL in every frontier post-training recipe: specialize in stages, then consolidate without forgetting.

DeepSeek-R1's training recipe — described on the R1 page — mixes reasoning and general tasks in a single RL phase. That worked well at DeepSeek's scale. As labs pushed to cover more capabilities (reasoning, agentic coding, tool use, safety, helpfulness) in a single model, a new problem surfaced: RL signals for different domains interfere with each other, and specializing on one domain tends to degrade the others. The answer that Nemotron-Cascade 2, GLM-5, and MAI-Thinking-1 all converged on independently is Cascade RL: sequential domain-focused RL climbs, with explicit consolidation mechanisms between stages.

1. Why Single-Stage RL Breaks Down

Domain interference

When a single RL stage trains simultaneously on math, code, tool-use, and open-ended chat, the gradients from each domain point in different directions. A policy update that helps the model solve a differential equation may hurt its ability to write a bash one-liner, because the two tasks reward different token patterns at different positions. The more domains are mixed, the more each gradient is diluted by noise from the others.

Catastrophic forgetting

If you instead train sequentially — first RL for math, then RL for coding — the model forgets what it learned in stage 1 by the time stage 2 is done. This is catastrophic forgetting: the new gradient signal overwrites the weights that encoded the previous capability. Without an explicit mechanism to preserve stage-1 knowledge, sequential training is worse than joint training.

The gradient dead zone

A third problem specific to RL with binary rewards (covered in more detail in the curriculum section): when problems are too easy or too hard, the reward signal carries no learning information. Too easy: all \(G\) sampled responses are correct, all advantages are zero. Too hard: all responses are wrong, same result. A fixed task distribution will inevitably hit these dead zones as the model improves — problems that were once instructive become trivially easy, and the training signal collapses.

The naive fix — mix all domains, run one big RL job — produces a mediocre generalist that's worse at every domain than specialists trained separately. The sophisticated fix — cascade — gets you the specialist performance while keeping the generalist coverage.

2. The Cascade Pattern

Cascade RL solves domain interference and forgetting by separating the two concerns: each stage specializes without distraction, and a consolidation step between stages prevents forgetting. The general template:

Mid-trained model

The key constraint is that each stage runs a focused RL job — the reward environments, prompts, and verifiers are domain-specific. The model is not trying to improve on everything at once; it is climbing one hill at a time. The consolidation mechanism is what makes the sum greater than the parts.

The analogy to supervised learning: curriculum learning trains on easy examples first, then hard ones. Cascade RL trains on one domain first, then others. Both avoid the gradient noise of mixing everything simultaneously.

3. RLCS — Curriculum Sampling Within a Stage

Even within a single RL stage, the learning signal degrades as the model improves. Problems that challenged the model at the start of training become trivial by the end — and problems beyond the model's reach never produced useful gradients at all. Reinforcement Learning with Curriculum Sampling (RLCS, from GLM-5) addresses this by dynamically adjusting which problems are sampled for training.

The signal dead zone

With group-size \(G\) and binary rewards, the group-relative advantage for problem \(i\) is:

\[ \hat A_{i,t} = \frac{r_i - \bar r}{\sigma_r} \]

where \(\bar r\) and \(\sigma_r\) are the group mean and standard deviation. If all responses to a prompt are correct (\(\bar r = 1,\, \sigma_r = 0\)) or all wrong (\(\bar r = 0,\, \sigma_r = 0\)), the advantages are undefined or zero — there is no learning signal. The model should never see these problems during training.

RLCS scheduling

How RLCS works

RLCS maintains a running estimate of the model's current success rate \(p\) for each problem in the training pool. Problems are sampled with probability proportional to \(p(1-p)\) — favouring problems where the model sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails. As the model improves on a problem (p → 1), that problem is gradually de-prioritised and harder problems are introduced. As a result:

  • The training distribution shifts continuously to track the model's capability frontier.
  • The model always receives a dense gradient signal — never wasting compute on trivially easy or impossibly hard batches.
  • Harder problems are naturally introduced as the model becomes ready for them, similar to human tutoring.
Connection to GRPO group statistics

RLCS and GRPO interact naturally. GRPO already computes per-prompt success rates as a byproduct of group sampling: if 3 of 8 responses to a prompt are correct, \(p \approx 0.375\). RLCS simply feeds this per-prompt \(p\) estimate back into the sampling scheduler rather than discarding it after computing advantages. No additional inference is needed — the information is already available.

4. Consolidation — Preventing Forgetting Between Stages

After a domain-specific RL stage, the model has gained capability in that domain but has drifted away from the weights that encoded prior capabilities. Three distinct consolidation mechanisms have emerged, each representing a different point on the offline–online spectrum:

Trace-Distillation SFT

Used by: MAI-Thinking-1 (between specialist climbs and final RL)

Each specialist model generates rollouts on its domain. The best traces (verified correct, well-formatted) from all specialists are pooled into a single SFT dataset. The base model fine-tunes on this mixture before the final RL stage.

Key property: simple and stable — SFT is well-understood. The specialist models act as generators of high-quality training data, not as live teachers.

Offline
On-Policy Cross-Stage Distillation

Used by: GLM-5 (between each sequential RL stage)

During stage \(k\), the stage \(k-1\) model serves as a frozen teacher. The training loss is augmented with a KL penalty toward the teacher, evaluated on the student's own rollouts:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{teacher}} \| \pi_{\theta}) \]

This prevents the current stage from diverging too far from the previous stage's weights while still allowing RL improvement on the new domain.

On-policy
Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation (MOPD)

Used by: Nemotron-Cascade 2 / Nemotron 3 Ultra (as a final consolidation stage)

More than 10 domain-specialist models trained to peak performance each serve as a teacher. The student generates its own rollouts; each teacher provides token-level probability distributions on those rollouts. The student is trained to match the best teacher for each token:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{MOPD}} = -\sum_{k} w_k \sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{teacher}_k \text{ is best at } t] \]

Token-level routing means the student can simultaneously learn coding style from the code teacher, reasoning structure from the math teacher, and tool-call format from the agent teacher — on its own rollout distribution.

On-policy

5. Why On-Policy Distillation Outperforms Offline

The distinction between offline and on-policy distillation is the same as the distinction between behaviour cloning and RL — it is a distribution shift problem.

Offline distillation (standard knowledge distillation): teacher generates rollouts → student fine-tunes on them. The student learns to match the teacher's distribution. But at inference time, the student is generating from its own distribution, which may differ from the teacher's after even a few token steps — small deviations compound into completely different sequences. This is exactly the compounding error problem from imitation learning.

On-policy distillation: student generates rollouts → teacher is evaluated at those states → student learns to match the teacher on its own distribution. Because the teacher is consulted on states the student actually visits, there is no distribution mismatch. The student learns to behave like the teacher from the states the student actually reaches — which is the distribution it will encounter at inference time.

On-policy distillation loss (per student token)
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{OPD}}(t) = \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\text{teacher}(\cdot \mid s_t)\;\|\;\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big), \quad s_t \sim \pi_\theta \]

The state \(s_t\) is sampled from the student's own rollout. The teacher provides the target distribution; the student is trained to match it at that state. Crucially, if the student never visits a state, it receives no gradient for it — preventing the offline problem where irrelevant teacher states dominate training.

MOPD extends this to the multi-teacher case by routing each token to the most relevant specialist teacher. A token in a math derivation gets guidance from the math teacher; a tool-call token gets guidance from the agent teacher. The routing can be learned or rule-based (domain classification on the prompt).

6. Adaptive Entropy Control

As RL training progresses within a domain, the policy becomes increasingly confident — the entropy \(H[\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)]\) decreases as the model converges on winning strategies. This is usually desirable within a domain, but creates two problems across stages:

  • Within-stage collapse: if entropy falls too fast, the policy stops exploring alternative strategies and gets stuck in a local optimum. The model finds one way to solve problems and never discovers better ones.
  • Cross-domain rigidity: a low-entropy policy that's been tightly specialized for math is harder to fine-tune for coding in the next stage — the weights are more committed and the gradients smaller.

MAI-Thinking-1 uses adaptive entropy control: the entropy bonus coefficient \(c_2\) in the GRPO objective (analogous to PPO's entropy bonus \(-c_2 S[\pi_\theta]\)) is adjusted dynamically based on measured entropy:

  • If measured entropy drops below a target \(H_{\min}\), increase \(c_2\) to push entropy up.
  • If entropy is high (model still exploring freely), reduce \(c_2\) to allow convergence.

This keeps the policy in a productive exploration regime across the full training run — not so exploratory that it can't converge on good strategies, not so collapsed that it can't adapt when the domain changes in the next stage.

All four use GRPO (or a variant) as the within-stage RL algorithm. The differentiation is entirely in the stage structure and consolidation mechanism — not in the RL primitive itself. GRPO from the GRPO page is the shared foundation.

Parallel vs. sequential specialist training

MAI trains specialists in parallel (each specialist is an independent RL run) and merges afterwards. GLM-5 and Nemotron train sequentially (each stage inherits from the previous). The tradeoff:

  • Parallel: total wall-clock time is the length of the longest specialist run. But merging parallel specialists is harder — each has drifted independently from the base, and trace-distillation SFT is a blunt tool that may not perfectly reconcile contradictory specialist behaviors.
  • Sequential: wall-clock time is the sum of all stages. But each stage builds on the previous, so the model naturally learns to integrate capabilities. On-policy distillation is a finer consolidation mechanism than SFT.

Summary

Why cascade

Single-stage RL on mixed domains suffers gradient interference. Sequential stages specialize without distraction.

Forgetting fix

Cross-stage distillation (offline SFT, on-policy KL, or MOPD) preserves prior capability while the new stage trains.

RLCS

Sample problems where \(p \in [0.3, 0.7]\) — the group-advantage variance sweet spot. Adapts as the model improves.

On-policy advantage

Teacher is consulted on student-generated states, not pre-cached teacher states. Eliminates distribution mismatch.

MOPD

10+ domain-specialist teachers, token-routed on student rollouts. The generalist inherits each specialist's best behaviors simultaneously.

Entropy control

Adaptive entropy bonus prevents within-stage collapse and keeps the policy flexible enough to enter the next stage.

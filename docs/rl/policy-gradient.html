1. The Policy Gradient Theorem

Value-based methods (Q-learning, DQN) learn a value function and act greedily on it. Policy gradient methods parameterise the policy \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) directly, define a scalar objective \(J(\theta)\) = expected return, and do gradient ascent on \(\theta\). A critic is optional for REINFORCE and useful in actor–critic methods. The policy gradient theorem gives \(\nabla_\theta J\) in a form you can estimate from rollouts.

Where \(\theta\) sits in the objective

Write the objective as an expectation over trajectories \(\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \dots)\) sampled by running the policy:

\[ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[R(\tau)] = \sum_\tau \pi_\theta(\tau)\, R(\tau) \]

Compare this to a supervised loss \(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\text{data}}[\ell_\theta(x)]\). There the distribution is fixed and \(\theta\) is inside the averaged quantity — sample \(x\), backprop through \(\ell_\theta\). Here it is the other way round: the return \(R(\tau)\) is a number handed back by the environment with no \(\theta\) in it (and no gradient through it — the environment is a black box), while all of the \(\theta\) is in \(\pi_\theta(\tau)\), the probability of seeing that trajectory. Sampling \(\tau\) and backpropagating through \(R\) gives exactly zero. The whole theorem is about moving \(\theta\) from "inside the distribution" to "inside the thing being averaged".

Step 1 — Differentiate the sum

\(R(\tau)\) is constant in \(\theta\), so the gradient lands on the probability:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_\tau \big[\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(\tau)\big]\, R(\tau) \]

True, but not usable. A sum over all trajectories is not something we can compute. The only operation available in practice is to run the policy and look at the trajectories that come out, which computes averages of the form \(\sum_\tau \pi_\theta(\tau)\,[\cdots] = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[\cdots]\). The expression above has \(\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(\tau)\) where it would need a bare \(\pi_\theta(\tau)\), so it is not the average of anything. The next step restores that shape.

Step 2 — The log-derivative trick

The chain rule on \(\log\) gives \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau) = \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(\tau) / \pi_\theta(\tau)\). Rearranged:

\[ \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(\tau) = \pi_\theta(\tau)\,\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau) \]

Substituting puts a bare \(\pi_\theta(\tau)\) back in front:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \sum_\tau \pi_\theta(\tau)\,\underbrace{\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)}_{\text{averaged quantity}} = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\big] \]

This is now a sample average: roll out trajectories, compute \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau)\,R(\tau)\) for each, take the mean. The estimate is unbiased for the same reason any Monte Carlo average is. The identity is nothing more than multiplying by \(\pi_\theta(\tau)/\pi_\theta(\tau) = 1\), but it changes what gets measured: \(\nabla\log\pi\) is the relative change in a trajectory's probability ("make this one 5% more likely"), and relative changes are what samples can tell you about, because you only ever see trajectories in proportion to their probability.

Step 3 — The environment drops out

\(\pi_\theta(\tau)\) is the probability of a whole trajectory, and it contains dynamics we do not know. Factorise it into the initial state, the policy's choices, and the environment's responses:

\[ \pi_\theta(\tau) = p(s_0)\prod_{t} \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\, P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t, a_t) \]

The log turns the product into a sum of three kinds of terms:

\[ \log \pi_\theta(\tau) = \log p(s_0) + \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) + \sum_t \log P(s_{t+1}\mid s_t, a_t) \]

Now take \(\nabla_\theta\). The first and third terms contain no \(\theta\) — the environment does not depend on the agent's weights — so their gradient is identically zero. Only the policy terms survive:

\[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(\tau) = \sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) \]

This is the payoff of the whole derivation: the transition model \(P\) never has to be known or differentiated. Each remaining term is the log-probability of a single action the network output, which is ordinary backprop.

Putting the three steps together:

Policy Gradient Theorem
\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\!\left[\Big(\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)\;R(\tau)\right] \]

Read each sample's contribution as a signed push on its action log-probabilities. A positive raw return reinforces the sampled actions, even if that return is below average; a negative return suppresses them, and zero contributes nothing. Baseline subtraction can turn below-average outcomes into negative weights. Reward-to-go and valid action-independent baselines preserve the expected gradient; arbitrary reweighting does not, and bootstrapped GAE can introduce bias with an imperfect critic.

Two things that routinely confuse people. (1) The negative reward-weighted log-likelihood is an optimizer surrogate loss, not the expected-return objective itself. On fresh on-policy samples its gradient estimates \(-\nabla_\theta J\); its scalar value is not a performance metric. Evaluate returns separately. (2) Raw REINFORCE multiplies every action by the same \(R(\tau)\), including actions unrelated to a particular reward. Sections 2–3 address that variance.
Each \(\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\) is one log_softmax gather over the policy's output logits — \(z_{a_t} - \operatorname{logsumexp}(\mathbf{z})\). See computing log-probabilities from logits for the derivation.

From imitation to a weighted-likelihood surrogate

Behavior cloning increases the likelihood of expert actions. Policy gradients instead weight the learner's own sampled actions by returns or advantages. Holding sampled inputs and weights fixed during differentiation, minimize

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_t \operatorname{sg}[w_{i,t}]\,\log\pi_\theta(a_{i,t}\mid s_{i,t}), \qquad w_{i,t}=\gamma^t\big(G_{i,t}-b(s_{i,t})\big). \]

Here \(\operatorname{sg}\) means stop-gradient. This is weighted cross-entropy for categorical actions, or weighted squared-error regression of the mean for a fixed isotropic-variance Gaussian. Weights may be negative, so it is a signed gradient surrogate, not ordinary maximum likelihood with nonnegative example weights. Average episode sums across a batch; replacing them with a mean within each episode changes length weighting.

Minimal REINFORCE update

This CPU categorical-policy example retains the legacy Gym-style interface: reset() → obs and step(action) → (obs, reward, done, info). Assume at least one step, true termination rather than time-limit truncation, and no policy update during collection. It uses no baseline or return normalization. The default matches the lecture's undiscounted objective; other discounts use the explicit \(\gamma^t\) weights derived below.

import torch

def reinforce(policy, env, optimizer, gamma=1.0):
    if not 0.0 <= gamma <= 1.0:
        raise ValueError("gamma must be between 0 and 1")

    log_probs, rewards = [], []
    obs, done = env.reset(), False
    while not done:
        dist   = policy(torch.tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32))
        action = dist.sample()
        log_probs.append(dist.log_prob(action))   # tracks gradient through π_θ
        obs, r, done, _ = env.step(action.item())
        rewards.append(r)

    G, returns = 0.0, []
    for r in reversed(rewards):
        G = r + gamma * G
        returns.append(G)
    log_probs = torch.stack(log_probs)
    returns = log_probs.new_tensor(returns[::-1])
    time_weights = log_probs.new_tensor([gamma ** t for t in range(len(rewards))])

    # Rewards are fixed data; detach weights if built from a learned critic.
    loss = -(time_weights * returns * log_probs).sum()
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()

The loss gradient is a single-episode Monte Carlo estimate of \(-\nabla_\theta J_\gamma\), under the assumptions above. Its expectation is correct, not necessarily its direction on any one episode. With \(\gamma=1\), terminal-only reward \(R\) correctly weights every action by \(R\); a one-step episode also gives a finite update without estimating any standard deviation.

Worked example — why raw REINFORCE is noisy

A single state with two actions, \(A\) and \(B\), and a softmax policy with logits \(\theta = (\theta_A, \theta_B)\); currently \(\pi(A) = 0.6\), \(\pi(B) = 0.4\). For a softmax the score is \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi(a) = e_a - \pi\), with \(e_a\) the one-hot vector for \(a\):

\[ \nabla_\theta \log \pi(A) = (0.4,\; -0.4), \qquad \nabla_\theta \log \pi(B) = (-0.6,\; 0.6). \]

The episode ends after one step, deterministically: \(R = 1.0\) for \(A\) and \(R = 0.2\) for \(B\). Action \(A\) is clearly better, and the true gradient agrees:

\[ \nabla_\theta J = 0.6 \cdot 1.0 \cdot (0.4, -0.4) + 0.4 \cdot 0.2 \cdot (-0.6, 0.6) = (0.192,\; -0.192). \]

Now look at what a single REINFORCE sample does:

Sampled actionProbability\(R\)\(\hat g = R \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a)\)Direction for \(\theta_A\)
\(A\)0.61.0\((0.40,\; -0.40)\)up ✓
\(B\)0.40.2\((-0.12,\; 0.12)\)down ✗

Forty percent of the time the update moves \(\theta_A\) the wrong way. The average is right — \(0.6 \cdot 0.40 + 0.4 \cdot (-0.12) = 0.192\) — only because the correct pushes are larger than the incorrect ones. The first coordinate of \(\hat g\) has variance

\[ \operatorname{Var}[\hat g_A] = 0.6 \cdot 0.40^2 + 0.4 \cdot 0.12^2 - 0.192^2 \approx 0.0649, \]

a standard deviation of \(0.255\), larger than the gradient being estimated. The culprit is that both returns are positive: REINFORCE increases the probability of whichever action it happened to take, and relies on the better action being taken more often and pushed harder. The next section fixes exactly this.

2. Variance Reduction: From Return to Advantage

REINFORCE is correct but high-variance — every action is credited with the full trajectory return, including outcomes it had nothing to do with. Three reductions bring it to the form used in practice:

Reward-to-go (causality)

For the undiscounted objective, action \(a_t\) cannot affect rewards collected before it, so replace \(R(\tau)\) with the return-to-go \(G_t = \sum_{t'\ge t} r_{t'}\). The dropped early rewards contribute zero to the expected gradient:

\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\, r_{t'}\right] = 0 \quad \text{for } t' < t \]

Condition on the history before \(a_t\): the earlier reward is fixed and the action's score has zero conditional mean. Removing these terms is a standard variance-reduction device, but the size of the benefit is problem-dependent.

Discounted objectives need absolute time weights

When the objective discounts rewards from the start of each episode, define

\[ J_\gamma(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t r_t\right], \qquad G_t=\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}r_k. \]

Causality removes past rewards, but the remaining absolute discount factors are \(\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^k r_k=\gamma^tG_t\). Thus

\[ \nabla_\theta J_\gamma =\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t\big(G_t-b(s_t)\big)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\right]. \]

The state-only baseline remains valid with these weights. Equivalently, a state/time sampling measure can absorb \(\gamma^t\), with its corresponding normalization; uniformly averaging timesteps does not do that automatically. The code supports this start-state objective. All other unweighted policy-gradient sums in Sections 1–2 assume \(\gamma=1\); using discounted returns with unweighted timesteps is a different practical surrogate, not this exact gradient.

Baseline subtraction

A fixed function \(b(s_t)\), independent of the current sampled action conditional on the state, can be subtracted from \(G_t\) without introducing bias. Treat its output as a constant during the policy update:

\[ \hat g_b = \sum_{t} \big(G_t - b(s_t)\big)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat g_b] = \nabla_\theta J(\theta). \]
Step 1 — the score has zero mean under its own policy

Fix a state \(s\) and take the expectation of the score \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) over actions drawn from the policy itself. Write the expectation as a sum, apply the log-derivative identity in reverse, and notice the \(\pi_\theta\) cancels:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\!\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\big] &= \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) \\ &= \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\, \frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)} \\ &= \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) . \end{aligned} \]

The sum is over a finite set of actions and the gradient is linear, so the two can be exchanged. The sum of the probabilities is \(1\) for every \(\theta\), and the gradient of a constant is zero:

\[ \sum_a \nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \nabla_\theta \sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0 . \]

Check it on the example above: \(0.6 \cdot (0.4, -0.4) + 0.4 \cdot (-0.6, 0.6) = (0, 0)\). Geometrically, the score vectors of the different actions are arranged so that their probability-weighted average cancels — increasing one action's probability must decrease the others'.

Step 2 — a state-only baseline factors out of the action expectation

Take the baseline term at a single timestep, \(\mathbb{E}\big[b(s_t)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\big]\), where the expectation is over the whole trajectory. Split it with the law of total expectation: first average over \(a_t\) holding \(s_t\) fixed, then average over \(s_t\):

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[b(s_t)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{s_t}\Big[\; \mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)}\big[\, b(s_t)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \,\big] \;\Big] . \]

Inside the inner expectation \(s_t\) is fixed, so \(b(s_t)\) is a constant and can be pulled out — this is the one place the "state-only" assumption is used:

\[ = \mathbb{E}_{s_t}\Big[\; b(s_t)\, \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)}\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\big]}_{=\,0 \text{ by Step 1}} \;\Big] = \mathbb{E}_{s_t}\big[\, b(s_t)\cdot 0 \,\big] = 0 . \]

Now apply this to the full estimator. It is linear in the weighting, so it separates into the original REINFORCE term minus a sum of baseline terms, each of which is zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\hat g_b] &= \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t \big(G_t - b(s_t)\big)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\Big] \\ &= \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\Big]}_{=\;\nabla_\theta J(\theta)} \;-\; \sum_t \underbrace{\mathbb{E}\big[b(s_t)\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\big]}_{=\;0} \\ &= \nabla_\theta J(\theta) . \end{aligned} \]

The requirement is statistical independence from the sampled action conditional on the state, not merely omitting an action argument from a function. A baseline fitted using that very sample may be coupled to its action and return. Detaching it stops an unwanted gradient path but does not remove that statistical coupling.

Estimated baselines and normalization are not automatically unbiased

For \(N\) independent one-step samples from the same state, let \(z_i=\nabla\log\pi(a_i\mid s)\) and \(\bar R=N^{-1}\sum_jR_j\). Since the current return is included in its own baseline,

\[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_i(R_i-\bar R)z_i\right] =\left(1-\frac{1}{N}\right)\mathbb{E}[Rz]. \]

Cross-sample terms vanish by independence; the self-term subtracts \(1/N\) of the expected gradient. For \(N>1\), a leave-one-out mean excludes that self-term. This argument does not treat correlated timesteps of one episode as independent samples, nor does it establish unbiasedness after division by a random sample standard deviation.

The earlier per-episode normalization recipe is deliberately absent from the code. At \(\gamma=1\), a terminal-only reward makes every \(G_t=R\), so centering returns within that episode deletes its entire update. For one step, PyTorch's default sample std has zero degrees of freedom; adding epsilon afterward does not repair the undefined value. Population std avoids that numerical issue but not self-centering or statistical bias. Use raw returns, an independently fitted baseline, or a justified estimator; label optional normalization as a heuristic.
Step 3 — which baseline minimises variance

Any fixed state-only \(b\) preserves the expected gradient, so choose the one that makes the estimator least noisy. Work at a single state and write \(\psi = \nabla_\theta \log \pi(a)\), a random vector because \(a\) is random, and \(G\) the random return. The estimate is \((G - b)\,\psi\), and its total variance (summed over coordinates) is

\[ \operatorname{Var}\big[(G - b)\,\psi\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[(G-b)^2\, \|\psi\|^2\big] \;-\; \big\|\mathbb{E}\big[(G-b)\,\psi\big]\big\|^2 . \]

The second term is \(\|\nabla_\theta J\|^2\) — by Step 2 it does not depend on \(b\) — so only the first term needs minimising. Expand the square:

\[ \mathbb{E}\big[(G-b)^2\, \|\psi\|^2\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[G^2\|\psi\|^2\big] \;-\; 2b\, \mathbb{E}\big[G\,\|\psi\|^2\big] \;+\; b^2\, \mathbb{E}\big[\|\psi\|^2\big] . \]

This is a convex quadratic in the scalar \(b\). Differentiate and set to zero:

\[ \frac{\partial}{\partial b} = -2\,\mathbb{E}\big[G\,\|\psi\|^2\big] + 2b\, \mathbb{E}\big[\|\psi\|^2\big] = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad b^\ast = \frac{\mathbb{E}\big[G \,\|\psi\|^2\big]}{\mathbb{E}\big[\|\psi\|^2\big]} . \]

So the optimal baseline is a score-weighted average of the return: returns from actions whose score is large (actions whose probability the gradient can move a lot) count more. In practice the weights are dropped and the plain average \(b(s) = V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}[G \mid s]\) is used. It is close to \(b^\ast\) whenever \(\|\psi\|^2\) and \(G\) are weakly correlated, it is a quantity we already know how to estimate by sampling or bootstrapping, and it has a clean interpretation — the advantage, below.

Worked example, continued. The value of the state is \(V = 0.6 \cdot 1.0 + 0.4 \cdot 0.2 = 0.68\). Subtract it:

Sampled actionProbability\(R - V\)\(\hat g_b = (R - V)\cdot\nabla_\theta \log \pi(a)\)Direction for \(\theta_A\)
\(A\)0.6+0.32\((0.128,\; -0.128)\)up ✓
\(B\)0.4−0.48\((0.288,\; -0.288)\)up ✓

Now both samples move \(\theta_A\) the right way — sampling the worse action and seeing a below-average return is evidence for the better one. The mean is unchanged, \(0.6 \cdot 0.128 + 0.4 \cdot 0.288 = 0.192\), while the variance of the first coordinate is

\[ \operatorname{Var}[\hat g_{b,A}] = 0.6 \cdot 0.128^2 + 0.4 \cdot 0.288^2 - 0.192^2 \approx 0.0061 . \]
Effect of the baseline on this example

Same expected gradient \((0.192, -0.192)\); variance down from \(0.0649\) to \(0.0061\) — a factor of about \(10\), the equivalent of running ten times as many rollouts.

This example motivates critics and group sampling. \(V^\pi(s_t)\) must be estimated by a learned network or additional rollouts; a group mean estimates expected return, but its use inside the same samples' policy gradients has the self-inclusion caveat above. Even a crude baseline between \(0.2\) and \(1.0\) flips the sign of the \(B\)-sample's push.

Advantage

With the exact state-value function \(V^\pi(s_t)=\mathbb{E}[G_t\mid s_t]\) as the baseline, a sampled return gives an advantage estimate, not generally the exact advantage:

\[ \hat A_t=G_t-V^\pi(s_t), \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat A_t\mid s_t,a_t]=Q^\pi(s_t,a_t)-V^\pi(s_t)=A^\pi(s_t,a_t). \]

The advantage strips out "this state was generically good/bad" and isolates the signal that is actually about the action: how much better did this specific action perform compared to the average from \(s_t\)? Applying all three reductions:

Policy Gradient with Advantage
\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sum_{t} \hat A_t \, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \right] \]

Push up the log-probability of actions that outperformed expectations (\(\hat A_t > 0\)); suppress those that underperformed. The remaining question is how to estimate \(\hat A_t\) in practice — that is GAE.

3. Advantage Estimation — GAE

We need \(\hat A_t\) but \(V^\pi(s_t)\) is unknown and is approximated by \(V_\psi\). Subtracting an independently fixed state-only critic from a full Monte Carlo return preserves the expected policy gradient even when the critic is imperfect. Bootstrapping future returns from that imperfect critic can introduce bias, trading it for lower variance. The residual is not an unbiased estimate of the true advantage unless the baseline is correct. TD Learning §6 develops the return-estimation spectrum.

This section allows general \(\gamma\) and uses relative-discounted \(G_t\). To use its advantages for the start-state objective \(J_\gamma\), retain the outer \(\gamma^t\) policy-gradient weights or an equivalent sampling measure. Discounting inside the TD recursion alone does not supply them.

Define the one-step TD error:

\[ \delta_t = r_t + \gamma\, V_\psi(s_{t+1}) - V_\psi(s_t) \]

The \(n\)-step advantage is a discounted sum of \(n\) consecutive TD errors: \(\hat A_t^{(n)} = \sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l}\). Generalised Advantage Estimation (GAE) takes an exponentially-weighted average over every \(n\) — which collapses to a single discounted sum:

GAE
\[ \hat A_t^{\text{GAE}(\gamma,\lambda)} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l} \]
\(\lambda=0\) recovers one-step TD, \(\hat A_t=\delta_t\). At \(\lambda=1\), a complete episode with zero terminal value telescopes to \(G_t-V_\psi(s_t)\), preserving the policy-gradient expectation under the baseline and weighting conditions above. A time-limit cutoff instead needs an appropriate bootstrap. \(\gamma\) defines reward discounting; \(\lambda\) controls the bootstrapping trade-off. RLHF commonly uses \(\lambda\approx0.95,\ \gamma\approx1\).

Computing it efficiently

The infinite sum is never formed directly — it satisfies a one-line recursion run backward from the end of the trajectory, an \(O(T)\) pass:

\[ \hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\hat A_{t+1} \]

The TD(\(\lambda\)) return — training the critic

GAE assumed a critic \(V_\psi\); this subsection is where the critic comes from. The advantage was defined as return minus baseline, \(A_t = G_t - V(s_t)\), so rearranging any advantage estimate defines a matching return estimate:

\[ \hat R_t \;:=\; \hat A_t + V_\psi(s_t) \]

called the TD(\(\lambda\)) return. It is used in a two-stage pipeline repeated every training iteration — and keeping the two stages separate is the key to reading the formulas below:

  1. Build labels (no learning): after a rollout, run one backward pass that turns the rewards and the current critic's values into one plain number \(\hat R_t\) per timestep.
  2. Regress (learning): treat those numbers as supervised targets and fit \(V_\psi(s_t) \to \hat R_t\) by gradient descent on a squared error.

Stage 1 — the label factory

Adding \(V_\psi(s_t)\) to both sides of the GAE recursion \(\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\hat A_{t+1}\) and substituting \(\delta_t\) gives a recursion directly on \(\hat R_t\):

\[ \hat R_t = r_t + \gamma\Big[(1-\lambda)\,V_\psi(s_{t+1}) + \lambda\,\hat R_{t+1}\Big] \]

Read it as: one real reward, then a \(\lambda\)-mixture of "bootstrap now from the critic" and "keep going with the next \(\lambda\)-return." Run backward from the terminal state (\(\hat R_T = 0\)), it is the same \(O(T)\) pass that computes \(\hat A_t\). The two endpoints of \(\lambda\) say what kind of label this produces:

\(\lambda = 0\): the one-step TD target

The recursion keeps only the bootstrap branch: \(\hat R_t = r_t + \gamma V_\psi(s_{t+1})\). One real reward, then the critic's guess — the ordinary TD(0) target. Quietest label, but it inherits whatever error the critic currently has.

\(\lambda = 1\): the Monte Carlo return

The recursion never bootstraps: \(\hat R_t = r_t + \gamma \hat R_{t+1}\), which unrolls to \(\hat R_t = \sum_{l}\gamma^l r_{t+l} = G_t\). Equivalently, in \(\hat A_t = \sum_l \gamma^l \delta_{t+l}\) each \(+\gamma^{l+1}V_\psi(s_{t+l+1})\) cancels against the next term's \(-\gamma^{l+1}V_\psi(s_{t+l+1})\), telescoping to \(G_t - V_\psi(s_t)\). All real rewards, no critic — unbiased, but as noisy as any Monte Carlo return.

\(0 < \lambda < 1\): a geometric average of \(n\)-step returns

Define \(G_t^{(n)} = r_t + \gamma r_{t+1} + \dots + \gamma^{n-1} r_{t+n-1} + \gamma^n V_\psi(s_{t+n})\) — \(n\) real rewards, then bootstrap. Stopping the telescoping after \(n\) lines gives \(\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\gamma^l\delta_{t+l} = G_t^{(n)} - V_\psi(s_t)\), and regrouping the GAE sum by \(n\) (the weight identity \((1-\lambda)\sum_{n>l}\lambda^{n-1} = \lambda^l\)) yields

\[ \hat R_t = (1-\lambda)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda^{n-1}\, G_t^{(n)} . \]

The weights sum to \(1\): \(\hat R_t\) is a weighted average of every \(n\)-step return — mostly \(1\)-step, a little less \(2\)-step, and so on. This is Sutton's \(\lambda\)-return, hence the name. At \(\lambda = 0.95\) the effective horizon before bootstrapping dominates is about \(1/(1-\lambda) = 20\) steps.

A concrete pass. Three steps, \(\gamma = 1\), \(\lambda = 0.5\), rewards \((r_0, r_1, r_2) = (0, 0, 1)\), and the current critic says \(V_\psi(s_0, s_1, s_2) = (0.5,\; 0.9,\; 0.2)\). Backward:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat R_2 &= r_2 = 1.0 \\ \hat R_1 &= r_1 + 0.5\,V_\psi(s_2) + 0.5\,\hat R_2 = 0.5(0.2) + 0.5(1.0) = 0.6 \\ \hat R_0 &= r_0 + 0.5\,V_\psi(s_1) + 0.5\,\hat R_1 = 0.5(0.9) + 0.5(0.6) = 0.75 \end{aligned} \]

Stage 1 is done: the labels are \((0.75,\; 0.6,\; 1.0)\). Plain numbers — nothing has been trained yet.

Stage 2 — the regression

Now fit the critic to the labels with ordinary supervised learning:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{VF}}(\psi) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[ \big(V_\psi(s_t) - \hat R_t\big)^2 \right] \]

On the example: pull \(V_\psi(s_0)\) from \(0.5 \to 0.75\), \(V_\psi(s_1)\) from \(0.9 \to 0.6\), \(V_\psi(s_2)\) from \(0.2 \to 1.0\). The connection between the two formulas is a pipeline, not a derivation: the recursion builds the dataset, the loss fits the critic to it.

Why this learns \(V^\pi\) — two facts stack:

Squared error regresses to the conditional mean

For fixed labels, the minimiser of \(\mathbb{E}[(V_\psi(s_t) - \hat R_t)^2]\) is \(V_\psi(s_t) = \mathbb{E}[\hat R_t \mid s_t]\) — the same reason the sample mean minimises squared deviations. So the loss drives the critic toward the average label at each state.

The labels average to \(V^\pi\) at the fixed point

\(\hat R_t\) is a \(\lambda\)-mix of \(n\)-step returns, each of which is "real rewards for \(n\) steps, then the critic's guess." If the critic were already correct, \(\mathbb{E}[G_t^{(n)} \mid s_t] = V^\pi(s_t)\) for every \(n\), so \(\mathbb{E}[\hat R_t \mid s_t] = V^\pi(s_t)\): a correct critic generates labels that tell it to stay put — \(V^\pi\) is a fixed point. If the critic is wrong, the labels are still anchored by real rewards, so they point back toward \(V^\pi\), and repeating build-labels \(\to\) regress converges toward it. This iterate-to-a-fixed-point loop is exactly TD learning wearing a regression costume.

\(V_\psi\) appears on both sides — inside the labels and as the thing being trained — which looks like the critic grading its own homework. The resolution: at label-building time the critic values are frozen numbers (.detach() in code), so each update is honest supervised learning against fixed targets, and the self-reference only enters across iterations as the fixed-point loop above — the intended mechanism, not a bug. Without the detach, gradient descent could also shrink the loss by bending the labels toward the predictions, and the regression argument collapses.

GAE requires a critic \(V_\psi\) trained alongside the policy \(\pi_\theta\) — the actor–critic structure that PPO builds on. In LLM RL this is expensive — a second model as large as the policy. GRPO sidesteps it by replacing the learned critic with a group-sampling baseline; see the GRPO page.

4. From Fresh Rollouts to Reusing a Batch

The vanilla estimator samples from the policy being differentiated. After an update, the old batch is no longer fresh on-policy data. Lecture slides 20–25 separate exact change of measure from a practical local surrogate.

Exact trajectory importance sampling

For behavior policy \(\mu\) and target \(\pi_\theta\), with the same initial distribution and environment dynamics,

\[ W_\theta(\tau)=\frac{P_{\pi_\theta}(\tau)}{P_\mu(\tau)} =\prod_t\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t\mid s_t)}, \qquad \nabla J_\gamma(\theta) =\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\mu}\!\left[ W_\theta(\tau)\,R_\gamma(\tau)\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\right]. \]

Behavior must cover every trajectory with positive target probability, and the expectation must exist. Under those conditions this is exact even for distant policies; distance and horizon can make its finite-sample variance enormous. Mathematical validity and practical estimator quality are different questions.

Action-only ratios define a local surrogate

An action ratio corrects actions at a fixed state, not the probability of visiting that state. A state–action density ratio also contains \(d_{\pi_\theta}(s)/d_\mu(s)\), usually approximated as 1 in practical reuse. Old-policy returns or advantages additionally describe old-policy continuations. Replacing the trajectory product by one action ratio per term therefore does not generally recover the new policy's gradient.

PPO's surrogate keeps old-policy state samples and frozen advantages. Its gradient agrees locally at the collection policy when the advantage estimates and state/time weights match the intended objective; away from it, it is a surrogate, not exact trajectory IS. A KL-constrained update limits distribution change on the measured states. PPO clipping instead removes some incentives for large changes and supplies no hard KL or ratio bound. See exact sequence products versus token-local correction for the systems extension.

5. Summary

PG theorem (undiscounted)

\(\mathbb{E}[\sum_t R(\tau)\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)]\)

Reward-to-go

For \(J_\gamma\), weight each score by \(\gamma^tG_t\); past rewards cancel in expectation

Advantage

\(\hat A_t=G_t-V^\pi(s_t)\); its conditional mean is \(A^\pi(s_t,a_t)\)

GAE

\(\hat A_t = \sum_l(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}\); computed backward in \(O(T)\)

Policy update

\(\mathbb{E}[\sum_t\gamma^t\hat A_t\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)]\) for \(J_\gamma\), with a valid estimator

Critic loss

\(\mathbb{E}_t[(V_\psi(s_t) - \hat R_t)^2]\) where \(\hat R_t = \hat A_t + V_\psi(s_t)\)

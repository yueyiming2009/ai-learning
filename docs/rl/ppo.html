PPO — Proximal Policy Optimization

Reuse a batch through a local policy-gradient surrogate, estimate advantages with a critic, and clip incentives for large updates — without mistaking clipping for a hard trust-region guarantee.

1. The Problem: One Step, One Batch

The policy gradient \(\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}[\sum_t \hat A_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)]\) is on-policy: the expectation is taken under the very policy being differentiated. Two painful consequences follow.

  • Uncorrected reuse changes the estimator. After a gradient step, the old batch is off-policy. Vanilla REINFORCE recollects to preserve its on-policy expectation; reuse instead needs correction or an explicitly approximate surrogate. For LLM RL, discarding expensive rollouts after one step is costly.
  • One large step can be fatal. Nothing in the raw gradient bounds the size of the update. A single step that moves the policy too far can collapse it into a degenerate distribution from which it never recovers — and unlike supervised learning, the next batch is collected by that broken policy, so the damage compounds.

The motivation is to take controlled steps while reusing data. TRPO formulates an average-KL-constrained update, approximately solved with second-order methods. PPO-Clip changes the first-order objective to discourage excessive movement; it does not impose that constraint. Step size, epoch count, and KL monitoring remain important.

2. Reusing Rollouts: the Importance Ratio

For a batch collected by \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), the action importance ratio reweights the conditional action distribution at each sampled state:

\[ \rho_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)} \]
In practice this ratio is formed in log space — \(\rho_t = \exp\!\big(\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\big)\) — where each log-prob is a log_softmax gather over the policy logits. See computing log-probabilities from logits.

The surrogate objective multiplies this ratio by the frozen old-policy advantages \(\hat A_t\):

\[ \mathcal{L}^{\text{IS}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\big[\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t\big] \]

At \(\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}\), its expected gradient matches the policy gradient when advantages are exact or conditionally unbiased and state/time weighting matches the objective. GAE with an imperfect critic and practical weighting can add approximation error. Away from the collection policy, sampled states and continuation values still belong to the old policy: the action ratio does not correct either, so this is a local surrogate, not exact new-policy trajectory reweighting.

Exact trajectory IS uses a product of ratios and remains valid under support and integrability assumptions even when policies are far apart; high variance may make it unusable in practice. That variance issue is separate from omitting state-distribution correction and retaining old-policy advantages. See sequence products versus token-local correction for the same distinction in RL systems.

3. The Clipped Surrogate Objective

PPO removes a sample's incentive to move its ratio farther in the advantage-improving direction once it crosses a threshold. Take the minimum of the raw surrogate and a version with the ratio clipped to \([1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon]\):

PPO clipped surrogate
\[ \mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[ \min\!\Big( \rho_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t,\;\; \mathrm{clip}\big(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat A_t \Big) \right] \]

The \(\min\) makes the objective a pessimistic (lower) bound on the unclipped surrogate. Read it by the sign of the advantage:

  • \(\hat A_t > 0\) (a better-than-average action). Raising its probability grows this sample's objective until \(\rho_t=1+\epsilon\), then the term flattens. Its own policy-loss gradient is zero beyond that point, but other samples, shared parameters, or auxiliary losses can still change its probability.
  • \(\hat A_t < 0\) (a worse-than-average action). We want to lower its probability, so \(\rho_t\) falls. Below \(\rho_t = 1-\epsilon\) the objective flattens — same brake on the downside. But note the asymmetry: if \(\rho_t\) instead rises for a bad action, the unclipped term keeps getting worse and the \(\min\) lets it, so an action that became too probable is always pulled back, even from outside the band.
\(\hat A_t > 0\) (good action) \(\hat A_t < 0\) (bad action)

The plateaus bound a sample's objective incentive, not the optimizer's step. With \(\epsilon = 0.2\) the band is \([0.8, 1.2]\) (vertical gridlines). For \(\hat A_t > 0\) the objective plateaus once \(\rho_t > 1.2\) — zero gradient, no reward for over-shooting. For \(\hat A_t < 0\) it plateaus once \(\rho_t < 0.8\), but keeps falling for \(\rho_t > 1\): a bad action that grew more probable is never let off the hook by its own loss term. Other terms can still move the policy through a plateau.

The min and clamp are simple to differentiate, and a typical \(\epsilon\) is \(0.2\). Neither bounds every realized ratio nor guarantees a maximum KL divergence or monotonic reward improvement. A finite optimizer step can overshoot a plateau, and shared parameters couple different samples.

For the complete derivative, see PPO & GRPO — Step-by-Step Backward Pass: resolve every clipping branch, differentiate the ratio and optional KL term, then carry the gradient through log-softmax to logits and model weights.

Spinning Up explicitly warns that PPO-Clip can still move too far and adds early stopping based on measured mean KL. That is an additional empirical safeguard, not an exact projection into a trust region. Distinguish the clip parameter, the KL-monitoring threshold, and an explicit KL penalty or constraint.

Outer ratio clipping: a second safeguard, also for GRPO

The clipped surrogate still has active tails. For \(A<0\) and \(\rho>1+\epsilon\), the minimized loss selects the raw branch \(-A\rho\), not the inner plateau. Writing \(\ell=\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\), with \(A\) and \(\ell_{\rm old}\) frozen, gives \(\partial\rho/\partial\ell=\rho\) and \(\partial L/\partial\ell=-A\rho\): an extreme ratio can amplify the gradient. For \(A>0\) and a small ratio, the raw branch also remains active, although its log-probability gradient tends to zero as \(\rho\to0\).

MAI-Thinking-1's GRPO recipe adds a hard outer ratio clamp. Its inner bounds are \(b_-=1-\epsilon\) and \(b_+=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}+k\); the entropy controller below adjusts \(k\). During a backward pass, both bounds and the outer bounds are frozen. For an outer interval enclosing the inner one, \(0\leq r_{\min}\leq b_-<1<b_+\leq r_{\max}\), their stated two-level rule can be written together as:

Inner incentive clip plus outer tail clamp
\[ \bar\rho=\operatorname{clip}(\rho,r_{\min},r_{\max}),\qquad L_{\rm outer}=-\min\!\left(A\bar\rho,\; A\operatorname{clip}(\bar\rho,b_-,b_+)\right). \] \[ \frac{\partial L_{\rm outer}}{\partial\ell} =-A\rho\, \mathbf1\{r_{\min}<\rho<r_{\max}\}\, \mathbf1\{(A>0\land\rho<b_+)\lor(A<0\land\rho>b_-)\}. \]

The derivative is stated away from the clipping kinks; \(A=0\) gives zero. Because the outer interval encloses the inner interval, clipping \(\bar\rho\) to the inner bounds is the same as clipping the original \(\rho\) to them. Outside the outer interval the policy-loss contribution is constant, so its derivative is zero, not \(-A\bar\rho\). This is a differentiable clamp, not a detached importance weight as in CISPO. Carry this scalar through log-softmax as in the backward derivation.

4. Actor–Critic: Where \(\hat A_t\) Comes From

A typical PPO implementation is actor–critic. The actor is the policy \(\pi_\theta\); the critic is a learned value function \(V_\psi(s)\) — usually a scalar head on the same network — whose job is to supply a baseline for lower-variance advantages. The clipped policy loss itself does not require a critic.

In this implementation, the advantages plugged into \(\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}\) are computed by GAE from the critic's value estimates — a single backward pass over the TD errors \(\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V_\psi(s_{t+1}) - V_\psi(s_t)\):

\[ \hat A_t = \sum_{l\ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^l\,\delta_{t+l} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\hat A_{t+1} \]
The reward reaches the policy through two paths, not one. It is tempting to read this as a clean chain — reward trains the critic, critic feeds the policy — but the reward \(r_t\) sits directly inside every \(\delta_t\). So it flows into \(\hat A_t\) both directly (the \(r_t\) terms) and indirectly (through \(V_\psi\), which was itself learned from reward). \(\lambda\) is the dial between them: \(\lambda\to 1\) leans on the raw rewards (Monte-Carlo return), \(\lambda\to 0\) leans on the critic's bootstrap (see Policy Gradient §3). And the critic bootstrapping off its own \(V_\psi(s_{t+1})\) is not circular: the real reward is injected at every step, so the estimates are continually pulled toward reality — the same reason TD learning converges.

The same pass gives the critic's own regression target, the TD(\(\lambda\)) return \(\hat R_t = \hat A_t + V_\psi(s_t)\), so actor and critic train from one rollout:

Value (critic) loss
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{VF}}(\psi) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[\big(V_\psi(s_t) - \hat R_t\big)^2\right] \]
A better critic means lower-variance advantages, which means a steadier clip — but it doubles the model footprint, since \(V_\psi\) is typically as large as the policy. That cost is exactly what GRPO removes by replacing the critic with a group-sampling baseline, dropping the value model entirely.

5. The Full PPO Objective

The complete per-step loss combines three terms: maximize the clipped surrogate, minimize the value error, and add an entropy bonus \(S\) that keeps the policy from collapsing too early:

PPO loss (written for minimization)
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}} = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[ -\,\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}_t \;+\; c_1\, \big(V_\psi(s_t) - \hat R_t\big)^2 \;-\; c_2\, S\big[\pi_\theta\big](s_t) \right] \]

The signs read as: maximize the clipped surrogate and the entropy (hence the minus signs in a loss being minimized), minimize the value error. The coefficients \(c_1, c_2\) balance the three.

6. Policy Entropy

The entropy of a policy at state \(s\) is the expected surprise of its action distribution:

\[ H\big[\pi_\theta\big](s) = -\sum_a \pi_\theta(a \mid s)\,\log\pi_\theta(a \mid s) \]

High entropy means the policy spreads probability mass broadly — many actions look roughly equally likely. Low entropy means it concentrates mass on a few actions — it is confident, or possibly stuck.

Why add it as a bonus. Policy gradient is on-policy — it only learns from actions it actually samples. If the policy gets confident too early it stops sampling the alternatives, so it stops receiving any gradient about them, so it can never discover that one was better: a positive-feedback trap that ends in a collapsed, possibly suboptimal policy. Without an exploration counter-force, PPO can enter this trap after finding a high-reward action. The entropy bonus is the counter-force that keeps the policy exploratory long enough to evaluate its options.

What the bonus does to the gradient

Differentiating the entropy makes its effect precise. From \(\nabla_\theta H = -\sum_a \nabla_\theta\pi_\theta\,(\log\pi_\theta + 1)\), the \(+1\) term drops because the score function sums to zero, \(\sum_a \nabla_\theta\pi_\theta = \nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta = \nabla_\theta 1 = 0\):

\[ \nabla_\theta H[\pi_\theta](s) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\big] \]

The weight \(-\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) is large for rare actions and near zero for likely ones, so this gradient lifts the log-probability of under-represented actions more than dominant ones — a flattening force, the exact opponent of the reward gradient that sharpens the distribution onto whatever currently scores well. This is the precise sense in which the bonus acts as a regularizer in log-probability space.

Entropy is a KL leash to the uniform policy

Writing \(u\) for the uniform distribution over the \(|\mathcal{A}|\) actions, the entropy is a constant minus a KL divergence:

Entropy as KL to uniform
\[ H[\pi] = \log|\mathcal{A}| - \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi \,\|\, u\big) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \max_\theta\, H[\pi_\theta] \;\equiv\; \min_\theta\, \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta \,\|\, u\big) \]

Since \(\log|\mathcal{A}|\) has no gradient, the entropy bonus's gradient is \(-c_2\,\nabla_\theta\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|u)\): an entropy bonus is exactly a KL penalty pulling the policy toward uniform. That makes it the structural twin of RLHF's reference-KL \(-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) — the same leash, a different anchor (uniform vs. the SFT model). It is also the policy-gradient counterpart of DQN's \(\epsilon\)-greedy: both inject exploration, but \(\epsilon\)-greedy bolts external randomness onto a greedy policy, while the entropy bonus shapes the policy's own distribution. Pushed to its limit — augmenting the reward itself with \(\alpha H\) at every step — this becomes maximum-entropy RL ("act as randomly as possible while still maximizing reward"), the principle behind soft actor-critic.

\(H[\pi]\) — entropy (the bonus) \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,u)\) — equivalent penalty \(H + \mathrm{KL} = \log|\mathcal{A}|\)

The bonus and the penalty are the same curve, flipped. For a two-action policy \(\pi = (p,\,1-p)\), entropy (teal) and KL-to-uniform (amber) are mirror images that sum to the constant \(\log|\mathcal{A}| = \log 2 \approx 0.69\) (dashed). Maximizing the entropy bonus is minimizing KL to uniform. Both are extremal at \(p = 0.5\) (maximally uncertain — entropy peaks, KL vanishes) and at the deterministic corners \(p \in \{0, 1\}\) (collapsed — entropy vanishes, KL peaks). The reward gradient is always trying to drag the policy toward a corner; \(c_2\) sets how hard the bonus pulls back toward the middle.

Setting \(c_2\) in practice

  • Classic PPO (Atari, control): \(c_2 \approx 0.01\) is standard — with no reference policy, the entropy bonus is the main thing preventing premature collapse.
  • RLHF with a KL leash: \(c_2\) is often set to \(0\). Reference-KL anchors at \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), rather than uniform over a large vocabulary. It can resist drift, but does not enforce a target entropy or guarantee against collapse.
  • Verifiable-reward GRPO / "RL-zero" reasoning: with little or no KL leash, entropy management is load-bearing again — too small and the policy collapses into a single template that games the reward. This is the regime the entropy-collapse diagnostics are built for.
  • Annealing versus feedback: decaying \(c_2\) on a preset schedule is open-loop. Adaptive entropy control changes a control variable in response to measured entropy, as below; the variable need not be \(c_2\).

Computing it for an LLM. The action space is the full vocabulary \(|\mathcal{V}|\) — often 32k–128k tokens — so the exact sum is expensive. Two common approximations:

  • Sample estimate — use only the sampled token \(a_t\): \(H[\pi] \approx -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \log\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\). Unbiased at a fixed state when \(a_t\) is sampled from that same policy, but noisy. Old-policy or temperature/top-p-modified samples do not automatically estimate the current actor's entropy.
  • Top-k partial sum — sum \(-p_a\log p_a\) over only the top-k actions (e.g., \(k=50\)). It drops the tail's entropy; renormalizing instead measures a different distribution. Use the same definition for both the measurement and its target.

Adaptive entropy control: close the feedback loop

Shared with GRPO, not a PPO-only feature. Group-relative advantages can feed the same clipped policy loss. The feedback loop is: update the actor, measure entropy \(\hat H_j\), compare it with a target \(H^\star\), then adjust a control variable for the next update. A target can be fixed while the controller adapts; “adaptive” does not necessarily mean the target itself changes.

Coefficient tuning from an entropy constraint. SAC §5 maximizes return subject to a minimum average entropy, \(H(\theta)\geq H^\star\). Its Lagrangian is \(J(\theta)+\alpha(H(\theta)-H^\star)\), with \(\alpha\geq0\): maximize over the policy, minimize over the multiplier. Holding the current policy fixed, the multiplier gradient is \(H(\theta)-H^\star\), so projected gradient descent gives:

Automatic entropy coefficient — negative feedback
\[ D(\alpha)=\alpha\,\operatorname{sg}[\hat H_j-H^\star],\qquad \alpha_{j+1}=\max\!\left(0,\alpha_j+\eta_\alpha(H^\star-\hat H_j)\right). \]

Below target, increase \(\alpha\) to strengthen the entropy bonus; above target, decrease it. Here sg means stop-gradient: controller measurements do not backpropagate into the actor. The actor's own entropy term still needs a correct differentiable entropy or entropy-gradient estimator. Setting \(c_2=\alpha_j\) adapts a PPO/GRPO bonus with this principle; it does not turn that algorithm into SAC or inherit SAC's convergence results. This is a lower-bound constraint, not exact per-state setpoint tracking: \(\alpha\) cannot become negative to force excess entropy down.

MAI: entropy selects the inner upper bound

The MAI report, Eqs. (7)–(8) estimates entropy on old-policy prefixes using the raw action ratio, then updates a bounded, sign-error controller after each training step:

\[ \hat H_j=\frac{1}{|\mathcal T|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T} \rho_t[-\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)],\qquad k_{j+1}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left( k_j+\delta\,\operatorname{sign}(H^\star-\hat H_j),0,k_{\max}\right). \]

It initializes \(k=0\), making \(1-\epsilon\) and \((1-\epsilon)^{-1}\) multiplicative inverses: symmetry in log-ratio space. Entropy below target raises \(k\); entropy above target lowers it. A wider upper bound can let rewarded, initially rare alternatives gain more probability before clipping, but the resulting entropy response depends on the advantages and shared parameters, not a guaranteed monotonic law. The separate outer cap limits extreme ratios; it is not the variable this controller tunes.

The importance-weighted measurement corrects the conditional action distribution when the old policy has the required support; it does not correct old prefixes into the new policy's state distribution. Truncated sampling can violate support, large weights raise variance, and clipping those weights changes the estimate. Track the policy snapshot, sampling distribution, response mask, token reduction, and units alongside any entropy target.

Choosing and diagnosing a controller

  • Choose a meaningful target. For categorical actions, \(0\leq H\leq\log V\); continuous-action differential entropy has different units and can be negative. Do not transfer SAC's continuous-control defaults to LLM vocabulary entropy. Initial entropy, as used by REPO/ADAPO, is a baseline, not a proven optimum.
  • Keep the measurement comparable. Task mix, response length, masks, temperature, and policy version can change an average without a controller failure. Choose token-mean versus sequence-mean deliberately and pair it with the same target definition.
  • Tune cadence and gain. Noisy or delayed feedback can oscillate. If smoothing with \(\bar H_j=(1-\lambda)\bar H_{j-1}+\lambda\hat H_j\), initialize from the first measurement and remember that smaller \(\lambda\) adds lag. Smoothing is an engineering option, not part of the reported MAI sign rule above.
  • Log the actuator and outcomes. Record measured entropy, target/error, coefficient or upper bound, time at controller limits, outer-clipped fractions, ratio tails, and gradient norms. Pair them with reward, held-out success, response length, and diversity/pass@k. Persistent error at a bound calls for checking gain, feasibility, and measurements—not blindly raising the target. See controller diagnostics.

What the value tells you. For a uniform distribution over a vocabulary of size \(V\), the maximum entropy is \(\log V\). In practice:

  • Gradually decreasing — normal: the policy becomes more confident as it learns which actions earn reward.
  • Rapid collapse to near zero — investigate lost diversity, degenerate outputs, and reward hacking; entropy alone does not identify the cause.
  • Rising late in training — inspect task mix, sampling settings, controller response, and outcome quality before attributing it to forgetting or gradient noise.

For the diagnostic use of entropy during async RL training — the entropy-collapse amplification loop and its interaction with TIM — see Monitoring & Diagnosis for Async RL.

7. The PPO Update Loop

One iteration collects a batch, then takes several surrogate-gradient steps while monitoring policy change:

  1. Collect — run \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) in the environment to gather a batch of trajectories (states, actions, rewards).
  2. Estimate — forward the critic \(V_\psi\) over the batch and compute advantages \(\hat A_t\) by GAE, plus the value targets \(\hat R_t\). Freeze these.
  3. Optimize — take up to \(K\) epochs (e.g. \(3\text{–}4\)) of minibatch SGD on \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}\). Track ratio and KL diagnostics; implementations may stop policy updates early when a configured KL threshold is exceeded. Clipping alone does not cap the drift.
  4. Refresh — set \(\theta_{\text{old}} \leftarrow \theta\) and repeat.
Reuse amortizes expensive collection over several updates, but does not guarantee stability. At the initial collection parameters the ratios are 1, so clipping is inactive for the first policy gradient evaluation. One epoch can still contain many minibatch updates, allowing clipping to activate later in that same epoch.
This is the generic algorithm. To turn it into RLHF, the environment becomes the language model generating tokens, the reward comes from a learned reward model plus a per-token KL penalty against a reference policy, and the "Collect" step is a dedicated inference engine. The optimizer in the middle stays exactly this.

8. Summary

Probability ratio

\(\rho_t = \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\)

Clipped surrogate

\(\mathbb{E}[\min(\rho_t\hat A_t,\,\mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,1\pm\epsilon)\hat A_t)]\)

Advantage (GAE)

\(\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\,\hat A_{t+1}\)

Critic loss

\(\mathbb{E}_t[(V_\psi(s_t) - \hat R_t)^2]\)

Full loss

\(-\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}} + c_1\mathcal{L}_{\text{VF}} - c_2 S\)

Update loop

Collect → estimate \(\hat A_t\) → \(K\) epochs of clipped SGD → refresh \(\theta_{\text{old}}\)

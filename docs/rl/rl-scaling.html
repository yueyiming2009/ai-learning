RL Scaling Laws

What to do with more compute: interpret the gains, choose what to scale, and know when not to extrapolate.

Primary sources checked November 22, 2026 · A decision guide, not a universal law

You have a trained policy and another compute budget. Should you run more updates, sample more attempts, or broaden the tasks? An RL scaling law is useful when it helps choose between those options, not merely because a training curve looks smooth.

This page concerns LLM reinforcement-learning post-training. Pretraining scaling allocates model size and data; test-time scaling spends compute answering a query. Here the weights change through reward-driven learning. Those budgets interact, but their fitted laws are not interchangeable.

1. Read the curve: gains now are not gains forever

First identify the axes. Training reward measures what the optimizer is rewarded for; held-out performance measures whether the improvement transfers. “Log-linear” score growth means that equal compute multiplications, such as successive doublings, give approximately equal score additions. It does not mean each additional FLOP buys the same improvement.

Example: Inkling 2 Small. Its launch post reports roughly log-linear growth in reward and selected evaluations versus cumulative learner-plus-sampler FLOPs. That is evidence about one production run, not a universal exponent or an unlimited growth guarantee. Keep the full recipe on the Inkling page.

ScaleRL fits a saturating curve to held-out pass rates for a fixed model and training dataset:

\[ R(C)=R_0+\frac{A-R_0}{1+(C_{\mathrm{mid}}/C)^B}. \]

\(R_0\) is starting performance, \(A\) the fitted ceiling, and \(C_{\mathrm{mid}}\) the compute needed to reach half the modeled gain; \(B\) controls steepness on a log-compute axis. The middle can look log-linear before the curve flattens. A better recipe might reach the same ceiling sooner, or move the ceiling itself. A fast early gain does not tell you which happened.

At 16 times compute both illustrative scores are 50%. At 256 times compute the straight extension predicts about 92%, while the saturating curve predicts about 76%.

These are synthetic curves, not measurements or a fit to Inkling: \(R_0=20\%\), \(A=80\%\), \(C_{\mathrm{mid}}=16\), \(B=1\); the straight line is tangent at the midpoint.

Do not force every result into this shape. A power law in failure rate uses different axes from a straight line in raw accuracy versus log-compute. Both are candidate descriptions over a measured range, not interchangeable formulas. For bounded scores, extrapolating a straight line forever eventually predicts impossible values.

2. Spend the next budget on the limiting resource

Compare alternatives at equal cost, not equal optimizer steps. One step can contain many more prompts, attempts, or generated tokens than another. IsoCompute Playbook separates prompts per batch, rollouts per prompt, and update count. Their product counts sampled rollouts; converting it to FLOPs requires accounting for model and sequence costs.

IsoCompute finds that optimal rollouts per prompt generally increase with budget, then saturate; the actual setting depends on the base model and problem difficulty. Its single-turn, binary-reward experiments do not establish a universal group size for long-horizon agents. Increasing prompts per batch also differs from expanding the unique training-task pool.

The agentic study How Data Scales in Agentic RL finds stronger scaling from environment diversity, especially new repositories or apps, than from repeated trajectories in its tested settings. Synthesis costs and reward quality still determine the best allocation: a larger fitted exponent alone does not make an option cheaper. See task generation for the mechanisms.

3. Check the prediction before buying the next run

  1. Declare the budget. Count learner, sampler, and model-based grader compute. Report environment costs and wall time separately. Faster communication can improve throughput without improving learning per FLOP; rollout count and GPU-hours are not interchangeable across systems. If comparing different base models, state whether their pretraining cost is excluded.
  2. Hold evaluation conditions fixed. Keep tasks, harness, grader, attempt count, and reasoning budget comparable. A later checkpoint that thinks longer may consume more inference compute even under the same token limit; report actual usage or compare at matched budgets. Otherwise “better training” may partly mean more expensive evaluation.
  3. Require healthy learning. Track held-out scores alongside reward. If reward rises while useful performance stalls, investigate proxy overoptimization. If the run destabilizes, inspect numerical mismatch and policy lag before interpreting the collapse as a scaling limit.
  4. Validate beyond the fit. Fit smaller budgets, predict a reserved larger run, and quantify uncertainty across tasks and seeds. Exclude transient warmup only with a declared rule. A ceiling estimated before visible curvature can be poorly identified; selecting the best of many noisy checkpoints also makes extrapolation optimistic.

The useful output is a budget decision with an evidence boundary: “For this policy, task mix, and evaluator, this allocation is predicted to reach this score at this cost.” When the base model, reward, curriculum, or implementation changes, validate the relationship again. No single curve licenses unlimited RL, guarantees transfer, or replaces a held-out comparison.

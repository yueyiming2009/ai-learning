Reinforcement Learning · Sources, treatments & implementation

Training–Inference Mismatch

Follow a sampled token from rollout to gradient. Understand why mismatch creates bias, choose the right treatment, and implement the loss and numerical contracts correctly.

Read this as one training loop, not a list of algorithms. Origins → one token's update → expected bias → treatments → loss and engine implementation → diagnosis. The prerequisites are log-probabilities and signed weighted likelihood. The running example has four tokens at one fixed prefix; the binary plots are explicitly labeled simplifications. Async RL covers scheduling, PPO/GRPO Backward the full training derivative, and Monitoring the operational dashboards.

1. One training loop, several sources of mismatch

Sampler generates tokens at their actual prefixes
  → a grader assigns rewards; the training recipe computes advantages
  → trainer recomputes token log-probabilities with gradients
  → optimizer updates the trainer; its weights later refresh the sampler

Which policy generated the token, and which one are we training?

At a fixed prefix \(h\), call the actual sampling distribution \(q(v\mid h)\) and the current trainer distribution \(p_\theta(v\mid h)\). A policy here is a probability distribution over the next token, not simply a checkpoint name. The sampler determines which tokens appear; the trainer determines which log-probabilities we differentiate. Both must describe the same vocabulary, prefix, and token.

For a saved sample, its behavior probability \(q(y\mid h)\) is fixed historical evidence. The trainer's \(p_\theta(y\mid h)\) changes with optimization. Fresh weights do not ensure \(p=q\): numerical execution and sampling transformations can differ even at the same checkpoint. Conversely, perfectly matched kernels do not make an old checkpoint current.

Separate the cause before choosing a correction.

Vocabulary note. Here “numerical TIM” means the same-checkpoint engine discrepancy. The score-centering paper uses TIM more broadly, including staleness. They are related sources of off-policy data, but need different operational fixes. Synchronizing weights before fresh sampling removes initial checkpoint lag; it does not align numerics or prevent the data from becoming stale during later updates.

Deterministic is not the same as matched. Two deterministic kernels can use different reduction trees and disagree. A kernel can also change its reduction grouping with batch shape without any race condition. In finite precision, \((a+b)+c\) can differ from \(a+(b+c)\); matching dtypes alone does not fix that. Quantization changes represented values, and a routing flip can change the computation itself. The batch-invariance explanation and engine walkthrough make these distinctions concrete.

Context fidelity is a prerequisite, not an IS problem. If training reconstructs a different prefix, the numerator and denominator are no longer probabilities of the same event. Preserve token fidelity before choosing any correction. With that contract in place, follow one sample into its gradient.

2. Turn one sampled token into an update

Use this four-token distribution throughout the main derivations. These are illustrative probabilities, not measured model outputs. Suppose token C was sampled:

Token:                 A      B      C      D
Sampler q:            0.60   0.25   0.10   0.05
Current trainer p:    0.50   0.30   0.15   0.05

Saved token: C
Recorded log q(C) = log(0.10)
Trainer  log p(C) = log(0.15)
Ratio r = p(C) / q(C) = 1.5

The ratio says the current trainer assigns C 1.5 times the sampler's probability. It does not say whether the difference came from numerics, checkpoint age, or optimizer movement. Nor is it an update by itself. We still need a direction and a signed strength.

Direction: differentiate the trainer's log-probability

Write \(s_y=\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y\mid h)\), the score vector. It is not a confidence score or a reward. For vocabulary logits \(z\):

\[ \log p_y=z_y-\log\sum_v e^{z_v}, \qquad \nabla_z\log p_y=e_y-p, \qquad s_y=J_{z,\theta}^{\mathsf T}(e_y-p). \]

The last equality is backpropagation through the logits. For our C sample, \(e_{\mathrm C}-p=[-0.50,-0.30,0.85,-0.05]\). This is a logit-space gradient, not a vector of probability increments.

Strength: signed advantage times the chosen correction. \[ g_t=A_t\,w_t\,\underbrace{\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(y_t\mid h_t)}_{s_{y_t}:\ \text{score vector}}. \]

The score is a vector over model parameters, not a reward or confidence score. Positive \(A_t\) reinforces the sampled behavior; negative \(A_t\) reverses that push. A nonnegative \(w_t\) rescales or removes it. For plain gradient ascent, \(\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\,\operatorname{aggregate}_t g_t\). A minimized loss has the opposite gradient sign.

Here \(A_t\) is a signed advantage, not the name of token A. Uncorrected policy gradient uses \(w=1\). Ordinary importance sampling uses \(w=p_y/q_y\). For our C sample, choose advantage \(+2\) and IS weight \(1.5\):

Ascent coefficient = 2 * 1.5 = 3
Logit-space contribution = 3 * [-0.50, -0.30, 0.85, -0.05]
                        = [-1.50, -0.90, 2.55, -0.15]

Learning rate, aggregation, backpropagation into shared parameters, and the optimizer still follow. Other samples and auxiliary losses can oppose this contribution. A positive raw reward need not produce a positive advantage after centering.

In group-relative training, an entire response often shares one advantage. That is a credit-assignment approximation, not evidence that every token caused the reward. The backward walkthrough follows the full chain.

# Schematic minimized loss for a detached importance weight:
loss = -advantage * stop_gradient(weight) * log_p

# Gradient descent follows +advantage * weight * gradient(log_p).
# A detached weight supplies a coefficient, not a second gradient path.

We now have a sample-level update. Before comparing different weights or clipping rules, ask what happens when we average those updates over the tokens the sampler actually generates. That is where mismatch creates a systematic error.

3. Average the updates: where bias and drift enter

A batch averages gradients for tokens drawn from \(q\), even though each score is computed through \(p\). For this analysis, write \(R\) for the detached signed training weight: it can be a return or an advantage. The uncorrected expected update at a prefix is \(\mathbb E_q[R s_y]\), not automatically the desired on-policy gradient.

Why the score averages to zero on-policy

Hold the prefix fixed and differentiate a normalized, differentiable policy with fixed support, such as softmax. For every parameter, the probability-weighted scores cancel because the total probability is always one:

\[ \mathbb E_p[s_y] =\sum_vp_v\nabla_\theta\log p_v =\sum_vp_v\frac{\nabla_\theta p_v}{p_v} =\nabla_\theta\sum_vp_v =\nabla_\theta1=0. \]

The expected score is zero; individual scores are not. Also, \(\mathbb E_p[R s_y]=R\mathbb E_p[s_y]=0\) only when \(R\) is constant across outcomes at this prefix. A reward that depends on the token or its continuation cannot generally be taken outside the expectation. “Zero in expectation” does not imply zero in every finite batch.

Why the real average uses the sampler

A sample mean \(N^{-1}\sum_i\nabla_\theta\log p(y_i)\), with \(y_i\sim q\), estimates \(\mathbb E_q[s_y]\). The expectation's subscript names the source of randomness, not the distribution written inside the logarithm. Its mean can be nonzero:

\[ \bar s=\mathbb E_q[s_y] =\sum_vq_vs_v =\sum_v(q_v-p_v)s_v. \]

Use the running four-token example and look at one component, A's logit. The scores are \(s^{(\mathrm A)}=[0.5,-0.5,-0.5,-0.5]\):

Average with trainer frequencies:
  0.50*(0.5) + 0.30*(-0.5) + 0.15*(-0.5) + 0.05*(-0.5) = 0

Average with actual sampler frequencies:
  0.60*(0.5) + 0.25*(-0.5) + 0.10*(-0.5) + 0.05*(-0.5) = 0.10

If all outcomes receive raw reward \(+1\), there is no reward preference to learn, yet the uncorrected update pushes A's logit upward. For independent vocabulary logits, the entire mean-score vector is \(q-p=[0.10,-0.05,-0.05,0]\); backpropagation maps it into model parameters.

Separate average-reward drift from reward-dependent signal

Covariance measures how deviations from the averages align. For scalar reward \(R\) and vector score \(s\), it is a vector computed coordinate by coordinate. Write \(\mu_R=\mathbb E_q[R]\) and \(\bar s=\mathbb E_q[s]\). Expanding gives:

\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Cov}_q(R,s) &=\mathbb E_q[(R-\mu_R)(s-\bar s)]\\ &=\mathbb E_q[Rs]-\mu_R\bar s. \end{aligned} \]

Rearrange this exact identity to obtain the paper's decomposition:

\[ \mathbb E_q[R\,s_v] =\underbrace{\mathbb E_q[R]\,\bar s}_{\text{drift}} +\underbrace{\operatorname{Cov}_q(R,s_v)}_{\text{reward-dependent signal}}. \]

Drift depends on the average reward, not its pairing with particular scores. Covariance preserves that pairing: above-average rewards aligned with above-average score components contribute positively. It is not a claim of causation, nor a claim that the remaining signal equals the on-policy gradient.

Keep the same probabilities but now reward only token A: \(R=[1,0,0,0]\). For A's logit:

Mean reward = 0.60; mean score = 0.10
Full update = 0.60 * 1 * 0.50 = 0.30
Drift       = 0.60 * 0.10     = 0.06
Covariance  = 0.30 - 0.06     = 0.24

Centered-deviation check:
  A:      0.60 * (1 - 0.60) * ( 0.50 - 0.10) = 0.096
  B,C,D:  0.40 * (0 - 0.60) * (-0.50 - 0.10) = 0.144
  Total covariance = 0.240

Add one to every reward: the covariance stays \(0.24\), but drift becomes \(1.60(0.10)=0.16\), and the update becomes \(0.40\). Off-policy, even changing the reward's zero point changes the uncorrected expected update. On-policy the extra term vanishes because \(\bar s=0\).

Why can drift accumulate? With positive mean reward, \(\bar s\) points in the direction of fitting \(p\) to samples from \(q\), like distillation. A fixed teacher provides a stationary target. A biased sampler refreshed from the trainer can keep moving that target, repeatedly incorporating numerical bias. This is the score-centering paper's explanation for its mismatch experiments, not a proof that every mismatch or RL collapse behaves this way. The next comparison separates this feedback from another instability.

Why offline and online fail differently

In the paper's controlled mismatch experiment, offline training is stable with \(+1/0\) rewards but unstable with negative weights; online training under mismatch is least stable with \(+1/0\) rewards. These are two different mechanisms, not a general rule that negative rewards are bad offline or positive rewards are bad online.

Offline negative updates: punishment without a stopping point. Let \(A\) be a fixed signed training weight: a raw reward or a detached advantage. For one saved response, the uncorrected surrogate is \(L_A=-A\log p_\theta(y)\), without importance weighting, clipping, or regularization. With \(A=+1\), minimizing \(-\log p_\theta(y)\) has a lower bound of zero. With \(A=-1\), minimizing \(+\log p_\theta(y)\) can drive the loss toward \(-\infty\) as the response probability approaches zero. Making an already unlikely response another thousand times less likely still improves this surrogate.

The key is fixed replay frequency: the negative example remains in the dataset regardless of its current probability. To see why its gradient need not fade, use a one-step binary policy with \(p(y)=\sigma(z)\), reward \(-1\) for \(y\), and reward zero for the other action. Gradient ascent on the negative example gives:

\[ g_{\mathrm{replay}} =-\frac{\partial\log p(y)}{\partial z}=-(1-p), \qquad g_{\mathrm{fresh}} =p\,[-(1-p)]+(1-p)\,0=-p(1-p). \]

The first quantity is per replayed negative example; the second is the expectation per fresh on-policy draw. As \(p\to0\), replay's logit push approaches \(-1\), but fresh sampling's expected push approaches zero because the bad action is rarely sampled. For comparison, a positive example's per-replay push is \(1-p\), which fades as its probability approaches one.

Fresh sampling supplies the missing probability factor. Move \(p\) toward zero: replay keeps pushing, while the fresh expected update fades. The offline curve assumes negative-only replay; if negatives occupy a fixed fraction \(f\) of a dataset whose other weights are zero, its per-record expectation is \(-f(1-p)\), not \(-p(1-p)\). Endpoints show limits. This is an exact binary illustration, not an LLM training trajectory.

Unbounded loss does not imply an exploding logit gradient: the replay gradient above stays bounded. The risk is persistent pressure to sharpen the distribution; shared model parameters can also change probabilities of useful responses. Fresh sampling reduces this particular pressure only insofar as the sampler tracks the current policy. Stale data need not supply the factor \(p\). The paper cites prior work for offline degeneration rather than establishing a universal collapse theorem; regularization or corrected objectives change this setup.

Online positive updates: chasing a moving, biased teacher. Now give both binary actions raw reward \(+1\): there is no preference to learn. The expected uncorrected logit update is \(q-p\), as illustrated below. A fixed teacher with \(q=0.51\) pulls a trainer at \(p=0.50\) toward \(0.51\); the pull vanishes when they match. For an illustrative moving teacher, \(p=\sigma(z)\) and \(q=\sigma(z+\delta)\) with a persistent \(\delta>0\), the sampler remains ahead at every finite \(z\). Repeated refreshes can keep pushing the trainer toward a deterministic policy despite equal rewards. This constant logit offset is an explanatory toy, not a model of every quantizer.

Different feedback, different failure. Offline negative replay keeps pushing away from fixed examples without reducing their frequency. Online positive training under systematic mismatch keeps pulling toward a teacher that moves with the student. Score centering targets the latter's mismatch drift; removing that drift is not a general solution to offline negative-loss degeneration.

Binary lab: isolate drift with nothing to learn

Use two tokens and a constant raw reward \(R=1\). Every outcome is equally good, so the true reward gradient is zero. Let \(p\) and \(q\) denote the first token's trainer and sampler probabilities. At the first logit, \(s_1=1-p,\ s_2=-p\), so the uncorrected expected gradient is \(q-p\).

Mismatch can move a policy without any reward preference. This is an exact, one-prefix expectation, not a simulated training curve or a benchmark. The second logit's gradient is the negative of the first. TIS leaves drift only where its cap changes the exact weights. Exact IS and exact SC have equal means here, but need not have equal finite-sample variance.

Group-centering a constant reward would trivially set all advantages to zero; that is deliberately not this experiment. In real group-centered training, advantages average to zero across responses to a prompt, but not necessarily conditional on each later prefix. A promising prefix can have positive expected advantage and a failed prefix negative expected advantage. Prompt-level centering therefore need not remove prefix-level drift.

4. Choose the treatment by what it changes

Now return to the C sample with ratio \(1.5\). A correction can change its coefficient, stop its objective's gradient, or subtract a mean-score vector. These operations are not interchangeable. First separate the objectives:

Importance sampling: change the measure, not the score

At a fixed prefix, the identity \(\mathbb E_p[f(v)]=\mathbb E_q[(p(v)/q(v))f(v)]\) changes the next-token distribution exactly when the usual support and integrability assumptions hold. For a complete response, exact trajectory IS instead uses:

\[ W(y)=\frac{P_p(y\mid x)}{P_q(y\mid x)} =\prod_t r_t =\exp\left(\sum_t \log r_t\right). \]

A token ratio does not by itself correct the distribution of prefixes or the continuation rewards sampled under another policy. With terminal response rewards, token-local IS is not full trajectory IS. Products, however, can have prohibitive variance on long responses. Clipping or masking adds further bias; it does not make that distinction disappear.

No support, no reweighting. If \(q(v\mid h)=0\) where the target needs probability mass, no finite ratio reconstructs the missing samples. Log-space arithmetic helps numerical range, not support or trajectory fidelity.

For token-local IS, \(q_v(p_v/q_v)s_v=p_vs_v\); this restores the zero-mean score at a fixed prefix. TIS replaces \(p_v/q_v\) by a capped weight, and MIS sets out-of-range weights to zero. Once modified, \(q_vw_v\) need not equal \(p_v\), so the mean can again be nonzero. These methods intentionally trade exact correction for a more manageable estimator.

Why PPO “drops” a good token at ratio 1.5

PPO does clip at 1.2: it clips the objective's incentive, not a detached gradient weight. For \(A>0\), its maximized surrogate is \(J=A\min(r,1.2)\). It is flat beyond 1.2, so the derivative vanishes:

\[ \frac{\partial J_{\mathrm{PPO}}}{\partial\log p_y} = A\,r\,C(A,r),\qquad C(A,r)= \begin{cases} 1 & A>0,\ r<1.2,\\ 1 & A<0,\ r>0.8,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \]

This expression is ordinary PPO before the table's additional dual clip. A good action whose probability fell too far still receives a push; a bad action whose probability rose too far still receives a corrective push. PPO does not drop both tails regardless of advantage sign.

Running C sample: advantage = +2, r = 1.5
PPO objective = 2.4       gradient coefficient = 0
TIS weight   = 1.5       gradient coefficient = 3.0

Even with a TIS cap of 1.2:
TIS weight   = 1.2       gradient coefficient = 2.4

Cap the push and stop the incentive to push farther solve different problems. PPO's original motivation is controlled reuse of old evidence; bounded IS weights limit a sample's influence. Numerical TIM can produce \(r=1.5\) before any optimizer movement, so PPO can suppress a signal without the trainer having already taken a large learning step. Neither clipping rule imposes a hard bound on the final parameter step or every token's probability.

DPPO needs probabilities, not just a ratio

Binary TV groups the vocabulary into “sampled token” and “everything else.” Its total-variation distance is \(|p_y-q_y|\). For a positive advantage:

q_y = 0.001, p_y = 0.010: r = 10, binary TV = 0.009 -> keep
q_y = 0.050, p_y = 0.500: r = 10, binary TV = 0.450 -> drop

The same ratio can move very different amounts of probability mass. In the Table 1 variant, a retained rare-token contribution can still have a very large IS weight: the divergence gate is not an importance-weight cap. Its motivation and binary/top-k approximations are developed in the DPPO paper.

Score centering: subtract the unwanted mean

Rather than multiply a sampled score by a ratio, replace \(s_y\) with \(s_y-\bar s\). At a fixed prefix, \(\bar s\) is the same vector for every possible sampled token:

\[ \mathbb E_q[R(s_y-\bar s)] =\mathbb E_q[Rs_y]-\mathbb E_q[R]\bar s =\operatorname{Cov}_q(R,s_y). \]

The drift disappears, including the arbitrary reward-offset effect. With constant reward the expected update is zero. With varying rewards, the covariance is still measured under \(q\), not \(p\). This is why centering can complement, rather than replace, sampling-distribution correction. The remaining question is practical: how do we obtain this vector through an ordinary scalar training loss?

5. Implement the loss without losing the intended gradient

All losses below are minimized; their negative gradients are ascent directions. Returns, advantages, stored sampler probabilities, and designated correction weights are treated as fixed for a backward pass. A scalar loss is a device for producing the desired gradient, not itself a reward metric.

Start with the basic autograd contracts

# Framework-neutral, one-token formulas; sg means stop-gradient.
ratio = exp(log_p - stored_log_q)
loss_IS  = -sg(advantage) * sg(ratio) * log_p
loss_TIS = -sg(advantage) * sg(min(ratio, cap)) * log_p
loss_MIS = -sg(advantage) * sg(ratio * in_allowed_interval(ratio)) * log_p

# PPO's ratio is differentiated inside the objective:
loss_PPO = -min(ratio * sg(advantage),
                clip(ratio, low, high) * sg(advantage))

Detaching the ratio in the weighted-logprob losses avoids an unwanted product-rule term. Detaching the whole PPO objective would instead destroy its gradient. Apply masks and the chosen token/response reduction after constructing the intended per-token loss; keep auxiliary KL and entropy terms separate.

Full score centering: one scalar loss, a vector correction

Score centering replaces \(s_y\) by its centered version under the sampler:

\[ g_{\mathrm{SC}}=A\left(s_y-\bar s\right),\qquad \bar s=\sum_v q_v s_v,\qquad \mathbb E_q[g_{\mathrm{SC}}]=\operatorname{Cov}_q(A,s). \]

The correction is deterministic given the prefix and distributions, rather than depending on which next token was sampled. It cancels the mean-score drift exactly when that expectation is computed exactly. If \(p=q\), \(\bar s=0\) and the correction is a no-op. Unlike a classical on-policy reward baseline, it deliberately changes the off-policy update's mean.

A scalar loss can produce this gradient; there is no need to materialize a vocabulary-sized collection of parameter-gradient vectors:

\[ L_{\mathrm{SC}}=-A\left(\log p_y-\sum_v\operatorname{sg}[q_v]\log p_v\right). \]

It is not full off-policy correction. The remaining covariance is measured under the sampler, not the trainer, and prefix distributions can still differ. An exact conditional value baseline could also cancel drift in expectation; score centering obtains this effect without estimating a critic.

The practical cost: more sampler information

Ordinary token IS only needs the sampled token's behavior log-probability. Exact score centering requires the full next-token distribution at every generated position. The paper estimates 608 KB per position for 152K FP32 logprobs, or roughly 20 TB for 1,024 rollouts of 32K tokens. It instead logs the sampler's top-k token IDs and their original, full-vocabulary-normalized logprobs. Let \(H\) be those IDs and \(T\) their complement; gather trainer logprobs at the same IDs, not at the trainer's independently selected top-k.

The head gives the sampler's missing total tail mass exactly: \(q(T)=1-\sum_Hq_v\). Approximate the individual tail probabilities using the trainer's relative tail proportions, scaled to match that mass:

\[ \widetilde q_v= \begin{cases} q_v & v\in H,\\ \rho p_v & v\notin H, \end{cases} \qquad \rho=\frac{1-\sum_{v\in H}q_v}{1-\sum_{v\in H}p_v}. \]

This preserves tail mass, not necessarily tail shape. It is an approximation to the real sampler, not a new sampling policy: logging top-k does not restrict generation to top-k, and the sampled token \(y\) can be outside \(H\). Do not renormalize the logged head to sum to one, which would erase the tail mass.

Why a head-only sum still includes the tail

We need the mean of the trainer's scores under the reconstructed sampler. The zero-mean identity under the trainer provides the shortcut:

\[ \sum_Hp_vs_v+\sum_Tp_vs_v=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_Tp_vs_v=-\sum_Hp_vs_v. \]

Substitute that equality into the reconstructed expectation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \bar s_{\widetilde q} &=\sum_Hq_vs_v+\rho\sum_Tp_vs_v\\ &=\sum_Hq_vs_v-\rho\sum_Hp_vs_v\\ &=\sum_H(q_v-\rho p_v)s_v. \end{aligned} \]

The tail is represented, not discarded. Its modeled contribution is the negative of the trainer-weighted head contribution, multiplied by \(\rho\). The coefficients \(q_v-\rho p_v\) are not probabilities: they can be negative and need not sum to one. This reduction is exact for \(\widetilde q\); the approximation happened when reconstructing the tail.

Use \(k=2\) only to make the arithmetic visible. The paper's default is \(k=128\). Return to the same four-token example. Suppose we retain only the sampler's head, A and B; the exact tail probabilities are now unavailable to the correction, although we know them for this synthetic illustration:

Probabilities are not scores. To compute actual numbers, focus on one gradient component: the derivative with respect to A's logit \(z_{\mathrm A}\), holding the other logits fixed. Differentiate the trainer's softmax, not the reconstruction:

\[ \log p_v=z_v-\log\sum_j e^{z_j}, \qquad s_v^{(\mathrm A)} =\frac{\partial\log p_v}{\partial z_{\mathrm A}} =\mathbf1[v=\mathrm A]-p_{\mathrm A}. \]

Exact algebra does not make the tail assumption exact. In our original example, \(q=[0.60,0.25,0.10,0.05]\), whereas \(\widetilde q=[0.60,0.25,0.1125,0.0375]\). The sampler's true tail has ratio \(2:1\); we modeled it with the trainer's \(3:1\). A's logit does not expose this error because all tail tokens have the same score component there. Across all four logits:

True mean score:        q - p = [0.10, -0.05, -0.0500,  0.0000]

More generally, approximation leaves an explicit residual drift term:

Build a scalar loss with the required derivative

Use \(R\) for the fixed signed training weight here (the advantage \(A\) in the earlier equations). We want ascent direction \(R(s_y-\bar s_{\widetilde q})\). Since each score is the gradient of a trainer logprob, choose the minimized loss:

\[ c_v=\operatorname{sg}[q_v-\rho p_v], \qquad L=-R\left(\log p_y-\sum_Hc_v\log p_v\right), \qquad -\nabla_\theta L=R\left(s_y-\sum_Hc_vs_v\right). \]

For the default example, an A sample and \(R=1\) give \(L=-[\log p_{\mathrm A}-0.225\log p_{\mathrm A}-0.025\log p_{\mathrm B}]\). Its first-logit derivative is \(-[0.5-0.225(0.5)-0.025(-0.5)]=-0.4\). Gradient descent subtracts that derivative, producing the desired \(+0.4\) ascent direction before learning rate. Autograd computes the full parameter vector without explicitly materializing every token's score.

Stop-gradient freezes coefficients, not the final logprobs. Without it, the product rule adds an unwanted term:

\[ \nabla_\theta(c_v\log p_v) =c_vs_v+\log p_v\,\nabla_\theta c_v. \]

The second term differentiates through \(p_v\) and \(\rho\) inside the coefficient; it is not part of the desired centered score. Recompute coefficients at each forward pass, then treat the entire \(q_v-\rho p_v\) expression as constant for backward.

# Framework-neutral pseudocode for one prefix, with nonzero tail mass.
# trainer_logp is normalized over the full vocabulary; R is detached.
head_logp = trainer_logp[head_ids]
q_head = exp(sampler_head_logp)
p_head = exp(head_logp)
rho = (1 - sum(q_head)) / (1 - sum(p_head))
coeff = stop_gradient(q_head - rho * p_head)
loss = -R * (trainer_logp[sampled_token] - sum(coeff * head_logp))

Numerical and batching boundaries. Accumulate probability masses in adequate precision and handle tiny tails: the paper's generalized implementation floors both tail masses at \(10^{-6}\). The pinned code instead permits zero sampler tail mass and floors the trainer tail. Such safeguards perturb the ideal identity when active; a full-vocabulary head should use full centering rather than divide zero by zero. Keep sampler IDs, logprobs, and sampled tokens aligned to the same prefix, and apply the job's loss masks and reduction after per-token losses. The head-only correction does not eliminate the trainer's vocabulary normalization.

The main experiments use \(k=128\); \(k=32\) also matches full centering in the reported ablation, not necessarily every task. That empirical result does not remove the tail-shape approximation or the residual drift derived above.

Why it can compose with TIS or MIS

For a token-local ratio weight \(w_v=w(p_v/q_v)\), such as TIS or MIS, center the weighted score, not the unweighted one:

\[ g_{\mathrm{SC}+w} =A\left(w_y s_y-\sum_v q_v w_v s_v\right). \]

The centering mass \(q_v w_v\) is not normalized. Exact IS makes \(q_vw_v=p_v\), so its expected weighted score already vanishes. Truncation or masking can break that cancellation; the additive term removes the residual mean. This formula is for the stated token-local weights, not an automatic recipe for every sequence-level or advantage-dependent gate.

For the top-k implementation, the modeled tail has constant ratio \(p_v/\widetilde q_v=1/\rho\), so its weight is \(f(1/\rho)\). Repeat the head/tail reduction with \(\alpha=\rho f(1/\rho)\):

\[ \bar s_{\widetilde q,\widetilde w} =\sum_H(q_vw_v-\alpha p_v)s_v, \qquad L=-A\left(\operatorname{sg}[w_y]\log p_y -\sum_H\operatorname{sg}[q_vw_v-\alpha p_v]\log p_v\right). \]

The sampled-token weight \(w_y\) still uses its actual logged \(q_y\), even outside the head; only the unsaved tail weights inside the centering expectation are modeled. Detach both the head coefficients and the sampled-token weight. Vanilla centering has \(f=1\), so \(\alpha=\rho\). Exact IS with the required support has \(f(r)=r\), giving \(\alpha=1\) and zero head coefficients. These are useful implementation checks; Appendix A.2 derives the composition.

Wire the loss into a real token batch

The per-prefix algebra is small; most implementation errors occur at its boundaries. Keep these tensors aligned, with \(B\) responses, \(T\) prediction positions, vocabulary size \(V\), and \(k\) logged head tokens:

QuantityShape / roleContract

Head IDs and sampler head logprobs\([B,T,k]\); score centeringOriginal normalized probabilities at each exact prefix; preserve IDs and ordering.

The pinned implementation shifts targets and masks to next-token prediction and evaluates the output head and log-softmax in rematerialized chunks, limiting live vocabulary-sized intermediates. If the vocabulary is sharded, the gather and normalization must respect that sharding. Top-k reduces stored sampler metadata; it does not remove the trainer's full-vocabulary normalization.

Aggregate per-token losses using the job's declared token or response normalization, and handle an empty valid-token mask without division by zero. The pinned code also adds detached scalar offsets: these can change reported loss values without changing gradients. Compare the intended gradient and reduction, not only the displayed loss.

Optional implementation detail: separate sampler mismatch from optimizer movement

A frozen trainer snapshot provides a third reference point

The core guide needs only \(p\) and \(q\). A decoupled PPO recipe can additionally freeze a trainer snapshot \(\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\) before optimizing a batch. For our sampled C token, suppose its probabilities are \(q=0.10\), snapshot \(0.12\), and current trainer \(0.15\):

\[ \underbrace{\frac{p_y}{q_y}}_{r=1.5} =\underbrace{\frac{p_y}{\pi_{\mathrm{prox},y}}}_{u=1.25:\ \text{optimizer movement}} \underbrace{\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{prox},y}}{q_y}}_{b=1.2:\ \text{starting mismatch}}. \]

Before those optimizer steps, \(p=\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\), so \(u=1\), while \(b\) can already differ from one. A three-policy recipe can apply detached TIS/MIS to \(b\) and PPO's objective clip to \(u\):

\[ L=-\mathbb E_q\!\left[ \operatorname{sg}[\widetilde b]\, \min(uA,\operatorname{clip}(u,l,h)A)\right]. \]

Here \(\widetilde b\) is the bounded/masked behavior weight; \(A\) is detached. A bypass/two-policy recipe uses the sampler as the anchor, giving \(b=1\) and \(u=p/q\). The paper's correction grid uses the sampler directly in the denominator. These are policy roles, not necessarily three resident copies of a model.

Other notation names \(q\) as \(\mu\), and \(p\) as \(\pi_\theta\). The Async RL summary uses \(q_t\) for an update ratio, not this page's sampler distribution. A separate KL reference policy penalizes deviation from a chosen reference; it is not automatically the sampler or this update snapshot.

StableReinforce: a production score-centering variant

TML's Inkling 2 Small launch describes StableReinforce as a score-centering variant for asynchronous, low-precision RL. The equations above explain the published score-centering method, not the complete StableReinforce objective. Keep the shared idea separate from its implementation.

  • Advantages are external. The loss accepts already-computed advantages, so it can be used with group-relative reward centering. Subtracting a group's mean reward and centering the gradient score are different operations; one does not replace the other.
  • Sampler–learner KL is configurable. The API offers k1 (default) and k3, with kl_coeff=0.01 by default; zero disables it. This term compares sampler and learner, not the learner and a separate fixed reference model.
  • Sampling provenance is required. Enable record_stability_info=True and preserve sequence IDs and both provenance span lists. Nonzero advantages must identify stability-recorded sampled tokens. Ordinary token/log-probability records alone do not supply the required server-side information.
  • The core estimator remains undisclosed. The inspected public sources do not specify the recorded information, exact centering approximation, or additional stabilization details. Do not equate it with this paper's top-k estimator or TIS/MIS composition.

The default k1 KL term adds an advantage-independent pull toward sampled tokens. Even if centering removes reward-driven drift, the complete loss can still update the policy through KL regularization.

See the alpha API documentation (checked November 27, 2026) and the Inkling usage discussion.

6. Reduce mismatch in the engine and rollout pipeline

Loss-side correction tolerates a discrepancy; engine-side work tries to prevent it. They are complementary. Match the effective quantization, normalization, routing, and reduction behavior of both forwards rather than assuming that equal checkpoint files or dtypes imply equal probabilities. Any fake-quantization backward still needs its own declared gradient contract.

Control policy age separately from numerical parity

Record the policy version for generated tokens or segments, then measure its gap from the version that trains on them. Refresh sampler weights, avoid excessive post-generation queues, and account for long tool calls and group completion. Faster training can increase version staleness even if rollout wall time is unchanged.

Rocket's reported controls include frequent weight transfers, prioritizing in-flight rollouts, dropping failed rollouts instead of holding whole groups, and short learner queues. These trade throughput, utilization, or selection bias against freshness; dropping failures can bias group-relative advantages. The Async RL guide owns the scheduling details. None of these controls alone aligns floating-point execution.

Numerical parity: make the reduction structure part of the contract

MAI's November 23 Rocket article reports bitwise sampler/learner parity for identical checkpoints and inputs, with no noticeable performance degradation in its setup. Matching dtypes alone is insufficient: both paths must also agree on arithmetic grouping, intermediate rounding, and the values being combined. The mechanisms below are reported design choices; the illustrative kernel contract is not released Rocket source code.

Attention: gather inputs, rather than reduce projected outputs

For one token, write its concatenated head outputs as \(z=[z_0,z_1]\), and partition the rows of \(W_O\) into \(W_0,W_1\), using the convention \(y=zW_O\). Normal attention TP computes \(y_0=z_0W_0\) and \(y_1=z_1W_1\) separately, then sums the full-width partial outputs across GPUs:

\[ y=zW_O=z_0W_0+z_1W_1 \qquad \text{in exact arithmetic}. \] 
Usual row-parallel projection:
  GPU 0: z0 @ W0 -> y0
  GPU 1: z1 @ W1 -> y1
  AllReduce(sum): y = y0 + y1

Rocket's reported change:
  AllGather head outputs: z = concatenate(z0, z1)
  Replicate W_O; compute each token's full z @ W_O locally

AllGather transports values without summing them. Attention-head work stays distributed, but the projection's entire contraction for a token can use the same local reduction structure as the non-TP learner. This spends replicated projection-weight memory and gathered activations; it does not remove communication. Replicated weights do not imply that every GPU must redundantly project every token. See standard head-split attention TP.

MoE: return separate contributions, then combine in FP32

Rocket distributes complete experts with EP, without TP inside each expert. A token still selects several experts, so its MoE output is \(y_t=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}a_{t,k}e_{t,k}\), where \(e_{t,k}\) is an expert's output vector and the normalized routing weights satisfy \(\sum_k a_{t,k}=1\). An all-to-all returns individual expert outputs to the device that owned the token at this layer's entry. That device performs the weighted sum locally in FP32. The exchange is transport; the subsequent combination is arithmetic.

For example, routing weights \(0.75,0.25\) and expert vectors \([2,4],[10,0]\) yield \([4,3]\). At real tensor sizes, differences in reduction grouping and precision can change rounded results even when the mathematical sum is the same. The dispatch/combine walkthrough explains the token and expert ownership.

One way to enforce the contract, not Rocket's disclosed kernel:
  1. Carry token and expert-slot IDs through dispatch. Restore outputs and corresponding weights to one agreed slot order, independent of network arrival order or EP placement.
  2. Wait for the required outputs. Give each token/hidden-coordinate pair a single accumulator owner, and traverse its experts in that fixed order.
  3. Convert operands to FP32 before multiplication; agree on fused versus separately rounded multiply/add, accumulator precision, final cast, and subnormal handling.
  4. Use the same arithmetic in both forward paths. Keep the reduction structure fixed across batch sizes; a fixed parallel tree is also valid. Avoid unordered floating-point atomic additions into the result.
// Illustrative CUDA kernel core: buffers are FP32.
// One thread owns token t and hidden coordinate d.
float acc = 0.0f;
for (int k = 0; k < K; ++k) {
    float w = weights[t * K + k];
    float v = outputs[(t * K + k) * D + d];
    acc = __fmaf_rn(w, v, acc);
}
result[t * D + d] = acc;

__fmaf_rn specifies an FP32 fused multiply-add with round-to-nearest-even. Matching compiler numerical modes, including flush-to-zero behavior, remains necessary. Threads can run freely across tokens and hidden coordinates: no other thread changes a coordinate's accumulator. A learner integration also needs a correct backward; this sketch specifies only the forward combination. FP32 combining neither makes expert GEMMs FP32 nor restores information already lost in their outputs.

Verify with identical expert vectors and routing weights, comparing raw output bits while varying batch size, token order, EP placement, and communication completion order. A deterministic kernel can still disagree with another deterministic kernel. Likewise, fixing the combine step cannot repair different routing choices, normalization, or expert outputs.

Attention kernels: match tiles and split boundaries

MAI uses a shared FA4 implementation across sampler decode, sampler prefill, and learner forward. Sliding-window attention aligns sampler K-block boundaries with learner tiles and masks positions outside the valid window. This prevents compact cache layouts from changing the grouping in online-softmax and attention-value reductions. The stack jointly tunes the number of KV tokens per split, then uses that fixed value and matching split boundaries across all three paths. See the online-softmax recurrence for the arithmetic being grouped.

Parity is scoped, not a universal on-policy switch. The reported learning-curve comparison is on a smaller 180B-total/7B-active model, with runs launched roughly a month apart from the same checkpoint. It does not isolate each change. Same-checkpoint parity removes an implementation discrepancy; it does not make old-checkpoint rollout data fresh. Rocket's case study keeps the precision, scheduling, and evidence context together.

Forward parity, backward correctness, and successful learning

These are three separate claims: sampler and learner forward outputs agree; backward implements the declared gradient reference; and training remains stable and learns effectively. None alone establishes the others. Both forward paths can agree while the learner's backward uses an inappropriate saved value or violates a gradient invariant. A stable loss curve also does not identify which gradient was followed.

The FlashAttention stability evidence map distinguishes forward-deviation proxies, reimplementation precision, saved-output errors, and a post-cast score-gradient conservation defect. The saved-state contract explains how to specify the backward reference, including any straight-through treatment of rounding. These are reasons to validate each contract, not evidence that FA4 or Rocket exhibits the failures reported in other implementations.

7. Diagnose, choose, and verify

Start with provenance, then isolate the source, then change one treatment at a time. A method name alone does not specify the behavior policy, ratio denominator, mask, reduction, auxiliary losses, or numerical safeguards.

First preserve the exact tokens and probability provenance. Next use a same-checkpoint, same-prefix replay to isolate numerical TIM from staleness. Track log-probability errors, ratio tails, effective sample size, and correction activation by advantage sign. Label whether a metric compares \(p/q\), \(p/\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\), or \(\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}/q\).

A high Pearson correlation does not exclude a dangerous rare tail; a low correlation does not alone establish large probability error or a causal drift mechanism. Clipped-token fraction counts interventions, not their vector-valued gradient bias. Continue to PPO versus correction metrics, ratio statistics, and queue/version controls for operational diagnosis.

Verify the algebra before trusting a learning curve

  1. Prediction alignment: replay the exact prefix and target IDs; verify next-token shifts, masks, and logged sampler probabilities before comparing ratios.
  2. On-policy and constant-reward checks: on a small full vocabulary, verify \(\sum_vp_vs_v=0\), exact IS's zero mean, and exact score centering's zero mean under constant reward. Finite batches fluctuate; enumerate the toy distribution when testing these expectation identities.
  3. Loss-gradient checks: differentiate the scalar losses, test both advantage signs, and probe either side of clipping/masking boundaries. For finite differences of a detached-weight loss, freeze the coefficients during parameter perturbations; recomputing them tests a different derivative.
  4. Top-k checks: compare the head-only sum with the full reconstructed distribution, test sampled tokens outside the head, and separately measure residual drift against the true sampler. Test tiny tails and numerical floors explicitly.
  5. Engine checks: compare the same inputs/checkpoint across batch shapes, parallel layouts and reduction paths. Validate backward against its declared reference separately, then evaluate training stability and held-out capability.

What the experiments establish—and what they do not

In the reported severe quantization settings, score centering matches or outperforms the IS baselines; under severe staleness, its composition with TIS/MIS performs best. That supports treating drift removal and distribution correction as complementary. It does not establish that score centering universally replaces PPO, engine alignment, or importance sampling.

The evidence comes from Countdown and math training with Qwen models, deliberately severe mismatch, short sequences, a shared objective, and uneven seed counts in the expensive 30B comparisons. Some figures report training accuracy rather than held-out accuracy. Preserve those limits when applying the result to long-horizon agents or production workloads.

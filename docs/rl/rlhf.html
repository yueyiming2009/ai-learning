RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

The full math of the classic three-stage pipeline: a reward model from preferences, a KL-regularized RL objective, and the PPO update that optimizes it.

Notation

\(\pi_\theta\)Policy being trained (the LM), parameters \(\theta\)

1. The Three-Stage Pipeline

RLHF (as in InstructGPT) aligns a pretrained language model to human preferences in three sequential stages. Each stage produces the input for the next.

Stage 1
SFT
Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations. Produces \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).
→
Stage 2
Reward Model
Fit \(r_\phi\) to human preference comparisons (§2).
→
Stage 3
RL (PPO)
Optimize \(\pi_\theta\) against \(r_\phi\) with a KL leash (§3–5).

This page focuses on the math of Stages 2 and 3. Stage 1 is ordinary cross-entropy fine-tuning — see the cross-entropy page.

This page is the applied capstone of the RL series. The policy-gradient theorem, advantage estimation, and GAE are derived on the Policy Gradient page; the PPO optimizer they feed — the clipped surrogate, actor–critic value loss, entropy bonus, and update loop — is on the PPO page. RLHF is what those add up to once the environment is a language model: a reward model, a reference-policy KL leash, and a rollouter that is its own inference engine.

2. Reward Model

We can't ask humans for absolute scores, only comparisons. The reward model is a scalar head on a transformer that maps \((x,y)\) to a single number, trained so that preferred completions score higher. Bradley–Terry treats each pairwise comparison as a soft probability rather than a hard winner: the probability that a human annotator would prefer \(y_w\) over \(y_l\) given prompt \(x\) is

\[ p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp r_\phi(x,y_w)}{\exp r_\phi(x,y_w) + \exp r_\phi(x,y_l)} = \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big) \]
Where the sigmoid comes from. Let \(r_w = r_\phi(x,y_w)\) and \(r_l = r_\phi(x,y_l)\). Divide numerator and denominator by \(\exp r_w\): \[ \frac{e^{r_w}}{e^{r_w} + e^{r_l}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{r_l - r_w}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_w - r_l)}} = \sigma(r_w - r_l) \] Exact algebra — no approximation. Only the gap \(\Delta = r_w - r_l\) matters; shifting both scores by the same constant cancels out and leaves the probability unchanged.
Reading the probability. \(p = \sigma(\Delta)\) where \(\Delta = r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\):
  • \(\Delta = 0\): \(p = 0.5\) — model sees the two responses as equally good; coin-flip likely either is preferred.
  • \(\Delta \gg 0\) (e.g. \(+4\)): \(p \approx 0.98\) — model is confident \(y_w\) is the better response.
  • \(\Delta \ll 0\) (e.g. \(-4\)): \(p \approx 0.02\) — model currently scores \(y_l\) higher than \(y_w\); training will push hard to correct this.
The soft probability also captures annotation noise: two annotators can genuinely disagree on a borderline pair. Rather than forcing a hard 0/1 label, BT lets the model reflect that ambiguity.

To turn this into a training loss, we fit \(\phi\) by maximum likelihood over the whole comparison dataset \(\mathcal{D}\), in three steps.

Step 1 — Likelihood of the dataset

Each example in \(\mathcal{D}\) is already labeled so that \(y_w\) is the human-preferred answer. Assuming examples are independent, the probability of observing all the labels is the product of the per-comparison probabilities:

\[ \mathcal{P}(\phi) = \prod_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in\mathcal{D}} p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \prod_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in\mathcal{D}} \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big) \]
Step 2 — Take the log

Maximizing \(\mathcal{P}\) is the same as maximizing \(\log\mathcal{P}\) (the log is monotonic), and the log turns the product into a sum — easier to optimize and numerically stable:

\[ \log \mathcal{P}(\phi) = \sum_{(x,y_w,y_l)\in\mathcal{D}} \log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big) \]
Step 3 — Negate and average

Flip the sign to turn "maximize log-likelihood" into "minimize a loss," and average over the dataset (the \(\tfrac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum\) is written as an expectation \(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}}\)). Minimizing the result drives \(r_w\) up and \(r_l\) down: a pair already ranked correctly contributes almost no gradient, while a pair ranked backwards contributes a large one.

Reward model loss
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal{D}} \Big[\, \log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big) \Big] \]

Only reward differences matter, so \(r_\phi\) is identified only up to an additive constant; implementations usually normalize scores to zero mean. The same Bradley–Terry likelihood reappears in DPO — the difference is where the reward lives: a separate network \(r_\phi\) here, versus the policy itself in DPO.

3. The RL Objective

With \(r_\phi\) frozen, Stage 3 trains the policy to maximize reward while staying close to the reference model. The KL leash stops the policy from drifting into degenerate text that fools the reward model (reward hacking):

KL-regularized RL objective
\[ \max_{\pi_\theta}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)} \big[\, r_\phi(x,y) \,\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \]

This is the same objective DPO starts from — DPO solves it in closed form, whereas standard RLHF optimizes it directly with reinforcement learning. The rest of this page is how that direct optimization works.

This \(\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) leash is the same device as PPO's entropy bonus, which is a KL pull toward the uniform policy (\(H[\pi] = \log|\mathcal{A}| - \mathrm{KL}(\pi\|u)\); see PPO §6). Same regularizer, different anchor — \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) here, uniform there. Because this reference KL already arrests entropy collapse and anchors at a fluent distribution, RLHF usually sets the entropy coefficient \(c_2 \approx 0\); it matters again only when the KL leash is weak or absent, as in verifiable-reward GRPO.

Folding the KL into a per-token reward

In practice the KL is not computed exactly, because written as a sum, \(\mathrm{KL} = \sum_y \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), it ranges over every possible completion \(y\) — \(|\text{vocab}|^{\text{length}}\) sequences, far too many to enumerate (the same wall as \(Z(x)\) on the DPO page).

But the very same quantity is an expectation, \(\mathrm{KL} = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\big]\), and RLHF is already sampling \(y\sim\pi_\theta\) to generate rollouts. So the log-ratio on that one sampled sequence is a free, unbiased Monte-Carlo estimate of the KL. Absorbing it into the reward gives the effective reward:

\[ R(x,y) = r_\phi(x,y) \;-\; \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \]

Because generation is autoregressive, \(\pi(y\mid x) = \prod_t \pi(a_t\mid s_t)\), and \(\log\) of a product is a sum — so the sequence log-ratio splits exactly into per-token log-ratios, \(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} = \sum_t \log\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)}\). Each per-token \(\log\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\) is a single log_softmax gather over that position's logits, \(z_{a_t} - \operatorname{logsumexp}(\mathbf{z})\) (computing log-probabilities from logits). The reward model then contributes a single terminal reward at the final token, while the KL penalty becomes a dense per-token shaping reward:

\[ R_t = \underbrace{\mathbf{1}[t = T]\, r_\phi(x,y)}_{\text{terminal RM reward}} \;-\; \underbrace{\beta\, \log\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t\mid s_t)}}_{\text{per-token KL penalty}} \]
Framed as RL: each generated token is an action \(a_t\), the state \(s_t\) is the prompt plus tokens generated so far, and an episode is one full completion. The reward is sparse (one RM score at the end) plus a dense KL penalty at every token. A dense penalty also aids credit assignment — it flags which tokens drifted, instead of one lump sum at the end.
This single-sample log-ratio is unbiased but can be negative for an individual sample (a true KL cannot) and is high-variance. GRPO swaps in the always-positive \(u-\log u-1\) estimator to fix this.

The policy gradient theorem, advantage estimation, and GAE are derived step by step on the Policy Gradient page. The result used here: given per-token advantages \(\hat A_t\) computed via GAE \(\bigl(\hat A_t = \sum_l (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}\bigr)\), the gradient of the expected return is:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_t \hat A_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\right] \]

Push up the log-probability of actions that outperformed expectations (\(\hat A_t > 0\)); suppress those that underperformed. The PPO objective below clips this gradient to prevent a destructive large step.

4. The Optimizer: PPO

With per-token rewards \(R_t\) and advantages \(\hat A_t\) in hand, Stage 3 maximizes the objective with PPO — a clipped policy-gradient update that keeps each step inside a trust region of the policy that collected the rollouts, \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\). Writing the probability ratio \(\rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\):

PPO clipped surrogate
\[ \mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[ \min\!\Big( \rho_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t,\;\; \mathrm{clip}\big(\rho_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat A_t \Big) \right] \]

The clip removes the incentive to take a destructive large step and lets each batch of (expensive) rollouts fund several gradient epochs. The full objective adds the critic's value regression and an entropy bonus, \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}} = -\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}} + c_1(V_\psi(s_t) - \hat R_t)^2 - c_2\,S[\pi_\theta]\). The mechanics — the trust-region rationale, the actor–critic value head, the entropy term, and the \(K\)-epoch update loop — are derived on the PPO page. Everything RLHF adds sits around this optimizer (reward model, reference-policy KL, the token-level MDP), not inside it.

5. The Full RLHF Loop

Stage 3 repeats the following until the policy converges or KL budget is exhausted:

  1. Rollout — sample prompts \(x\sim\mathcal{D}\) and generate completions \(y\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot\mid x)\). Unlike a gym, the "environment" is the model decoding its own tokens, so this step is run by a dedicated inference engine (vLLM / SGLang) — the rollouter — and is usually the most expensive part of the loop. See RL Inference.
  2. Score — compute the terminal reward \(r_\phi(x,y)\) and the per-token KL penalty to form \(R_t\) (§3).
  3. Estimate — run the critic \(V_\psi\) and compute advantages \(\hat A_t\) via GAE (see Policy Gradient §3).
  4. Optimize — take several PPO epochs of minibatch updates on \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}\) (see the PPO page), then refresh \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\).
This requires four models in memory: the policy \(\pi_\theta\), the critic \(V_\psi\), the frozen reference \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), and the reward model \(r_\phi\). That cost and instability is precisely the pain DPO removes. GRPO takes a different path: drop the critic entirely and use a group of sampled completions as the baseline, reducing memory to three models.

6. RLHF vs. DPO

Both optimize the identical KL-regularized objective of §3. They differ in how.

Standard RLHF (PPO)DPO
Reward modelExplicit network \(r_\phi\)Implicit: \(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\)
OptimizationOnline RL, sampling rolloutsOffline supervised loss on pairs
Models in memoryPolicy, critic, reference, RMPolicy + reference
Objective solvedDirectly, via PPOIn closed form (no RL loop)
StabilitySensitive; many knobsStable; a single loss
Trade-offCan use online data & any rewardTied to the preference dataset

See the DPO derivation for how the explicit reward and the PPO loop are algebraically eliminated.

Two findings from a production run

The comparison above is mostly architectural. DeepSeek-V2's alignment section reports two empirical results on the same axis, stated without hedging, that are worth carrying:

Online beats offline

On their preference-alignment experiments, the online approach "significantly outperforms" the offline one — enough that they invested in building online RL infrastructure rather than taking the simpler offline path. The row above reads as a neutral trade-off; at least one team measured it and found it wasn't neutral. They also note the conclusion may not transfer across contexts.

The alignment tax is real and measurable. Human-preference alignment improved open-ended generation on both AI and human ratings, while regressing standard benchmarks — BBH specifically. The KL term of §3 is the standard defence, but it constrains distance from the reference model, not performance on any particular capability; nothing in the objective prevents trading BBH for AlpacaEval. DeepSeek-V2 treated this as a data-and-schedule problem and reports settling for "a tolerable trade-off" rather than eliminating it. Worth expecting when reading any post-RLHF benchmark table.

7. Summary

RM loss

\(-\mathbb{E}[\log\sigma(r_w - r_l)]\)

RL objective

\(\max\,\mathbb{E}[r_\phi] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\)

Effective reward

\(r_\phi - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\)

Policy gradient

\(\mathbb{E}[\sum_t \hat A_t \nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)]\)

Advantage (GAE)

\(\hat A_t = \sum_l (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}\)

PPO surrogate

\(\mathbb{E}[\min(\rho_t\hat A_t,\,\mathrm{clip}(\rho_t)\hat A_t)]\)

Monitoring & Diagnosis for Async RL

What to log, how to read it, and how to trace training pathologies back to policy lag, TIM, entropy collapse, or the TIS constant-corrector trap.

This page is a companion to Async RL — Policy Lag, IS Correction, and TIM. The concepts below (IS ratios, TIM, TIS, entropy collapse) are derived there. Applies to any async RL system — verl, slime/Miles, or otherwise.

These metrics fall into three groups: staleness signals (is the buffer too old?), signal health (is the gradient informative?), and stability signals (is training about to collapse?). Most apply to any async RL system, not just VeRL. Before interpreting any field containing ratio or clipfrac, identify which pair of policies it compares.

Two ratios, two clipping layers. A decoupled async implementation can distinguish the rollout behavior policy \(\mu\), the frozen proximal policy \(\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\) used for the current update, and the actor being optimized \(\pi_\theta\): \[ q_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}(a_t|s_t)} \qquad b_t = \frac{\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}(a_t|s_t)}{\mu(a_t|s_t)}. \] PPO clipping constrains \(q_t\), the movement made inside the optimizer update. TIS clips or masks \(b_t\), the mismatch already present when a rollout reaches the trainer because of policy lag, engine numerics, quantization, or token reconstruction. Older two-policy implementations may set \(\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}=\mu\) or log only one combined ratio, which is why the same metric name can have different diagnostic meaning.

1. pg_clipfrac — Proximal-Update Clipping

In a three-policy implementation, pg_clipfrac is the fraction of response tokens where the PPO proximal ratio \(q_t\) leaves its allowed interval:

\[ \text{pg\_clipfrac} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \mathbf{1}\!\left[q_t < 1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{low}} \;\lor\; q_t > 1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{high}}\right]. \]

Here \(\mathcal{T}\) is the set of non-padding response tokens. Some loggers count every out-of-band ratio; others count only tokens for which PPO's clipped surrogate branch actually wins. Check the implementation before comparing absolute values across frameworks.

What it catches:

  • Repeated-update drift — later PPO epochs or minibatches move \(\pi_\theta\) away from the frozen \(\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\), so more ratios hit the bound.
  • Update aggressiveness — a high learning rate, large advantages, or too many optimization passes raises clipfrac even when the rollout data itself is fresh.
  • Combined-ratio behavior in older stacks — if the denominator is the rollout policy rather than a separate proximal snapshot, policy lag and TIM also enter this metric. In that design pg_clipfrac mixes two failure modes that a three-policy objective reports separately.

There is no implementation-independent healthy range. With several PPO epochs, a moderate non-zero value can be normal and a sustained value above ~50% means most updates are being truncated. With one actor iteration per fresh batch, however, \(q_t \approx 1\) at measurement time and pg_clipfrac = 0 can be exactly expected. The policy-gradient loss can still be non-zero because the advantages still produce a gradient; zero clipfrac means only that the proximal guardrail did not activate.

Metric keys — verl: actor/pg_clipfrac (combined upper+lower) and actor/pg_clipfrac_lower (lower clip tracked separately as a first-class metric) · Miles: train/pg_clipfrac (combined only; upper vs lower split requires custom logging).

Asymmetry matters — track upper and lower clipfrac separately

The aggregate clipfrac hides the direction of proximal-policy movement. The ratio \(q_t = \pi_\theta(a_t)/\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}(a_t)\) can leave the interval from either side:

  • High ratio \((q_t > 1+\varepsilon_{\mathrm{high}})\): \(\pi_\theta\) assigns more probability than the proximal snapshot.
  • Low ratio \((q_t < 1-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{low}})\): \(\pi_\theta\) assigns less probability than the proximal snapshot.

The advantage sign determines whether standard PPO actually activates the clipped branch:

Positive advantage \((A_t > 0)\)
action helped — should repeat it		 Negative advantage \((A_t < 0)\)
action hurt — should avoid it
High ratio
actor moved toward action		 Clipping-active: caps excessive reinforcement of a helpful action. Normally unclipped: the actor moved toward an action with negative advantage; inspect optimizer noise or conflicting batches.

actor moved away from action		 Normally unclipped: the actor moved away from an action with positive advantage; inspect optimizer noise or conflicting batches. Clipping-active: caps excessive suppression of a harmful action.

A logger that records all out-of-band ratios can populate all four cells; a logger that records only activation of PPO's clipped surrogate should primarily populate positive/high and negative/low. Large mass in the two normally-unclipped cells is still useful as a directional diagnostic, but it is not itself evidence that PPO discarded those tokens.

True-on-policy/TIM elimination affects \(b_t\), not \(q_t\). Making SGLang and Megatron bitwise equal sets \(\mu=\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\), so TIS correction becomes 1. PPO clipfrac can still rise after repeated optimizer passes because \(\pi_\theta\) moves away from \(\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\). Miles logs the behavior mismatch directly through train/train_rollout_logprob_abs_diff and train/train_rollout_kl.

2. Pearson Correlation — Log-prob Alignment

Measures how well the rollout behavior log-probs \(\log \mu\) and the recomputed proximal/training log-probs \(\log \pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\) agree in relative ordering across all tokens in a batch:

\[ \rho = \frac{\mathrm{Cov}(\log\mu,\;\log\pi_{\mathrm{prox}})} {\mathrm{Std}(\log\mu)\cdot\mathrm{Std}(\log\pi_{\mathrm{prox}})} \]

where the vectors run over all \((sequence, position)\) pairs in the batch.

Why not just KL? KL divergence \(\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\mu\|\pi_{\mathrm{prox}})\) is dominated by high-probability tokens and averages over the full batch — a few badly misaligned low-probability tokens produce extreme IS ratios but barely move aggregate KL. Pearson is scale-invariant: a constant offset between the two log-prob vectors (e.g., from a different softmax temperature or calibration shift) doesn't change \(\rho\), but does change IS ratios. Falling \(\rho\) means the ranking of tokens by probability is diverging, which is what actually drives IS ratio variance.

Connection to IS ratio variance

If \(\log\mu\) and \(\log\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}\) are jointly distributed with correlation \(\rho\) and roughly equal variance \(\sigma^2\), then:

\[ \mathrm{Var}(\log b_t) = \mathrm{Var}(\log\pi_{\mathrm{prox}} - \log\mu) = 2\sigma^2(1 - \rho) \]

At \(\rho = 0.97\): \(\mathrm{Var}(\log b_t) \approx 0.06\sigma^2\) — behavior-correction ratios tightly clustered around 1. At \(\rho = 0.80\): \(\mathrm{Var}(\log b_t) \approx 0.40\sigma^2\) — ratios scattered widely; many tokens can enter the TIS tails even when the separate PPO ratio \(q_t\) remains near 1.

Healthy: \(\rho \geq 0.97\). Below 0.90 is a warning; sustained drop below 0.85 typically precedes training collapse.

Metric keys — verl: training/rollout_actor_probs_pearson_corr — logged automatically whenever rollout_log_probs is in the batch (i.e., whenever the rollout engine stores its log-probs separately, which is the case in async/TIM-correction mode). Implemented in verl/utils/debug/metrics.py via torch.corrcoef over valid response tokens. Note: verl computes this on the probability scale (\(\pi_{actor}\) vs \(\pi_{rollout}\), i.e., exp(log_probs)), not on log-probs directly — the interpretation is the same but the numeric value will differ slightly from a log-space Pearson. Companion metrics from the same call: training/rollout_probs_diff_mean, training/rollout_probs_diff_max, training/rollout_probs_diff_std (mean/max/std of \(|\pi_{actor} - \pi_{rollout}|\) per token, on probability scale) · Miles: not built-in; use train/train_rollout_kl as the closest proxy.

3. Entropy Collapse — How TIM Compounds Over Training

The "gradual drop at \(K=0\)" pattern has a specific mechanism. At initialization, the policy is relatively diffuse — log-probs span a wide range (e.g., \([-12, 0]\)). As training proceeds and the policy learns to prefer correct actions, the distribution sharpens: sampled tokens cluster toward \(\log p \approx 0\), while rejected tokens fall to \(-10\) or below.

The two engines have a fixed absolute numerical discrepancy \(\delta\) per token from TIM. When log-probs are spread across a wide range, \(\delta\) rarely causes rank inversions — token A is still more probable than B even with small errors. When log-probs are clustered tightly near 0, the same \(\delta\) frequently swaps the relative ordering of tokens within the dense cluster. Pearson, which measures rank agreement, collapses.

Step 0:  policy diffuse, log-probs span [-12, 0]
         engine difference δ ≈ ±0.01 per token
         δ rarely exceeds gaps between tokens → few rank inversions → ρ = 0.80

Step 50: policy sharp, sampled tokens cluster in [-0.5, 0]
         same δ ≈ ±0.01, but now many tokens are within δ of each other
         frequent rank inversions within the dense cluster → ρ = 0.30

This creates a self-reinforcing feedback loop:

  1. TIM causes IS ratios to scatter from 1
  2. Scattered ratios produce large, noisy gradient updates
  3. Large updates push the policy into sharper, more confident weight regimes
  4. Sharper policy amplifies TIM's rank-inversion effect on \(\rho\)
  5. Lower \(\rho\) → wider IS ratio scatter → back to step 2

This is a runaway loop. \(\rho\) falls not because TIM is growing but because entropy collapse makes the existing TIM increasingly visible.

4. TIS Constant-Corrector Trap

TIS (Truncated IS) acts on the behavior-correction ratio \(b_t=\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}(a_t|s_t)/\mu(a_t|s_t)\), not on PPO's proximal-update ratio \(q_t\). Implementations use either a one-sided upper cap or a two-sided interval:

\[ \tilde{b}_t = \operatorname{clip}(b_t,\; c_{\mathrm{low}},\; c_{\mathrm{high}}). \]

When TIS is enabled and \(\rho\) stops falling and holds at its step-0 value (e.g., 0.8), TIS has broken the feedback loop — not fixed TIM. By bounding \(b_t\), TIS bounds the gradient updates, which slows entropy collapse, which prevents further amplification of TIM's Pearson effect. The Pearson stabilizes at exactly the baseline TIM level from step 0.

But at \(\rho = 0.80\), behavior-correction ratios are widely scattered, so a large fraction of tokens can exceed the TIS bounds on every batch. TIS is doing significant corrective work on every training step, not occasionally. The gradient being computed is:

\[ \tilde{g} = \sum_t \tilde{b}_t \cdot \nabla\log\pi_\theta \cdot A_t \] \[ \text{Correction removed} = \sum_t (b_t-\tilde{b}_t) \cdot \nabla\log\pi_\theta \cdot A_t \]

The tokens being truncated are those with the largest rollout-to-trainer mismatch. They are not necessarily the strongest learning signals, but clipping them changes their weight systematically. This introduces:

  • Reduced effective learning rate — the policy moves more slowly than the configured LR implies, because the largest updates are capped.
  • Directional bias — TIS consistently underweights high-ratio tokens, skewing the gradient toward smaller, more conservative policy changes. The converged policy reflects a truncated objective, not the true PPO objective.
  • Compounding bias — each step's truncation accumulates; over hundreds of steps the policy drifts toward a different optimum than intended.

Diagnostic: log tis_clipfrac — the fraction of response tokens where \(b_t\) leaves the configured correction interval. If consistently above ~20–30%, TIS is in constant-corrector mode and the gradient bias is substantial.

TIS tail × advantage-sign decomposition

MiMo exposes four fields whose sum is pg_tis_clipfrac:

Metric suffixConditionInterpretation
pos_high \(A_t > 0,\ b_t > c_{\mathrm{high}}\) Upper-tail correction on tokens carrying positive learning signal.
pos_low \(A_t > 0,\ b_t < c_{\mathrm{low}}\) Lower-tail correction on tokens carrying positive learning signal.
neg_high \(A_t < 0,\ b_t > c_{\mathrm{high}}\) Upper-tail correction on tokens carrying negative learning signal.
neg_low \(A_t < 0,\ b_t < c_{\mathrm{low}}\) Lower-tail correction on tokens carrying negative learning signal.

This decomposition answers whether truncation is symmetric or preferentially reweighting positive or negative learning signal. Persistent concentration in one advantage-sign bucket can create directional bias even when total tis_clipfrac looks modest. “High” and “low” describe the correction ratio—not whether an action or reward is good or bad.

Metric keys — verl: rollout_corr/rollout_is_ratio_fraction_high (fraction of tokens above the IS threshold, equivalent to tis_clipfrac); also rollout_corr/rollout_is_ratio_fraction_low (below lower threshold) · Miles: train/tis_clipfrac (fraction where TIS clip fired), train/tis (mean raw IS ratio before clipping), train/tis_abs (mean |IS ratio − 1|), train/ois (original IS ratio, pre-clip).

The tell: TIS stabilizing \(\rho\) at its step-0 value is a confirmation that the Pearson drop was entropy-collapse amplification of pre-existing TIM, not a new problem introduced by training. The stabilization level reveals the true baseline TIM. Fix that baseline (FP16 precision, matched TP) and the entire loop — runaway drop, TIS constant-correction, gradient bias — resolves at once.

5. IS Ratio Statistics — Direct Off-Policy Quantification

For async correctness, the fundamental per-token quantity is the behavior-correction ratio \(b_t=\pi_{\mathrm{prox}}(a_t|s_t)/\mu(a_t|s_t)\). TIS clip fractions and Pearson are summaries of this mismatch; logging the full distribution directly is more informative. Keep it separate from the PPO ratio \(q_t\), whose distribution answers a different question.

StatisticHealthyWarningWhat it signals
\(\mathbb{E}[b_t]\) ≈ 1.0 Drifting above or below 1 Systematic policy shift direction
\(\max(b_t)\) < 3–4 Spikes > 10 Individual tokens driving gradient explosions
\(\mathrm{Var}(b_t)\) Small and stable Growing over training Accumulating staleness or TIM
\(\mathbb{E}[\log b_t]\) ≈ 0 Consistently negative Policy being suppressed (lower-clip regime dominant)

Log \(b_t\) in log-space where possible — it is approximately symmetric around 0 for on-policy data, making outliers and drift easier to see than in ratio space where the distribution is right-skewed.

Metric keys — verl: rollout_corr/rollout_is_mean, rollout_corr/rollout_is_max, rollout_corr/rollout_is_min, rollout_corr/rollout_is_ratio_fraction_high / …_low (fractions above/below clip thresholds) · Miles: train/tis (mean IS ratio), train/ess_ratio (effective sample size ratio: \(\bigl(\sum w_t\bigr)^2 / \bigl(N \sum w_t^2\bigr)\); near 1.0 = on-policy, near 0 = high variance).

6. Policy Entropy — Collapse and Divergence

For a foundation on what policy entropy is and why it appears as a bonus term in the PPO loss, see PPO §6 — Policy Entropy. The diagnostic interpretation during training is below.

The entropy of the policy at each token position, approximated from sampled log-probs:

\[ H[\pi] \approx -\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}|}\sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \log\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t) \]

(This is a Monte Carlo estimate using only the sampled action, not the full vocabulary distribution. A more precise estimate uses the top-k logits.)

  • Gradually decreasing — expected during learning; the policy becomes more confident about good actions.
  • Rapid collapse to near-zero — reward hacking: the policy found a degenerate response pattern that scores well but has collapsed diversity (e.g., always generating the same short answer).
  • Increasing late in training — catastrophic forgetting or gradient noise overwhelming the policy signal.

Entropy collapse is also what causes the Pearson amplification loop described above — a rapidly falling entropy curve should prompt an immediate check of TIM baseline and tis_clipfrac.

Metric keys — verl: actor/entropy (logged directly by the actor worker each step) · Miles: rollout/entropy (MC estimate from −log π of sampled tokens, averaged over the rollout batch; computed over response tokens using the loss mask); train/entropy_loss (the entropy bonus term \(H[\pi]\) as it enters the loss, only present when entropy_coef ≠ 0).

7. Advantage Statistics — Signal Health

Measured before whitening, so they reflect raw reward signal strength. The diagnostic meaning differs between PPO (critic-estimated baseline) and GRPO (group-mean baseline):

  • \(\mathrm{Var}(A_t)\) before whitening — the spread of raw advantages.
    • PPO: collapse toward zero means the critic has overfit and is predicting near-perfect returns — the policy gradient signal is dead. Cross-check with vf_explained_var → 1.0. Common early warning before reward hacking appears in reward curves.
    • GRPO: collapse toward zero means reward collapse — all outputs in the group received the same score, so z-scored advantages are all near zero and the batch provides no training signal. Root cause is usually prompt difficulty mismatch (all too easy or too hard) or a miscalibrated reward function. Fix: check within-group reward std and adjust prompt difficulty sampling.
  • Fraction of tokens with \(A_t > 0\) vs \(A_t < 0\)
    • PPO: roughly 50% above and below the baseline in a healthy batch. A strong imbalance signals a nearly one-sided reward (ceiling or floor, or miscalibrated KL penalty).
    • GRPO with std-norm (default): always exactly 50/50 by construction — z-scoring forces the group mean to zero. This metric is uninformative in standard GRPO; ignore it.
    • GRPO / Dr.GRPO (std-norm off, norm_adv_by_std_in_grpo: false): fraction positive reflects actual reward difficulty — a majority negative means the policy is mostly wrong on the current prompt distribution.
  • Advantage mean before whitening — should be near zero (GAE is critic-baselined in PPO; z-score mean is exactly zero in standard GRPO). A persistent non-zero mean in PPO signals the critic is systematically off; in GRPO it only arises with std-norm disabled.

PPO only: vf_explained_var — critic quality

\(1 - \mathrm{Var}(R-V)/\mathrm{Var}(R)\): approaches 1.0 when the critic perfectly predicts returns (policy gradient signal is dead); near 0 when the critic is uninformative. This metric does not exist in GRPO runs — there is no critic to overfit. verl key: critic/vf_explained_var; Miles: not logged.

GRPO only: within-group reward std — dead-batch detection

The standard deviation of raw rewards within each group: \(\mathrm{std}(\{r_1,\ldots,r_G\})\) per prompt. Near zero means all completions scored identically — the group provides no contrastive signal and the gradient update is noise. Neither verl nor Miles logs this as a first-class metric; add a custom logger that reads raw rewards before normalization. As a proxy, watch critic/advantages/std (verl) or rollout/advantages (Miles) — when reward diversity collapses, advantage std follows.

Metric keys — verl: critic/advantages/mean, critic/advantages/max, critic/advantages/min; PPO only: critic/vf_explained_var · Miles: rollout/advantages (mean only); rollout/returns.

8. Gradient Norm — Instability Signal

The \(\ell_2\) norm of the gradient across all trainable parameters before the optimizer step. In most frameworks this is logged as grad_norm.

  • Stable with small fluctuations — healthy training.
  • Isolated spike — a single bad batch (high IS ratio, extreme advantage). Usually self-correcting.
  • Sustained rise — policy diverging. Cross-check with clipfrac and IS ratio variance; if both are also rising, the staleness threshold or sync cadence needs tightening.
  • Sudden drop to near zero — gradient vanished. Check for: dead-advantage regime (Var(A_t) → 0), NaN/Inf in the loss that got masked, or all tokens hitting the lower PPO clip.

Metric keys — verl: actor/grad_norm · Miles: train/grad_norm.

9. Response Length — Reward Gaming

Mean and distribution of generated response lengths. Length is not directly optimized but is often gamed as a side-effect of reward signals that are length-correlated (e.g., a reward model trained on human preferences that prefers longer answers). Sudden shifts in length distribution — especially length collapse to very short or runaway length growth — are an early signal that the policy has found a reward shortcut rather than solving the task.

Monitor the full distribution, not just the mean — a bimodal length distribution (some very short, some very long) indicates the policy is splitting into multiple degenerate modes.

Metric keys — verl: response_length/mean, response_length/max, response_length/min, response_length/clip_ratio (fraction of responses hitting the max-length limit — a high clip ratio means many completions are being cut off, which can corrupt reward signals for length-sensitive tasks) · Miles: rollout/response_len/mean, rollout/response_len/median, rollout/response_len/max, rollout/response_len/min; rollout/truncated_ratio (equivalent to verl's clip_ratio).

Policy lag tracking (Miles only) — Miles additionally logs rollout/weight_version/mean, rollout/weight_version/max, rollout/weight_version/min: the weight version number stamped on each sample at generation time. A growing gap between max and mean indicates the buffer contains samples from a wide range of policy versions — the older ones carry the most lag. rollout/weight_version/mixed_version_ratio flags individual samples whose tokens span a weight-sync boundary (partial rollout resumed after sync).

10. Operational Context — MiMo-V2.6 Live Runs

Xiaomi MiMo's September 2026 live RL dashboard, announced by Fuli Luo, is a rare public example of these diagnostics embedded in the control plane of an active frontier-scale run. The announced batch shape is 1,568 prompts × 16 rollouts = 25,088 agent trajectories per step, roughly 2B tokens per step, with generation and training fully asynchronous.

PlanePublic signalsQuestion answered
Optimization dynsam/avg@n, reward, entropy, policy-gradient loss, gradient norm, PPO and TIS clip fractions Is the batch producing useful signal, and is the update stable?
Off-policy correctness partial/avg_staleness, train_infer_diff/new_infer/kl How old are admitted samples, and how far do trainer and inference probabilities differ?
Sampler and mixture all-fail/all-pass fractions, pass-rate histogram, measurable prompts, per-source accepted/target counts, per-harness training rollouts Which task and harness distributions actually reached the trainer?
Capacity and reliability active sandboxes, infrastructure-error rate, accepted/judged/remaining work, partial rollouts, grader backlog, prewarm count, restart and redo events Is a curve movement caused by learning, queue state, environment capacity, or an operational incident?
External evaluation DeepSWE v1.1 with mini-swe-agent, avg@3, attached to selected steps Does an asynchronously evaluated checkpoint improve on a held-out agent benchmark?

The live data also separates the two clipping layers empirically. As observed on September 17, both MiMo runs report:

  • actor/pg_clipfrac = 0 and actor/ppo_kl = 0 at every completed step, globally and for inspected datasets such as code/dataset-4onq;
  • actor/updated_iter = 1 per step;
  • non-zero partial/avg_staleness and train_infer_diff/new_infer/kl;
  • tiny but non-zero pg_tis_clipfrac, partitioned into {pos,neg}_{high,low} buckets.

This is strongly consistent with one proximal actor iteration per fresh training batch: \(q_t\) is still 1 when measured, so PPO clipping never activates, while \(b_t\) still records asynchronous rollout lag and train–inference mismatch. The policy-gradient loss remains non-zero because advantages can drive an update without invoking the clipping branch. Xiaomi has not published the optimizer code, so treat this as the explanation best supported by the telemetry rather than a confirmed implementation detail. One UI detail follows directly: selecting a logarithmic y-axis makes the all-zero pg_clipfrac series appear empty, because zero is not representable on a log scale.

The important design lesson is that an async-RL dashboard needs causal context, not only optimizer curves. MiMo publishes operator notices for trainer restarts, cluster-to-grader connectivity, VRAM failures and bad rollout data; it also marks redone steps. Without those annotations, a discontinuity in reward, entropy or latency can be misread as an algorithmic event. Dataset and harness slices serve the same purpose: a stable global mean can hide one source saturating, one harness failing, or a mixture-weight shift.

Evidence boundary. The dashboard's aggregate trainer–inference KL is useful, but it cannot replace the ratio distribution, upper/lower clip decomposition, Pearson correlation or per-version lag distribution described above; a small mean can hide a damaging tail. The page is also an operational trace, not a controlled study: the dynamic sampler, reward normalization and announced “agentic in-group credit assignment” have not yet been specified. Treat the telemetry as evidence for what to observe, not yet as a reproducible training recipe.

Decoupling the Trainer from the Agent Graph

When the harness owns the loop and the trainer only sees LLM calls: the token-in/token-out seam, the five-field payload that must flow back (prompt_ids, response_ids, loss_mask, rollout_logprobs, reward), how proxies stitch calls into training samples, why the proxy's logprobs become the behavior policy, and where verl Uni-Agent, AReaL 2.0, slime, SkyRL, PRIME-RL, Polar, Agent Lightning and Tinker each sit.

Fourth page of the Agentic RL series. It generalises §10–§11 of Agentic RL Training in verl (which introduced the "port your agent vs. wrap your agent" question and the token-prefix predicate) across the whole 2026 framework landscape. It assumes agentic RL, the importance-sampling correction math, and the token-in/token-out argument. State of the world as of August 2026; the field moves monthly, so treat version numbers as dated.

1. The Seam: Who Owns the Loop?

A multi-turn RL rollout has two loops nested inside each other. The outer loop is the trainer's: sample tasks, generate trajectories, score, compute advantages, update, sync weights. The inner loop is the agent's: build a prompt, call the model, parse the output, run tools, append observations, decide whether to stop. For single-turn RLHF the inner loop is one call and nobody notices it exists. For a coding agent it is the product — the system prompt, the tool schema, the context-compaction policy, the retry logic, the subagent fan-out. Cursor, Claude Code, Codex, OpenHands, mini-SWE-agent: each is an inner loop that someone spent a year tuning.

The question this page is about is where the inner loop lives. Two answers:

Trainer owns the agent graphHarness owns the agent graph
Shape The agent is re-implemented inside the trainer's rollout worker (verl AgentLoopBase, slime custom-generate fn, OpenRLHF AgentExecutorBase). It calls the engine with token ids and builds the training sample as it goes. The agent runs unchanged, as a process that speaks an OpenAI/Anthropic-compatible API. A proxy sits between it and the inference engine, records every call, and reconstructs training samples afterwards.
What you get Exact tokens and masks for free — the loop is the tokenizer. Harness parity: you train the policy that you ship, including every quirk of its prompt and tool handling. The harness is swappable.
What you pay A second copy of the agent that drifts from production; every harness feature (compaction, subagents, streaming tool calls) must be re-ported. The trainer no longer sees the trajectory — only a bag of request/response pairs. Recovering tokens, masks, logprobs and the causal chain between calls becomes the central engineering problem.

How much the harness itself matters is now measured, not argued. OpenForgeRL (Jul 2026) ran the first cross-harness empirical study — the same base model trained through four harnesses on the same tasks — and the harness moves everything: ReACT and ZeroClaw, the two that let custom tools be added directly, reach 45.1 and 48.5 ClawEval pass@1 after SFT+RL, while OpenClaw and Codex land at 20.9 and 32.5 from far weaker bases. Training in one harness (ZeroClaw) transfers to harnesses never seen in training (+3.3 on OpenClaw, +4.6 on Codex), and multi-harness training does best of all (+20.3 over base on Codex). The harness is not plumbing around the policy — it is a training variable with its own learnability, which is the empirical case for training through the harness you ship rather than a trainer-side reimplementation, and for paying §5's bookkeeping costs to do it.

Microsoft's Agent Lightning v1.0 paper (August 2026) gives the second shape its name — harnessed agentic RL — and states the consequence precisely:

"In harnessed agentic RL, the harness owns this loop, while the training engine observes only a sequence of LLM request-response pairs. How to model and assemble these calls into training samples remains an open question."

The rest of this page is about that open question, and the one constraint that every surviving design shares: decoupling does not mean the interface becomes text. The harness talks text to the proxy, but what crosses from the proxy to the trainer is still tokens — plus masks, plus the logprobs the engine assigned when it sampled them, plus the reward. §3 is why; §4 is the exact contract.

2. Three Generations of Interface

Read the open-source frameworks in release order and three interface generations fall out. They coexist — most frameworks now ship all three — but each was a response to the last one's limitation.

Gen 1 — the trainer-owned token loop (2025)

A user coroutine runs inside the trainer's rollout worker and calls the engine directly with input_ids: verl's AgentLoopBase.run() (v0.5, July 2025), slime's --custom-generate-function-path against SGLang's /generate, OpenRLHF's AgentExecutorBase loaded inside the vLLM actor, NeMo-RL's run_multi_turn_rollout, ROLL's TrajEnvManager, RL2. Tool and observation text is tokenized by the loop and appended with loss_mask=0, logprob=0.0. Tinker belongs here too, even though its loop runs on the client: its Env.step(action: list[int]) is token-typed by construction, and the Datum it sends to forward_backward carries target_tokens, logprobs, and advantages (zero = masked). This generation gets tokenization exactly right and harness parity exactly wrong — it is verl §10's first row.

Gen 2 — OpenAI-compatible chat with token capture (late 2025)

The agent is ordinary application code holding an openai.OpenAI(base_url=…) client, but the endpoint is the trainer's engine and returns prompt_token_ids and per-choice token_ids alongside the text — vLLM's return_token_ids (merged for v0.10.2, PR #22587, with the Agent Lightning team as the motivating user). The trainer-side adapter groups calls by session and stitches consecutive calls whose prompt ids are an exact prefix extension of the last call. SkyRL's step-wise mode, rLLM's model gateway, ART's httpx hooks, Trinity-RFT's patched client, ROLL's ProxyEnvManager and NeMo Gym all work this way. The agent code is untouched; the transport is trainer-aware.

Gen 3 — the black-box harness behind a proxy (2026)

The 2026 wave drops even the requirement that the agent be Python you can import. A proxy process exposes OpenAI Chat, OpenAI Responses and Anthropic Messages dialects; the harness is launched with OPENAI_BASE_URL / ANTHROPIC_BASE_URL pointed at it; the proxy normalises the request, forwards it to the engine with token-id and logprob capture on, and records (prompt_ids, response_ids, logprobs) keyed by a rollout id embedded in the URL path or bearer token. Claude Code, Codex CLI, Gemini CLI, Pi, OpenHands and mini-SWE-agent have all been trained this way in public work. The entries: verl's Uni-Agent Gateway (v0.8–0.9, "request string in, training tokens out", 1,000+ concurrent stateful sessions), AReaL 2.0's agent service (rollout.agent.mode=online — "the agent code lives outside of AReaL"), slime v0.3's AnthropicAdapter/OpenAIAdapter plus TrajectoryManager, Miles' TITO session server, NVIDIA's Polar (May 2026, the ProRL-Agent-Server), Prime Intellect's verifiers v1 interception server, Microsoft's Agent Lightning v1.0 and OpenForgeRL. Binfeng Xu, Polar's author, states the design rule: "the training loop and the agent loop have to live in different processes, talk through a stable interface."

Agent Lightning separates execution control, event capture, and learning. The Customized Trainer declares work by creating rollout records in the API Gateway. The Rollout Controller reconciles those records into local subprocesses or Kubernetes Jobs and injects rollout-specific Gateway URLs. The external harness still owns the agent loop; its model calls pass through the Gateway to trainer-hosted vLLM, while exact token IDs, logprobs, rewards, and custom events are stored by rollout ID. After completion, the trainer fetches those events and performs merging, masking, advantage calculation, and optimization. The Gateway captures evidence; it does not construct the training sample.

Agent Lightning's control plane: controller, Gateway, and trainer

A rollout is one agent execution on one input. A rollout record is the trainer's declarative request for that execution—not a model call or an already-built training sample. A rollout controller is the control-plane service that turns a declared rollout into a running harness process. In Agent Lightning v1.0, for example, the trainer creates rollout records in the Gateway; the controller reconciles the queued records, starts a local subprocess or Kubernetes Job with rollout-specific proxy and event URLs, enforces execution capacity and timeouts, and drives status from QUEUING through RUNNING to SUCCEEDED or FAILED. It can retry reconciliation after a process, watch, or network interruption. It does not sample tokens, execute tools, compute rewards, or update weights.

The ownership rule
The harness owns the inner agent loop; the rollout controller owns the outer episode lifecycle; the Gateway owns shared rollout state and captured events; trainer-hosted vLLM generates tokens; the trainer turns completed events into samples and gradient updates.

“Rollouter” has no universal component boundary. Architecturally, people often use it for the whole rollout-producing subsystem, which can include a controller, agent runners, inference engines, and trace collection. Under that broad usage, yes, the controller and inference engine are both parts of the rollouter, but they remain separate control-plane and data-plane services. A framework class actually named Rollouter may be narrower: verl's experimental fully-async design, for example, lists Rollouter, MessageQueue, Trainer, ParameterSynchronizer, and its resource controller as separate components. Agent Lightning does not define one encompassing Rollouter; it exposes the Rollout Controller, API Gateway, and Customized Trainer as three peer components, with vLLM hosted by the trainer.

The pattern
Each generation moved the harness boundary one step further from the trainer — from a coroutine the trainer calls, to a client the trainer's engine serves, to a process the trainer never imports. What never moved is the trainer's input format. Token ids, masks and sampling-time logprobs are captured at whichever boundary the design puts closest to the engine, because that is the only place they exist.

3. Why Text Cannot Cross the Seam

The tempting version of decoupling is "the harness sends messages, the trainer re-renders them through the chat template and tokenizes." It is what most harnesses do by default and SkyRL's docs rank it first as "simplest — works almost out of the box." It is also wrong in four independent ways, each now documented with a primary source. The verl page made the BPE-seam argument \(T(a\Vert b) \neq T(a)\Vert T(b)\); Agent Lightning v1.0 and the Hugging Face TITO post (May 2026) decompose it further.

MechanismWhat happensCanonical example
1. Decoding is not injective Many token sequences decode to one string. Retokenizing the string need not return the sampled sequence. The model samples h+aving; re-encoding the same text inside a later prompt yields hav+ing. The trainer then evaluates \(\log\pi_\theta\) on tokens the policy never produced.
2. Chat templates don't compose \(\mathrm{Template}(A\Vert B) \neq \mathrm{Template}(A)\Vert\mathrm{Template}(B)\). Re-rendering the full history rewrites earlier turns. Qwen templates strip <think> blocks from previous assistant turns. The trajectory the model actually sampled is gone from the training prompt. Prime Intellect: this "destroys the actual trajectory the model sampled."
3. Outputs are transformed at inference time Tool-call parsers "parse, normalize, repair, and reserialize" the sampled response before it is stored in the message history. JSON whitespace and key ordering change; false becomes False; malformed JSON is auto-repaired. The text itself now differs from what was sampled, so even a perfect tokenizer cannot recover it.
4. The harness rewrites history Compaction, summarisation, subagent fan-out, and reasoning-stripping produce a prompt that is not an extension of any earlier call. Claude Code compacts context at a threshold; the post-compaction prompt shares no token prefix with the pre-compaction one. This is a legitimate behaviour of the harness, not a bug, and the stitcher must handle it.

Why this matters beyond hygiene: the whole PPO/GRPO machinery evaluates \(\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)\) on the sampled tokens. If the trainer's \(a_t\) is not the engine's \(a_t\), the ratio is meaningless; if \(s_t\) has been rewritten, the policy is being updated on a context it never saw. And — the point SkyRL's docs make explicitly — you "cannot do rollout correction like TIS reliably, so you cannot do fully async training with proper staleness correction." Text-level decoupling silently forbids the async designs of the async RL page.

The consensus statement. Prime Intellect's renderers post: "For RL, the inference server should be a simple token-only endpoint." Xu: "anything that round-trips through text decode/encode will silently corrupt the gradient." Hugging Face's TITO: keep "a running buffer of sampled tokens that never gets re-tokenized." The only template property TITO actually needs is prefix-preservation for tool messages — render the history with and without the tool result, take the delta, and append it by id concatenation; 18 of 19 model families tested already satisfy it (Qwen3 needed a one-line Jinja fix).

4. The Payload Contract

Strip away the framework-specific names and every design in §2 moves the same record from the rollout side to the trainer. Calling it a contract is not a stretch — the field lists of verl's AgentLoopOutput, SkyRL's GeneratorOutput, slime's Sample, AReaL's batch dict, Polar's Trace, PRIME-RL's TrainingSample, Agent Lightning's model_request event and Tinker's Datum are one-to-one up to naming.

Type notation: \(\mathbb{Z}\) the integers (token ids), \(\mathbb{R}\) the reals (log-probs), \(\{0,1\}\) bits; the superscript is a sequence length — \(L_p\) prompt tokens, \(L_r\) response tokens — not an exponent. See math notation §9.

FieldTypeWho produces itWhat the trainer does with it
prompt_ids \(\mathbb{Z}^{L_p}\) Engine, after chat-template processing (vLLM's prompt_token_ids); never the trainer's tokenizer. Conditioning context; no loss. Also the thing checked for prefix continuity across calls (§5).
response_ids \(\mathbb{Z}^{L_r}\) Engine's sampled ids, verbatim. The action sequence \(a_{1:L_r}\); the only tokens whose \(\log\pi_\theta\) enters the loss.
loss_mask \(\{0,1\}^{L_r}\) (or over \(L_p{+}L_r\)) Stitcher: 1 on model-sampled spans, 0 on tool outputs, template glue, boundary tokens, and anything that arrived by re-tokenization. Multiplies the per-token loss. Tinker encodes it as advantages=0 rather than a separate mask — same effect.
rollout_logprobs \(\mathbb{R}^{L_r}\) Engine, at sampling time, under the actual sampling distribution (verl: logprobs_mode=processed_logprobs so temperature/top-p are reflected). 0.0 where mask is 0. Becomes \(\log\mu(a_t\mid s_t)\), the behavior policy, in every off-policy correction (§7). Tinker's RL losses refuse a Datum without it.
reward scalar, or per-step / per-token Env / verifier / harness, delivered via a side channel (AReaL /rl/set_reward, Agent Lightning spans, verl reward_score on the last token). Advantage computation — and the granularity question of §6.
weight_version \(\mathbb{Z}\) per sample or per segment Engine / proxy stamps the policy version that produced each span (AReaL versions[B,T], slime weight_versions). Staleness gating and per-segment IS when a rollout spans a weight update (§8).
routed_experts ids per token per layer Engine (MoE only). Rollout routing replay (R3) so the trainer's forward reuses the engine's expert choices — eliminates the largest MoE mismatch source.

Two things the contract deliberately excludes. It never carries the message list — the trainer does not know or care what the system prompt said. And it never carries the trainer-side \(\log\pi_{\text{old}}\): that is either recomputed by the trainer (the classic three-policy form) or replaced by rollout_logprobs (the "bypass" form), but it is not something the harness side can supply.

5. Stitching Calls into Samples

A gen-1 loop produces one training sample per trajectory because it builds the tape incrementally. A proxy produces \(N\) independent (prompt_ids, response_ids) records for an \(N\)-call trajectory and has to decide how many training samples those become. The naive answers bracket the design space: one sample per call is always correct but costs \(O(T^2)\) tokens of forward/backward for a \(T\)-turn trajectory (every call re-includes the full history as prompt); one sample per trajectory is \(O(T)\) but only valid if call \(n\)'s prompt ids are literally prompt_{n-1} ‖ response_{n-1} ‖ tool_n.

The test is the token-prefix predicate from the verl page, now applied between calls rather than turns. Write \(p_n\) for the prompt ids of call \(n\), \(r_n\) for its sampled response ids, and \(a \Vert b\) for concatenation. Call \(n\) extends call \(n-1\) when everything the model saw and said last time reappears, token for token, at the start of the new prompt:

\[ p_{n-1}\,\Vert\,r_{n-1}\;\text{ is a prefix of }\;p_n \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad p_n[\,:\,|p_{n-1}| + |r_{n-1}|\,] \;=\; p_{n-1}\,\Vert\,r_{n-1} \] 
prev = prompt_ids[n-1] + response_ids[n-1]      # what the engine saw + what it sampled
extends = prompt_ids[n][:len(prev)] == prev       # exact id-for-id match, no decode/encode
tail    = prompt_ids[n][len(prev):]               # if extends: the new tool/template tokens

When it holds, the calls can be merged and the \(\texttt{tail}\) between the old response and the new response is tool/template tokens with mask 0. When it fails — any of §3's four mechanisms — the frameworks split into two camps, which Agent Lightning v1.0 is the first to name side by side:

StrategyMechanismCostWho
Per-call Every call is its own sample; no merging. Always correct; \(O(T^2)\). SkyRL step-wise (mitigated by merge_stepwise_output), AReaL export_style: individual (tree-deduplicated), Trinity-RFT, OpenForgeRL (reconstruction at rollout completion)
Buffer-and-replace The proxy keeps a buffer of what it sampled. When the harness sends a prompt whose text contains an earlier response, the proxy overwrites that span of prompt_ids with the buffered sampled ids before forwarding, so continuity is restored by force. \(O(T)\); but the model is now conditioned on a prompt the harness never sent — "training it under the stitched prompt therefore introduces an off-policy discrepancy" (AL v1.0). Assumes linear history. AReaL concat, verl Continuous Token (v0.9), NeMo-RL replace_prefix_tokens, Miles TITO session server
Best-effort merge, fork on break Merge only when the predicate holds exactly. On failure, close the current sample and open a new one whose prompt is frozen (mask 0) context. \(O(T)\) when templates behave, degrading toward \(O(T^2)\) when they don't; never changes what the harness saw. slime TrajectoryManager (CLEAN / REALIGN / FORK), Polar prefix_merging, PRIME-RL MessageNode graph ("fork at the first divergence"), Tinker has_extension_property, Agent Lightning v1.0 (ids_startswith), ROLL MessageTracker hash-chain
Tree-structured Train on the shared-prefix tree directly with a custom attention mask. Optimal tokens; needs non-standard kernels. Discussed, not shipped. AL v1.0 (analysis only)

How often does the predicate break in practice? Often enough to design around: in AL v1.0's coding-agent run (mini-SWE-agent, Qwen3.5-9B) only 36% of rollouts survive as a single merged row, and the mean is 2.41 training samples per rollout. Two bookkeeping rules follow. Every flattened row must keep its rollout id and prompt-group id, so a rollout gains no statistical weight from having more rows (§6); and all rows of one rollout must land in the same optimizer update — splitting them across updates evaluates parts of one rollout under different policy versions, a within-rollout staleness that the §7 correction never sees.

OpenForgeRL marks the crude-but-safe end of the same design space, with two deliberately simple answers. Reconstruction is deferred to rollout completion: the proxy records each call's prompt–response pair and only assembles training samples when the container returns the terminal reward — one sample per call, each assigned \(r_t = \gamma^{T-t}\, r_T\) with \(\gamma = 1.0\), the terminal reward broadcast undiscounted to every step. And credit assignment on failures is by exclusion: following DAPO, all samples from a trajectory that ended in an error (wall-clock timeout included) are discarded, "since a partial rollout can inject misleading training signal." Where Agent Lightning merges best-effort and keeps what it can, OpenForgeRL refuses to train on anything a broken rollout produced — cheap and never wrong, at the price of the \(O(T)\) savings merging buys and of any signal the failures carried.

The strategies differ only when the predicate breaks. On a clean history all three token-faithful stitchers produce one \(O(T)\) sample. Under drift, per-call pays \(O(T^2)\) for safety, buffer-and-replace keeps one sample by conditioning on a prompt the harness never sent, and fork-on-break pays extra prompt tokens only from the break onward. Text re-tokenisation produces one tidy sample whose "sampled" tokens are partly fiction — the one outcome you cannot detect from the sample alone.

6. Advantage Granularity

Once one rollout can become a variable number of samples, GRPO's group baseline has to be defined somewhere. Two choices are in production:

  • Rollout-level — compute \(\hat A\) over the \(G\) rollouts of a task exactly as in single-turn GRPO, then broadcast each rollout's advantage to all the samples it was split into. verl Uni-Agent, Polar and Agent Lightning do this. AL v1.0's argument: "Retokenization is an incidental phenomenon, and advantage assignment should not change simply because retokenization happened to split a rollout into more samples."
  • Sample-level — treat each training sample as a group member, so a rollout that forked into three samples contributes three entries to the baseline. slime and AReaL do this. It is simpler to implement (the trainer never needs to know which samples share a rollout) and it coincides with rollout-level when no forking happens — but it reweights tasks toward whichever rollouts happened to fork more.

Related and equally unsettled: how a scalar trajectory reward becomes per-sample signal. Most proxies broadcast the terminal reward to every sample of the rollout (AReaL adds a turn_discount); OpenRLHF sums step rewards into one scalar; SkyRL and Tinker allow per-step rewards from the env. The agentic RL page covers why credit assignment across long horizons is hard in principle; here the difficulty is purely bookkeeping, but it changes the gradient.

Loss normalisation under dynamic sample counts

The baseline is half of the story; the other half is how per-token losses are averaged once a batch of \(R\) rollouts contains a variable number of rows. Write \(N_\rho\) for the samples of rollout \(\rho\), \(L_{\rho,j}\) for the response tokens of sample \(j\), and \(\ell_{\rho,j,t}\) for the per-token loss. Three normalisers are in production:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal L_{\text{token-mean}} &= \frac{\sum_{\rho}\sum_{j}\sum_{t}\ell_{\rho,j,t}}{\sum_{\rho}\sum_{j}L_{\rho,j}} &&\text{DAPO; verl } \texttt{loss\_agg\_mode: token-mean}\\[4pt] \mathcal L_{\text{seq-mean}} &= \frac{1}{\sum_{\rho}N_\rho}\sum_{\rho}\sum_{j}\frac{1}{L_{\rho,j}}\sum_{t}\ell_{\rho,j,t} &&\text{original GRPO (seq-mean-token-mean)}\\[4pt] \mathcal L_{\text{rollout-mean}} &= \frac{1}{R}\sum_{\rho}\frac{\sum_{j}\sum_{t}\ell_{\rho,j,t}}{\sum_{j}L_{\rho,j}} &&\text{slime; rollout-level token-mean} \end{aligned} \]

AL v1.0's worked example makes the difference tangible: rollout A splits into two rows of 50 and 100 tokens, B into three rows of 30, C stays one row of 40. Seq-mean hands B half the batch's weight (three of six rows) for an accident of forking; token-mean weights by tokens (A gets 150 of 280); rollout-mean gives each rollout exactly one third. By the same principle as the advantage — sample count is incidental — seq-mean is the wrong choice here. Token-mean is principled, but in AL's runs it turned unstable late in training whenever a batch filled with long negative samples, so rollout-level token-mean is their default.

The ablation is the one result in the paper that the framing does not predict. Same GRPO objective, same coding setup (Qwen3.5-9B, mini-SWE-agent, SWE-smith), three settings:

SettingAdvantageLossBest val rewardPolicy entropy
Baselinesample-leveltoken-mean35.0%—
Advantage fix onlyrollout-leveltoken-mean33.1% (worse)grows fastest
Both fixesrollout-levelrollout-level token-mean38.2% (step 128)slowest, most stable
The fixes are a pair. Correcting the baseline alone hurts: the corrected advantages push entropy up and token-mean does nothing to contain it. Adding the rollout-level normaliser is what "controls the entropy increase introduced by the corrected rollout advantages while improving validation reward." The both-fixes checkpoint is the one reported at 41.8% → 56.4% on SWE-bench Verified (step 208). If a framework only lets you move one of the two knobs, moving neither may be safer than moving the baseline alone.

7. The Proxy's Logprobs Are the Behavior Policy

The async RL page treats the rollout-engine-vs-trainer logprob gap as an engine numerics problem: vLLM and FSDP compute different \(\log\pi\) on the same weights because of kernel, batch-size and TP accumulation order, and the fix is truncated importance sampling with the engine's logprob in the denominator (Yao et al., "Your Efficient RL Framework Secretly Brings You Off-Policy RL Training"). Harnessed RL adds two more reasons the sampled distribution is not the trained one:

  1. Stitching is itself off-policy. Buffer-and-replace conditions on a rewritten prompt; forked samples are trained with a frozen prompt that may contain retokenised history. Either way \(s_t^{\text{train}} \neq s_t^{\text{sample}}\) for some tokens.
  2. Staleness is per-call, not per-trajectory. A coding agent's 200 calls span tens of minutes; with in-flight weight updates, calls 1–80 came from \(\theta_{k}\) and 81–200 from \(\theta_{k+1}\). The trajectory has no single \(\pi_{\text{old}}\).

Both are handled by the same move: stop pretending \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) is something the trainer recomputes, and use the logprobs the engine returned when it sampled. Write the three policies explicitly — \(\mu\) the behavior policy (engine, at sampling time), \(\pi_{\text{prox}}\) the proximal policy (trainer's recompute at the start of the update, if any), and \(\pi_\theta\) the policy being updated:

\[ \underbrace{\frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t\mid s_t)}}_{\text{PPO ratio, clipped}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\min\!\Big(\frac{\pi_{\text{prox}}(a_t\mid s_t)}{\mu(a_t\mid s_t)},\,C\Big)}_{\text{behavior correction, stop-grad}} \;\cdot\; \hat A_t \;\cdot\; m_t \]

with \(m_t\) the loss mask. Every framework in §9 now exposes this in one of two forms:

The bypass form is what makes harnessed RL affordable: a trajectory of 200 calls does not need a 200-call recompute of \(\pi_{\text{old}}\) before the update. It is only sound because the proxy captured \(\mu\) per token — and it is exactly the thing SkyRL's docs say is lost under re-tokenisation. A 2026 paper, "Missing Old Logits in Asynchronous Agentic RL" (arXiv 2605.12070), formalises the failure: async pipelines that drop the sampling-time logits end up entangling mismatch repair with staleness correction and have no clean estimator for either.

The zero-mismatch alternative. Miles' --true-on-policy-mode makes SGLang's and Megatron's logprobs bitwise equal via batch-invariant kernels, so \(\mu \equiv \pi_{\text{prox}}\) and the correction term is identically 1. It is currently Qwen3-only and incompatible with fully-async training, which is why it eliminates the numerics source but not the stitching or staleness sources above. See TIM fix families.

8. Async, Weight Versions, and Pausing a Harness You Don't Control

The verl async page describes staleness gating, partial rollouts and the checkpoint-engine weight sync for a trainer that owns its rollout workers. A black-box harness changes three things:

  • You cannot pause the agent, only the engine. SkyRL states it plainly: "the in-flight weight update is implemented by the inference engines, not the generator / agent harness … the /chat/completions endpoint is simply blocked and not returning." Polar exposes /admin/inference/pause|resume; Agent Lightning's gateway drains with an X-Agl-Paused header; SkyRL's PauseMode keep resumes in-flight requests "from the same token position with the new policy." The harness sees a slow API call and nothing else.
  • Versions are per segment. Because a single call can straddle an update under keep-style resumption, AReaL stamps versions[B,T] per token and slime per sample; "each segment must be attributed to the version that actually generated it." This is what the §7 correction consumes.
  • Admission control replaces batch size. AReaL's StalenessManager grants capacity via /grant_capacity and answers HTTP 429 when too many rollouts would be more than max_head_offpolicyness versions behind; SkyRL's AsyncStalenessManager caps in-flight work at \((S + \text{step}) \times B\); PRIME-RL fixed the trainer at exactly one step ahead of inference and bounds max_off_policy_steps (default 8). The knob has moved from "how many rollouts per step" to "how stale may a rollout be when it lands."
  • Retries need an idempotent control plane. Two network hops can fail and be retried — trainer/controller → gateway, and harness → proxy. AL v1.0 makes every rollout-API endpoint idempotent so control-plane retries are free; a retried LLM call, though, is a fresh sample, so its trainer drops earlier model_request events that share an identical prompt and keeps only the last. Correct for transport retries; wrong for a harness that deliberately re-sends the same prompt (a parse-failure retry whose first response it did see). Know which of the two your harness does before adopting the rule.

Weight sync itself is unchanged by the harness — NCCL broadcast, CUDA-IPC, NIXL, or disk-delta (verl delta_sharded, slime --update-weight-mode delta, Miles' RDMA p2p path that took Kimi-K2's 1T sync "from 53.3 s to 7.2 s") — because the proxy is not a party to it. That is the one part of the loop the decoupling leaves alone.

10. How to Place — or Build — Anything New

Five questions classify any trainer–harness design, in the order they constrain each other:

  1. Where is the boundary? Coroutine in the trainer (gen 1), client of the trainer's engine (gen 2), or process behind a proxy (gen 3). Harness parity requires gen 3; gen 3 requires answers to the next four.
  2. Where are token ids captured? At the engine (vLLM return_token_ids, SGLang /generate meta_info) or at a token-only endpoint the client renders for (Tinker, PRIME-RL renderers). If the answer is "the trainer re-tokenises," the design is text-level and §3 applies in full — including losing TIS.
  3. What happens when the prefix predicate breaks? Per-call, buffer-and-replace, or fork-on-break (§5). The honest default for an arbitrary harness is fork-on-break: it never rewrites what the harness saw and it degrades gracefully. Buffer-and-replace is the right choice when you control the harness and know its history is linear.
  4. Where is the advantage baseline? Rollout-level unless you have a reason (§6). If the framework only offers sample-level, expect task reweighting under heavy forking.
  5. Which policy is \(\pi_{\text{old}}\)? If sampling-time logprobs are in the payload, bypass mode is available and the 200-call recompute disappears (§7). If they are not, the design is limited to synchronous, near-on-policy training.
One-sentence summary
Decoupling the trainer from the agent graph moves the harness out of the trainer but never moves the tokens out of the payload: every surviving design captures (prompt_ids, response_ids, loss_mask, rollout_logprobs, reward) at the engine boundary, stitches calls into samples by a strict token-prefix test, and uses the captured logprobs as the behavior policy — which is what lets the same trainer accept any harness and still train off-policy safely.

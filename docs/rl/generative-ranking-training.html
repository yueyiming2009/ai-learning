Generative Ranking RL

One request, six candidates, three positions, four rollouts. Follow the probabilities, rewards, masks, and gradients that turn a generated ranking into a policy update.

Start with: RL for Generative Recommendation — Concepts & Landscape. For the general algorithm, see GRPO; for distributed execution, see the verl training walkthrough.

This is a worked teaching example, not a reproduction of an industrial system. We rerank a fixed candidate set, use a known toy reward, and expose a six-parameter policy so every update can be checked. A production transformer would condition its logits on rich user context and the full output prefix.

2. The supervised starting point and the masked policy

A real system first learns a useful generator from logged interactions or curated slates, usually with teacher-forced next-token prediction. Context tokens do not receive the output loss. A fixed three-item output here needs neither padding nor an end token; variable-length production outputs need explicit termination and loss masks.

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{SFT}}=-\sum_{t=1}^{3}\log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid x,a_{<t},C). \]

Let \(V_t=C\setminus\{a_1,\ldots,a_{t-1}\}\) be the remaining eligible items. Sampling is without replacement:

\[ p_\theta(j\mid h_t)= \begin{cases} \displaystyle\frac{\exp z_\theta(h_t,j)}{\sum_{k\in V_t}\exp z_\theta(h_t,k)},&j\in V_t,\\ 0,&j\notin V_t, \end{cases} \qquad \pi_\theta(S\mid x,C)=\prod_{t=1}^{3}p_\theta(a_t\mid h_t). \]

In the explorer only, \(z_\theta(h_t,j)=z_j\): six shared item logits, with prefix dependence only through the no-repeat mask. We start all logits at zero to make the arithmetic transparent. This uniform initialization is not a recommendation to train a production policy from scratch, and the toy policy cannot represent every possible context-dependent slate preference.

Mask first, normalize second, log third. The rollout log-probability must be the probability that actually generated the token. Logging an unmasked softmax, or ignoring temperature/top-k changes, gives the wrong training ratio. The example uses temperature 1 and no top-k truncation. A rollout engine and learner must agree on their sampling distribution and its support.

3. Four rollouts become a batch

There are \(6\cdot5\cdot4=120\) distinct ordered slates. With uniform logits, the conditional probabilities are \(1/6,1/5,1/4\), so every slate has probability \(1/120\), and sequence log-probability \(-\log120\approx-4.78749\).

Use the fixed group ABC, ADE, BDF, CEF as one illustrative possible realization of four independent samples from that policy. This group is deliberately held fixed so updates are reproducible; it is not an exhaustive or representative sample of the 120 slates. Sampling without replacement is within each slate, not across rollouts.

For multiple requests, compute advantages within each request's group before flattening; do not compare rewards across unrelated users. With semantic IDs, an item may occupy several tokens, so the output length and token-level validity masks change. Multiple code sequences resolving to the same item also require item-level deduplication.

4. Score a slate, not a fictional user response

Define position weights \(w=(1,0.8,0.6)\), item relevance \(v\), and the fraction of same-category pairs \(D(S)\). Our hidden-in-practice but known-in-this-demo utility is:

\[ B(S)=\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{3}w_t v_{a_t}}{2.4},\qquad D(S)=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{t<u}\mathbf1[c(a_t)=c(a_u)],\qquad U(S)=B(S)-0.2D(S). \]

The default reward model predicts \(\hat R=U\) exactly, an intentionally optimistic teaching case. The biased mode instead predicts \(B+0.4D\): it rewards redundant Tech items. The flat mode gives every output 0.5. These are three explicit synthetic functions, not fitted production models.

5. One GRPO-style update, with the policies kept distinct

PolicyRoleFrozen during these updates?
\(\pi_{\mathrm{old}}\)Generated this rollout group; denominator of the update ratioYes
\(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\)Behavior to stay near, often the SFT checkpoint; target of the KL penaltyYes
\(\pi_\theta\)Current trainable policyNo

They happen to be identical at initialization here, but their roles differ. Neither frozen policy is automatically the production logging policy that selected a historical exposure. See behavior policies and importance sampling.

Group-relative advantages

\[ \bar R=\frac{1}{G}\sum_iR_i,\quad s=\sqrt{\frac1G\sum_i(R_i-\bar R)^2},\quad A_i=\frac{R_i-\bar R}{s+10^{-8}},\quad G=4. \]

We use the population standard deviation over this group and stop gradients through rewards and advantages. Each of a slate's three tokens receives the same \(A_i\). This is outcome-level credit, not evidence that each token was individually beneficial. The normalization defines this variant's update; it should not be mistaken for an unbiased estimator of every possible reward objective.

For the default group, \(\bar R=0.78375\), \(s\approx0.049239\), and the advantages for ABC, ADE, BDF, CEF are approximately −1.5316, +1.1593, +0.5331, −0.1608. ABC has the highest independent relevance but the lowest slate utility because all three items share a category.

Token ratios, clipping, and exact conditional KL

\[ \rho_{i,t}=\exp(\log p_\theta(a_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})-\log p_{\mathrm{old}}(a_{i,t}\mid h_{i,t})), \]\[ J=\frac{1}{GL}\sum_{i,t}\left[ \min\left(\rho_{i,t}A_i,\operatorname{clip}(\rho_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A_i\right) -\beta K_{i,t}\right],\qquad L=3, \]\[ K_{i,t}=\sum_{j\in V_{i,t}}p_\theta(j\mid h_{i,t}) \log\frac{p_\theta(j\mid h_{i,t})}{p_{\mathrm{ref}}(j\mid h_{i,t})}. \]

The demo computes this six-action KL exactly at the sampled prefixes. This is a tractable pedagogical alternative to sampled token-KL estimators, not a claim to reproduce a particular GRPO library. Averaging over these old-policy prefixes is not the same as exactly computing full-sequence KL under the new policy.

At an active, unclipped token, the reward gradient with respect to any allowed logit is \(\rho A(\mathbf1[j=a]-p_j)\). The conditional KL gradient is \(p_j(\log(p_j/q_j)-K)\), where \(q=p_{\mathrm{ref}}\). Sum over tokens, divide by \(GL\), subtract the KL gradient, then take gradient ascent: \(z\leftarrow z+\eta\nabla_zJ\).

A concrete first-token calculation. At initialization, \(p_{\mathrm{old}}(A)=p_\theta(A)=1/6\). Its log-probability gradient is \(5/6\) for A and \(-1/6\) for each other item. For the positive-advantage ADE slate, multiply that vector by \(A_{\mathrm{ADE}}/12\). Repeat at prefixes A and AD with the remaining five and four actions. At this first step, all ratios are 1 and every KL gradient is zero.

After summing all twelve token contributions and taking a step with \(\eta=1\), the six logits become approximately [-0.04499, -0.09513, -0.14706, 0.17629, 0.07986, 0.03103]. A still decreases overall despite appearing in ADE: its shared logit also receives the negative ABC contribution. Recompute the masked softmax to obtain the probabilities shown below.

Clipping makes the reward term locally flat when a positive-advantage action has \(\rho>1+\epsilon\), or a negative-advantage action has \(\rho<1-\epsilon\). It does not project the policy into a hard trust region: shared parameters and the KL term can still change those probabilities. \(\beta\) therefore affects later steps here, not the identical-policy first step.

7. From this toy batch to real deployment

Two distinct feedback paths

Reward-model rollout pathLogged user-feedback path
Generate alternative slates for a context; predict their rewards; update the policy.Learn from recommendations actually exposed, with measured outcomes, timestamps, and the available logging-policy information.
New model samples can be on-policy even though no user sees them.Fresh data are not automatically on-policy: other retrieval routes, rerankers, constraints, and checkpoint lag affect exposure.
Guard against reward-model extrapolation and exploitation.Handle selection/position bias, delayed or censored outcomes, and poor policy support. Counterfactual correction depends on identification assumptions and usable propensities.

You cannot turn one exposed slate into four GRPO rewards merely by rearranging it. Item outcomes may change with position and neighbors. A response model or additional assumptions are needed to score the alternatives. Likewise, the token-level ratio in Section 5 is not a general correction for the historical production system's exposure bias.

What a production-ready loop adds

  1. Independent evaluation: time-respecting held-out data, strong supervised baselines, reward diagnostics, and counterfactual estimates where support and assumptions permit. Separate reward-model evaluation from the model being optimized.
  2. Serving invariants: current eligibility, valid catalog IDs, no unwanted duplicates, stable tokenizer/catalog versions, latency budgets, and safe fallbacks to the existing recommendation path.
  3. Controlled exposure: randomized tests with user-experience guardrails, suitable outcome windows, and rollback. An offline NDCG improvement is not an online engagement improvement.
  4. Fresh learning: join feedback to the exact exposure and model version; update reward models and policies on appropriate schedules. Refresh rollouts rather than indefinitely replaying a stale group.

These mechanisms connect to the production learning loop, asynchronous RL, and policy-lag monitoring. If the generator becomes a full-catalog retriever, constrained semantic-ID decoding and retrieval coverage become additional concerns; the six-candidate reranking example does not model them.

The horizon is still one request. Three generated item tokens are not three observed user transitions. Planning for retention or future preference discovery requires an explicit user-state/transition formulation or a carefully justified long-term value target, not just a longer generated list.

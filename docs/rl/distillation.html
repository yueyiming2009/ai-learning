Knowledge Distillation for LLMs

Compress a large model's capabilities into a smaller one — without rerunning the expensive training. Off-policy SFT on teacher traces versus on-policy KL against the teacher's distribution: when each works and why.

Once you have a large, capable model — trained with RL, fine-tuned for reasoning, or specialized across domains — the obvious question is how to transfer those capabilities to smaller models that are cheap to deploy. Distillation is the answer, but the word covers two very different mechanisms with very different failure modes. This page builds the distinction from first principles and explains why modern LLM post-training uses each variant where it does.

1. What Distillation Means Here

The original knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) minimizes the KL divergence between teacher and student output distributions directly — the student learns from the teacher's soft labels (the full probability distribution over tokens, not just the argmax). This is sometimes called soft-label distillation.

LLM post-training can imitate either sampled teacher actions or teacher distributions. A separate choice is where the teacher is queried: on a fixed dataset of prefixes, or on prefixes generated by the current student. Target format and state collection should not be conflated.

The key distinction

Soft-label KD — match teacher and student output distributions, for example with \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\text{teacher} \| \pi_\theta)\). Teacher outputs can be cached for fixed inputs or computed for newly generated student prefixes.

Sample-based imitation — use sampled teacher actions as supervised targets. In the offline trace recipe, generate once and train on those samples without running the teacher again.

This page compares two common recipes: offline SFT on teacher traces and on-policy distribution matching at student states. Both transfer teacher behavior; neither state collection nor the use of soft targets alone makes a method reward-based RL.

2. The Off-Policy / On-Policy Taxonomy

Whose states supply the data? is separate from what supplies the learning signal? Offline and on-policy distillation both imitate an expert. DAgger is an example of interactive imitation without a reward function; conversely, RL can learn from offline data. The distinction below concerns rollout distributions, not imitation versus RL.

Off-policy

Teacher generates; student imitates

The teacher produces a dataset of (prompt, response) pairs — once, ahead of training. The student fine-tunes on this fixed dataset. The teacher never runs again during student training.

  • Student trains on teacher's distribution
  • Equivalent to supervised fine-tuning
  • Can filter for quality before training
  • Called "offline" because data is fixed
On-policy

Student generates; teacher guides

The student produces rollouts during training. The teacher scores those rollouts and provides a target distribution. The student is trained to match the teacher at states the student actually visits.

  • Student trains on its own distribution
  • Teacher must be available throughout training
  • Supervision covers current student-visited states
  • On-policy refers to the generating policy, not merely live data

3. Off-Policy Distillation

The algorithm

Step 1
Generate
Sample \(N\) (prompt, response) pairs from the teacher model. Prompts come from the target task distribution.
Teacher runs
Step 2
Filter
For verifiable tasks: keep only correct responses. For general tasks: apply quality heuristics or a reward model.
Optional
Step 3
Fine-tune
Standard SFT on the filtered dataset. Loss is cross-entropy on teacher tokens. Student never generates during training.
Student trains

The loss is simply the standard language modeling objective over teacher-generated tokens:

Off-policy distillation loss
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{off}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(s,a) \sim \mathcal{D}_\text{teacher}} \left[ \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s, a_{<t}) \right] \]

The dataset \(\mathcal{D}_\text{teacher}\) is collected once from the teacher and fixed. The student sees no gradient from its own rollouts.

The distribution shift problem

Off-policy distillation trains the student on teacher-generated prefixes, but at inference time the student generates its own. Small deviations can lead to prefixes absent from the training data, where further errors become more likely. The fixed dataset supplies no direct corrective labels at those unseen states, although the model may generalize to them. This is the compounding-error problem from behavior cloning.

Long reasoning traces and multi-step tasks offer more opportunities for errors to compound, but consequences depend on recoverability and state coverage: a short task can also fail after one wrong action. Evaluate complete rollouts rather than inferring success from token loss or sequence length alone.

There are two distinct levers: richer action distributions represent alternative valid behaviors, while corrective data improves coverage of learner-visited states. Increasing model capacity alone does not guarantee either.

When filtering rescues off-policy distillation

When the task has a verifiable outcome — math answers, code that passes tests, structured outputs with a correctness signal — off-policy distillation can apply a filter that changes its character entirely: only keep responses that the teacher got right. This is the rejection-sampling distillation loop:

Rejection-sampling distillation loop
  1. Sample many responses per prompt from the teacher
  2. Verify each response against the ground-truth answer or test suite
  3. Keep only responses that pass verification
  4. SFT the student on the filtered dataset

The filter removes all incorrect responses before the student ever sees them. The student is trained exclusively on verified-correct teacher reasoning — not a sample from the teacher's full distribution, but from the intersection of the teacher's distribution and the correct answer set.

This is what DeepSeek-R1's distillation to small models does: 800K prompts, many samples per prompt from the 671B teacher, rejection-sampling to keep only correct responses, then SFT on the survivors. The student never sees a wrong answer in training.

4. On-Policy Distillation

The algorithm

On-policy distillation addresses the teacher/learner state-coverage gap by making the student generate during training. The teacher is consulted on those student-generated states:

Step 1
Student rollout
The student generates responses from its current policy for a batch of prompts.
Student runs
Step 2
Teacher scoring
The frozen teacher computes token-level probability distributions over the student's generated sequences.
Teacher runs
Step 3
KL update
The student is trained to minimize KL divergence between teacher and student distributions at each token in its own rollout.
Student trains
Expected forward-KL over student prefixes
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{on}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi_k}}\!\left[ \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\text{teacher}(\cdot \mid s)\;\|\;\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big) \right] \]

An example forward-KL objective: \(d_{\pi_k}\) is the prefix distribution from the student checkpoint used for this collection round. Treat sampled prefixes as fixed inputs during the supervised update; refresh rollouts as the student changes. This is soft-label KD on student states, not an alternative to soft-label KD. Other distillation recipes use different divergences or estimators.

Coverage, not an exact-match guarantee

Teacher targets now cover sampled student states rather than only teacher states. This does not guarantee the final policy's deployment distribution: updates, sample reuse, decoding settings, new prompts, tools, or environments can change which states are visited. Unseen states have no direct loss term, but shared parameters can still change predictions there.

Connection to DAgger and human corrections

DAgger's collect → expert-label → aggregate → retrain loop supplies expert action labels at learner-visited states. HG-DAgger lets a human take control and supply corrective segments instead of labeling while the learner acts. The shared motivation is coverage; token-distribution KL and action-label aggregation are not identical algorithms. Aggregated historical data is not exactly the current policy's state distribution, and human intervention adds query, reaction-time, and selection costs.

Why learner-state supervision can help

When a reliable outcome verifier is unavailable, a capable teacher can still provide dense targets at the student's generated prefixes. This lets the student learn recovery behavior where it currently needs guidance, subject to the teacher's competence on those prefixes and the student's ability to learn from it.

On-policy distillation can also be combined with RL training. A natural augmentation of the GRPO objective adds a KL penalty toward the previous-stage teacher:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{teacher}} \| \pi_{\theta}) \]

This is the cross-stage distillation mechanism used in GLM-5's cascade training: each RL stage is regularized toward the previous stage's policy, preventing catastrophic forgetting while still allowing RL improvement on the new domain. Where the teacher runs matters: GLM-5 currently serves it from the inference engine, and its report lists moving the teacher into the training engine (MLA in MQA mode, \(\pi^{\text{teacher}}_{\text{infer}} \to \pi^{\text{teacher}}_{\text{train}}\)) as future work, so that teacher and student log-probs come from identical kernels and the KL term does not inherit the training-inference mismatch.

6. Why DeepSeek-R1 Uses Off-Policy Distillation

Given that on-policy distillation is generally more principled, the question is why R1 chose off-policy for transferring reasoning to small models. Three properties of R1's specific setup make off-policy the right call:

Reason 1 — The teacher has verified traces

R1's teacher outputs long chain-of-thought reasoning. These can be verified against ground-truth math answers and code test suites. Rejection sampling removes all wrong answers before the student sees them. The student is never exposed to incorrect reasoning paths — the distribution it trains on is not the teacher's full distribution, but the teacher's correct subset. This is impossible with on-policy distillation, where the student is generating the rollouts and you can't filter them away before training starts.

Reason 2 — The student can't bootstrap

On-policy distillation requires the student to generate rollouts. A 7B model that hasn't been trained to reason yet produces incoherent chains of thought — the rollouts are garbage, and the KL gradient against a teacher that writes 10,000-token derivations is pure noise. Off-policy SFT sidesteps this: the student just needs to predict teacher tokens one at a time, which is a well-behaved supervised learning problem even for an untrained model. The teacher already solved the hard search problem through RL; the student's job is reduced to imitation.

Reason 3 — Long traces transfer process, not just outcome

Standard distillation (train on teacher outputs) transfers outcome knowledge: the student learns to produce answers that look like the teacher's answers. R1's reasoning traces are different: they transfer process knowledge. The student sees the teacher working through a problem — the self-correction, the backtracking, the verification steps — at full resolution. This is equivalent to process supervision (PRMs), but with the process labels coming from the RL-trained teacher rather than human annotators. The distribution shift problem matters less when the supervision signal is this strong and this explicit.

The reasoning-trace case is the exception where off-policy distillation beats its general reputation. Most LLM distillation tasks don't have verifiable outcomes and don't produce rich step-by-step traces — for those, on-policy is typically better. R1's distillation works specifically because of the combination: verifiable tasks + RL-trained teacher with long traces + rejection-sampling filter.

7. Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation (MOPD)

On-policy distillation generalizes naturally to multiple teachers. When each teacher is a domain specialist — one trained on math, one on code, one on agentic tasks — the student can be routed to the most relevant teacher at each token during its rollout:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{MOPD}} = -\sum_{k} w_k \sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{teacher}_k \text{ is best at } t], \quad s_t \sim \pi_\theta \]

Each token in the student's rollout is guided by whichever specialist teacher is most competent for that token's context. A token inside a mathematical derivation gets gradients from the math teacher; a tool-call token gets gradients from the agent teacher. The student simultaneously learns from all teachers without any specialist needing to cover the full domain.

MOPD is used in Nemotron-Cascade 2 and Nemotron 3 Ultra with 10+ specialist teachers as a final consolidation stage after sequential domain RL. Because the student generates the rollouts, teacher routing happens in the context of states the student actually produces, addressing the expert/learner coverage gap rather than guaranteeing a match to every future deployment distribution.

See Cascade RL for full coverage of MOPD in the multi-stage training context.

8. Distillation vs RL from Scratch

The R1 paper includes a finding that reframes how to think about small-model training: a 7B model distilled from the full R1 teacher substantially outperforms a 7B model trained with GRPO from scratch (R1-Zero style) on the same compute budget.

RL from scratch (small model)

The hard path

The 7B model must discover reasoning strategies independently through trial and error. The reward signal is sparse and the search space enormous. The model may find local optima that don't match the reasoning patterns of the large model.

  • Search cost paid at 7B scale
  • No benefit from large model's discoveries
  • Slower convergence per reasoning type
Distillation from RL-trained teacher

The efficient path

The search cost was paid once at 671B scale. The teacher found the good reasoning paths. The student's job is to reproduce those paths — a supervised learning problem that scales much better at 7B.

  • Reasoning paths already found by teacher
  • Student learns to follow, not to search
  • Verified traces → no wrong patterns to unlearn
Why this matters

The key implication: the expensive RL exploration only needs to happen at one scale. Once the large model has been trained with RL to discover reasoning strategies, those strategies can be propagated to any number of smaller models via distillation at a fraction of the cost. The RL investment is amortized across all student models.

The limitation: distillation can only transfer what the teacher can do. If the teacher was trained only on math and code, the student won't gain general reasoning — it will specialize too. Distillation inherits the teacher's coverage, gaps and all.

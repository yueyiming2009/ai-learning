Knowledge Distillation for LLMs

Transfer capabilities between checkpoints, into smaller models, or back into your own model. Offline trace SFT, student-state distribution matching, and self-distillation for improvement, recovery, and consolidation.

Where this fits

Distillation is a reusable technique, not necessarily a final compression stage. The training lifecycle locates its different roles. In the MAI-Thinking-1 recipe, traces carry progress to a mid-trained checkpoint before more RL; later, specialist traces are consolidated into one model. Neither use requires a smaller student.

Once a model has learned useful behavior, distillation can transfer it to another checkpoint, combine specialist capabilities, or produce a smaller model that is cheaper to deploy. The word covers different supervision and data-collection choices with different failure modes. This page builds those distinctions from first principles.

1. What Distillation Means Here

The original knowledge distillation (Hinton et al., 2015) minimizes the KL divergence between teacher and student output distributions directly — the student learns from the teacher's soft labels (the full probability distribution over tokens, not just the argmax). This is sometimes called soft-label distillation.

LLM post-training can imitate either sampled teacher actions or teacher distributions. A separate choice is where the teacher is queried: on a fixed dataset of prefixes, or on prefixes generated by the current student. Target format and state collection should not be conflated.

The key distinction

Soft-label KD — match teacher and student output distributions, for example with \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\text{teacher} \| \pi_\theta)\). Teacher outputs can be cached for fixed inputs or computed for newly generated student prefixes.

Sample-based imitation — use sampled teacher actions as supervised targets. In the offline trace recipe, generate once and train on those samples without running the teacher again.

This page compares two common recipes: offline SFT on teacher traces and on-policy distribution matching at student states. Both transfer teacher behavior; neither state collection nor the use of soft targets alone makes a method reward-based RL.

2. The Off-Policy / On-Policy Taxonomy

Whose states supply the data? is separate from what supplies the learning signal? Offline and on-policy distillation both imitate an expert. DAgger is an example of interactive imitation without a reward function; conversely, RL can learn from offline data. The distinction below concerns rollout distributions, not imitation versus RL.

Off-policy

Teacher generates; student imitates

The teacher produces a dataset of (prompt, response) pairs — once, ahead of training. The student fine-tunes on this fixed dataset. The teacher never runs again during student training.

  • Student trains on teacher's distribution
  • Equivalent to supervised fine-tuning
  • Can filter for quality before training
  • Called "offline" because data is fixed
On-policy

Student generates; teacher guides

The student produces rollouts during training. The teacher scores those rollouts and provides a target distribution. The student is trained to match the teacher at states the student actually visits.

  • Student trains on its own distribution
  • Teacher must be available throughout training
  • Supervision covers current student-visited states
  • On-policy refers to the generating policy, not merely live data

3. Off-Policy Distillation

The algorithm

For verifiable tasks: keep only correct responses. For general tasks: apply quality heuristics or a reward model.

Step 3
Fine-tune
Standard SFT on teacher-generated content represented in the student's token space. Compatible tokenizers may permit direct reuse of captured IDs. The student does not sample rollouts during this update.
Student trains

The loss is the standard conditional language modeling objective over teacher-generated targets, with both context \(s\) and target sequence \(a\) represented in the student's token space:

Off-policy distillation loss
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{off}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(s,a) \sim \mathcal{D}_\text{teacher}} \left[ \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s, a_{<t}) \right] \]

The dataset \(\mathcal{D}_\text{teacher}\) is collected once from the teacher and fixed. The student sees no gradient from its own rollouts.

Data fidelity: teacher tokens versus student supervision

Preserve meaning; construct supervision for the student

Cross-tokenizer hard-target SFT does not require teacher-token identity. It requires faithful interaction semantics, correctly regenerated student-token supervision, and a training protocol aligned with the student's serving protocol. Exact sampled-token identity matters when the objective refers to those sampled actions; it is not a universal requirement of teacher–student training.

The objective determines which representation must remain faithful.
Training caseRequired contract
Same-tokenizer hard-target SFTCompatible vocabulary mappings allow token reuse, even across model sizes. Preserve captured prompt/response IDs for exact-call replay; intentional re-rendering defines a different, potentially valid dataset.
Cross-tokenizer hard-target SFTRender the selected content into the student's format and vocabulary. Token boundaries may differ, so a one-to-one teacher-token mapping may not exist. Regenerate masks; do not copy teacher-indexed masks onto student tokens.
Token-distribution distillationTeacher and student probabilities must refer to aligned events and conditioning contexts. Naive vocabulary-wise KL is invalid across incompatible tokenizations; use an explicit alignment or a different objective.
Student-generated on-policy trainingPreserve the student's actual sampled tokens and contexts. A different teacher tokenizer does not remove that requirement; teacher scoring still needs a compatible event/context alignment.

Capture and projection are separate. Keep raw per-call evidence immutable when available, and version the conversion into training examples. Teacher sampling logprobs are not required for ordinary hard-target cross-entropy. Likewise, a token's origin is not its supervision weight: a generated token may be excluded because it belongs to another agent, an unselected turn, or reasoning intentionally omitted by the recipe.

Roles alone are insufficient. The conversion must preserve, or explicitly transform:

  • Decision-time context: tool definitions, observations, ordering, branch identity, and context-compaction boundaries. Retaining an answer while silently changing the information it conditions on changes the learning problem.
  • Action semantics: distinguish assistant tool calls from tool results and other agents' outputs. Translating a teacher's tool-call syntax into the student's native syntax is valid if the intended action survives; silently repairing an output is a data transformation, not exact replay.
  • Supervision boundaries: select assistant turns and reasoning spans deliberately; regenerate labels after student rendering, with correct next-token alignment. Loss masking removes direct prediction-loss terms, not access to context or every gradient path through it.
  • The serving protocol: use compatible chat templates, assistant prefills, tool serialization, and termination conventions. Include intended generated end-of-turn targets, distinguish truncation from natural stopping, and mask padding by position rather than accidentally masking real EOS tokens when their IDs coincide.

The distribution shift problem

Protocol fidelity is not a behavior guarantee. Correct roles, rendering, and masks reduce accidental training–serving mismatch, but they do not prevent the student from taking different actions and visiting unfamiliar states.

Off-policy distillation trains the student on teacher-generated prefixes, but at inference time the student generates its own. Small deviations can lead to prefixes absent from the training data, where further errors become more likely. The fixed dataset supplies no direct corrective labels at those unseen states, although the model may generalize to them. This is the compounding-error problem from behavior cloning.

Long reasoning traces and multi-step tasks offer more opportunities for errors to compound, but consequences depend on recoverability and state coverage: a short task can also fail after one wrong action. Evaluate complete rollouts rather than inferring success from token loss or sequence length alone.

There are two distinct levers: richer action distributions represent alternative valid behaviors, while corrective data improves coverage of learner-visited states. Increasing model capacity alone does not guarantee either.

When filtering rescues off-policy distillation

When the task has a verifiable outcome — math answers, code that passes tests, structured outputs with a correctness signal — off-policy distillation can apply a filter that changes its character entirely: only keep responses that the teacher got right. This is the rejection-sampling distillation loop:

Rejection-sampling distillation loop
  1. Sample many responses per prompt from the teacher
  2. Verify each response against the ground-truth answer or test suite
  3. Keep only responses that pass verification
  4. SFT the student on the filtered dataset

The student learns from the subset that passes the verifier, not the teacher's full distribution. With a perfect outcome verifier, this selects correct final outcomes; real graders can admit false positives. Neither a correct answer nor passing tests certifies every intermediate reasoning step.

DeepSeek-R1's students reuse an approximately 800K-example mixture: roughly 600K reasoning samples selected with outcome and readability checks, plus 200K non-reasoning samples, including reused DeepSeek-V3 SFT data. This is not 800K independently verified reasoning traces. See the source dataset recipe (§2.3.3) and student SFT (§2.4).

4. On-Policy Distillation

The algorithm

On-policy distillation addresses the teacher/learner state-coverage gap by making the student generate during training. The teacher is consulted on those student-generated states:

Step 1
Student rollout
The student generates responses from its current policy for a batch of prompts.
Student runs
Step 2
Teacher scoring
The frozen teacher computes token-level probability distributions over the student's generated sequences.
Teacher runs
Step 3
KL update
The student is trained to minimize KL divergence between teacher and student distributions at each token in its own rollout.
Student trains
Expected forward-KL over student prefixes
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{on}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi_k}}\!\left[ \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi_\text{teacher}(\cdot \mid s)\;\|\;\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s)\big) \right] \]

An example forward-KL objective: \(d_{\pi_k}\) is the prefix distribution from the student checkpoint used for this collection round. Treat sampled prefixes as fixed inputs during the supervised update; refresh rollouts as the student changes. This is soft-label KD on student states, not an alternative to soft-label KD. Other distillation recipes use different divergences or estimators.

This token-level KL assumes aligned teacher/student prediction events and compatible conditioning contexts. With different tokenizers, raw vocabulary indices are not interchangeable; see the data-fidelity contract. On-policy collection still preserves the student's sampled sequence rather than reconstructing it from decoded text.

Coverage, not an exact-match guarantee

Teacher targets now cover sampled student states rather than only teacher states. This does not guarantee the final policy's deployment distribution: updates, sample reuse, decoding settings, new prompts, tools, or environments can change which states are visited. Unseen states have no direct loss term, but shared parameters can still change predictions there.

Connection to DAgger and human corrections

DAgger's collect → expert-label → aggregate → retrain loop supplies expert action labels at learner-visited states. HG-DAgger lets a human take control and supply corrective segments instead of labeling while the learner acts. The shared motivation is coverage; token-distribution KL and action-label aggregation are not identical algorithms. Aggregated historical data is not exactly the current policy's state distribution, and human intervention adds query, reaction-time, and selection costs.

Why learner-state supervision can help

When a reliable outcome verifier is unavailable, a capable teacher can still provide dense targets at the student's generated prefixes. This lets the student learn recovery behavior where it currently needs guidance, subject to the teacher's competence on those prefixes and the student's ability to learn from it.

On-policy distillation can also be combined with RL training. A natural augmentation of the GRPO objective adds a KL penalty toward the previous-stage teacher:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{GRPO}} + \alpha \cdot \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{teacher}} \| \pi_{\theta}) \]

This is the cross-stage distillation mechanism used in GLM-5's cascade training: each RL stage is regularized toward the previous stage's policy, preventing catastrophic forgetting while still allowing RL improvement on the new domain. Where the teacher runs matters: GLM-5 currently serves it from the inference engine, and its report lists moving the teacher into the training engine (MLA in MQA mode, \(\pi^{\text{teacher}}_{\text{infer}} \to \pi^{\text{teacher}}_{\text{train}}\)) as future work, so that teacher and student log-probs come from identical kernels and the KL term does not inherit the training-inference mismatch.

5. Self-Distillation: How a Model Can Teach Itself

Self-distillation describes the teacher relationship, not a single loss. In LLM reasoning literature, the term includes self-training on a model's own sampled traces as well as matching its token distributions. The teacher can be a stronger checkpoint from the same development process, or the very same weights with additional information in the prompt. Neither use requires a smaller student.

Three independent choices

Who teaches? An external model, your own earlier/specialist checkpoint, or the same model under richer conditioning.

Whose prefixes? Stored generated traces or newly sampled student states.

What target? Sampled tokens for SFT or a distribution for divergence matching. Calling a recipe “self-distillation” does not settle either of the last two.

Where does the teacher advantage come from?

Matching the same model's full distribution under identical conditioning gives zero KL. Unfiltered samples from that distribution also reproduce its targets in expectation; they are not, by themselves, a source of more accurate answers. A useful asymmetry comes from selection of successful outputs, extra sampling or search, privileged answers or solutions, or a stronger checkpoint. Training attempts to make those better behaviors available under the student's ordinary inference conditions.

“Self” therefore does not mean “no external information.” A correctness label, code test, reference solution, or human-written seed rationale may still supply the signal that makes self-generated supervision useful.

Filtered self-training: generate → select → SFT → repeat

Let checkpoint \(\pi_k\) generate candidate responses. A verifier or confidence rule selects a dataset \(\mathcal{D}_k\). The next supervised update uses the usual sequence loss:

SFT on selected self-generated traces
\[ \mathcal{L}_k(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_k} \left[\sum_t \log \pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\right] \]

The selection rule changes which targets are learned; this displayed loss is not GRPO's group-relative policy-gradient objective. ReSTEM gives the generate/filter and supervised-update loop an EM-for-RL interpretation. Data may come from the latest checkpoint, but it is a fixed trace dataset during each SFT round, not fresh distribution-matching supervision at every evolving student prefix. Specify both collection and update behavior rather than relying on “on-policy” alone.

STaR adds an important escape hatch: if a question gets no successful ordinary sample, reveal its correct answer during rationale generation, retain a successful rationalization, and train the question-only model to produce it. If no acceptable traces exist, that question supplies no positive SFT target; more trials do not guarantee that one will appear.

Why extra sampling can supply better traces

For a fixed question and decoding policy, suppose independent draws each have probability \(p\) of a correct outcome. With a perfect outcome verifier, the chance of finding at least one successful trace in \(N\) draws is \(1-(1-p)^N\). This is a data-collection calculation, not a prediction of the model's accuracy after fine-tuning.

For independent draws, the probability of at least one success is 1 - (1 - p)^N.

OPSD: privileged-context guidance on student rollouts

Zhao et al.'s On-Policy Self-Distillation uses one model with two contexts and a dataset \(\mathcal{S}\) of question/reference-solution pairs. The student samples \(\hat y\) from the question \(x\) alone. At each sampled prefix, the teacher sees \(x\), a reference solution \(y^\star\), and that same student prefix. It scores next-token distributions rather than generating a replacement trace.

Same weights, different information
\[ \begin{aligned} p_S(\cdot\mid\hat y_{<t}) &= \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,\hat y_{<t}) \\ p_T(\cdot\mid\hat y_{<t}) &= \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x,y^\star,\hat y_{<t}) \end{aligned} \] \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPSD}} = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^\star)\sim\mathcal{S}}\, \mathbb{E}_{\hat y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)} \left[\frac{1}{|\hat y|}\sum_t D\!\left(\operatorname{sg}[p_T]\;\|\;p_S\right)\right] \]

Here \(\operatorname{sg}\) means stop-gradient: sampled prefixes are held fixed and gradients flow only through student logits. The paper studies forward/reverse KL and generalized JSD; \(D\) is a chosen divergence, not a universal requirement to use forward KL.

Stop-gradient is not a permanently frozen teacher checkpoint. Both roles share weights; the teacher targets can change after the student update. Their different conditioning contexts create the teaching advantage. Keep the reference solution out of the student's input and deployment prompt, while aligning both distributions to the same next-token events.

Unlike successful-trace-only SFT, OPSD can supervise a student prefix even when its completed response is wrong. The reference-conditioned model must still be capable of providing useful targets on that prefix: dense feedback is not automatically correct feedback, nor is it a label certifying that each reasoning step is valid.

MAI's restart recipe is a different use of “self”

MAI-Thinking-1 collects strong RL rollouts, SFTs a chosen mid-trained checkpoint on those traces, and resumes an RL climb from the SFT result. The teacher advantage comes from behavior already discovered during earlier RL, not from giving the teacher a privileged solution at each student prefix. Its report cites the four papers above as background, but describes offline trace-SFT restarts, not OPSD. See §3.1.4.

What to verify before calling it improvement

  • Signal validity: self-consistency can confidently repeat an error; answer checks and tests can miss faulty reasoning or unsupported cases. Audit accepted examples, not just the acceptance rate.
  • Coverage: successful-only filtering can favor easy questions and collapse solution diversity. Track accepted samples by task/difficulty and retain broader data when needed; repeated rounds can overfit rather than improve.
  • No answer leakage: privileged answers belong only in the designated teacher/generation context. Keep training problems separate from held-out evaluation.
  • Matched evaluation: compare held-out pass@1 and pass@N at stated decoding/token budgets, and check safety, tool behavior, and long-context regressions. Include collection, teacher scoring, and SFT/RL compute in efficiency comparisons.

Reasoning traces are demonstrations, not process labels. SFT learns which tokens to produce. A process reward model instead learns step-level judgments from process supervision. Outcome-filtered traces can be useful without establishing that every intermediate step is correct.

7. Why DeepSeek-R1 Uses Off-Policy Distillation

R1 demonstrates that offline SFT on a curated dataset can transfer strong reasoning capabilities to small models. Three properties explain why this is an attractive recipe; they do not establish that offline distillation universally beats on-policy methods:

Reason 1 — Selected targets can be reused

The reasoning portion uses multiple samples, outcome checks, and readability filters to select useful demonstrations. Offline SFT can reuse the resulting mixture without live teacher scoring. On-policy recipes can also filter rollouts, but filtering adds collection cost and changes which student states receive supervision. Neither recipe makes outcome filtering a guarantee of correct reasoning steps.

Reason 2 — Demonstrations provide a starting point

SFT exposes the student to complete selected solutions without requiring it to discover those solutions before the first update. This can help when its initial reasoning is weak. It is not proof that on-policy gradients would be noise: useful guidance can exist at imperfect student prefixes, and effectiveness depends on both policies.

Reason 3 — Long traces transfer process, not just outcome

Long traces demonstrate intermediate strategies, including self-correction and verification, rather than only final answers. The student imitates that token sequence; it does not receive step-level correctness labels as a PRM would. Rich demonstrations can help learning, but the student can still deviate and visit unfamiliar prefixes.

Choose using task-specific evidence, teacher access, collection/scoring cost, and coverage—not a universal off-policy/on-policy ranking. R1 supports the effectiveness of its curated trace-SFT recipe; OPSD tests a different self-distillation setup.

8. Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation (MOPD)

On-policy distillation generalizes naturally to multiple teachers. When each teacher is a domain specialist — one trained on math, one on code, one on agentic tasks — the student can be routed to the most relevant teacher at each token during its rollout:

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{MOPD}} = -\sum_{k} w_k \sum_{t} \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \cdot \mathbf{1}[\text{teacher}_k \text{ is best at } t], \quad s_t \sim \pi_\theta \]

Each token in the student's rollout is guided by whichever specialist teacher is most competent for that token's context. A token inside a mathematical derivation gets gradients from the math teacher; a tool-call token gets gradients from the agent teacher. The student simultaneously learns from all teachers without any specialist needing to cover the full domain.

MOPD is used in Nemotron-Cascade 2 and Nemotron 3 Ultra with 10+ specialist teachers as a final consolidation stage after sequential domain RL. Because the student generates the rollouts, teacher routing happens in the context of states the student actually produces, addressing the expert/learner coverage gap rather than guaranteeing a match to every future deployment distribution.

See Cascade RL for full coverage of MOPD in the multi-stage training context.

9. Distillation vs RL from Scratch

The R1 report finds that its distilled 7B model outperforms its 7B reasoning-RL experiment. This supports transfer in the reported setup, not a universal compute-matched comparison: a complete cost accounting must include developing the teacher and curating data.

RL from scratch (small model)

The hard path

The 7B model must discover reasoning strategies independently through trial and error. The reward signal is sparse and the search space enormous. The model may find local optima that don't match the reasoning patterns of the large model.

  • Search cost paid at 7B scale
  • No benefit from large model's discoveries
  • Slower convergence per reasoning type
Distillation from RL-trained teacher

The efficient path

The search cost was paid once at 671B scale. The teacher found the good reasoning paths. The student's job is to reproduce those paths — a supervised learning problem that scales much better at 7B.

  • Reasoning paths already found by teacher
  • Student learns to follow, not to search
  • Curated traces supply demonstrations; filtering and coverage remain imperfect
Why this matters

The key implication: the expensive RL exploration only needs to happen at one scale. Once the large model has been trained with RL to discover reasoning strategies, those strategies can be propagated to any number of smaller models via distillation at a fraction of the cost. The RL investment is amortized across all student models.

The limitation: transfer depends on the quality and coverage of teacher supervision. Students may generalize beyond individual traces, but a math/code-only dataset does not guarantee capability in new domains. Measure gaps and regressions rather than assuming either perfect transfer or no generalization.

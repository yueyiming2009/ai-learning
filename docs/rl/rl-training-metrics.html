RL Training Basics & Metrics

One prompt. Three answers. Follow how their rewards become a model update, then recognize those same quantities on a dashboard.

Start with GRPO: let a model try the same question several times, compare the answers, and make the better answers more likely. There is no separate critic to learn first. Read the example straight through; the equations, PPO comparison, and full metric dictionary are expandable.

The whole loop: sample answers → score them → compare within the group → update the model → sample again.

1. Ask the same question three times

What is 2 + 2? Answer with one digit.

The model samples 3, 5, and 4. These are three rollouts: attempts generated by the model, not three supplied training labels. Together they form one group because they answer the same prompt.

The model we train is called the actor. Its policy is the probability distribution it uses to choose an answer. For this toy example, let it choose only among these three digits, with one token per answer. We train only from the correctness rewards.

2. Score the answers, then compare them

A checker gives reward 1 for correct and 0 for incorrect. The group's average reward is (0 + 0 + 1) / 3 = 1/3.

The advantage starts with one question: How much better or worse was this answer than the group's average? Subtract that average from each reward:

AnswerRewardReward minus group averageLearning direction
300 - 1/3 = -1/3Make this answer less likely.
500 - 1/3 = -1/3Make this answer less likely.
411 - 1/3 = +2/3Make this answer more likely.

Standard GRPO also divides these differences by the group's reward spread. That rescales the weights without changing their signs. In this example the resulting advantages are approximately [-0.577, -0.577, +1.155]. The important idea is still just negative: discourage; positive: encourage.

Optional: where do -0.577 and +1.155 come from?

The pinned verl estimator uses the sample standard deviation. For rewards \([0,0,1]\), it is \(\sqrt{1/3}\approx0.57735\). With a small numerical stabilizer \(\varepsilon=10^{-6}\):

\[ A_i=\frac{R_i-\bar R}{s_R+\varepsilon}, \qquad A\approx[-0.577349,-0.577349,1.154699]. \]

Disabling standard-deviation normalization leaves the centered weights \([-1/3,-1/3,2/3]\). It does not change which answers are encouraged. Estimator source.

3. Use those advantages to update the actor

Training recomputes the probabilities of the sampled answers and builds a policy-gradient loss, logged as actor/pg_loss. Minimizing it pushes probability toward positive-advantage answers and away from negative-advantage answers, subject to the update's safeguards.

Here is an illustrative probability change in that direction. It is not the measured result of a particular optimizer or learning rate:

AnswerProbability when sampledIllustrative updated probability
340%38%
540%38%
420%24%

The update changes the model's next attempts; it does not rewrite the three answers already scored. This old batch still has one success out of three. New samples remain random, so measure progress over many prompts and held-out attempts.

actor/loss is the total objective used to train the actor. Here it equals actor/pg_loss, because we disabled the extra terms. In a fuller run, extra loss terms can encourage exploration or keep the model close to a starting model; these can make the two losses differ.

Optional: compute pg_loss for this exact example

For this arithmetic, omit end-of-sequence tokens, entropy bonuses, and KL penalties. Keep the rewards, advantages, and sampling probabilities fixed. Compare each current probability with the probability that generated that answer:

\[ q_i=\frac{p_{\rm current}(a_i)}{p_{\rm old}(a_i)}, \qquad q_{\rm before}=[1,1,1],\quad q_{\rm after}=[0.95,0.95,1.20]. \]

GRPO can use a PPO-style clipped policy loss without a PPO critic. With clipping bounds 0.8 and 1.2, these ratios are inside or exactly on the bounds, so the ordinary clipped surrogate has the same value as:

\[ L_{\rm PG}=-\frac13\sum_{i=1}^3 q_i A_i. \]

Before the update the loss is approximately 0. Evaluating the same batch at the illustrative new probabilities gives approximately -0.0962. The loss decreased because the helpful answer's positive term received more weight. This is an optimizer objective, not a negative success rate.

Why not immediately make the correct answer certain? Clipping removes a sample's incentive to keep pushing in the already-improved direction past the chosen ratio bound. It is a guardrail on this objective, not a hard guarantee on the final probabilities. See the clipped surrogate for the full formula.

Why can a zero loss still produce an update?

The positive and negative terms cancel in the scalar value at the start, but they push on different answer probabilities. Evaluating all ratios at 1 does not make their derivatives zero:

\[ \nabla_\theta L_{\rm PG}\big|_{q=1} =-\frac13\sum_i A_i\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(a_i). \]

Zero mean advantage or near-zero PG loss alone therefore does not mean no learning. This example uses one action token per response, so token-mean and response-mean reductions coincide; longer responses need explicit weighting.

5. Collect another group and repeat

Use the updated actor to generate fresh attempts, score them, and repeat. If a whole group gets the same reward, subtracting its average gives all zeros: GRPO gets no within-group reward contrast, whether the answers all passed or all failed. That is different from our mixed group, whose weights merely average to zero.

Real runs use many prompts and longer responses. One response's advantage is usually applied to its valid generated action tokens; prompt, padding, and tool-observation tokens are not automatically action-loss tokens. Length and speed metrics describe the workload, not whether the answers improved.

The three ideas to keep

Reward: how did this answer do? Advantage: how did it compare with the other attempts? Loss: how do we turn that comparison into a probability update?

GRPO obtains its baseline from the group, so this loop needs no learned critic.

Actor metrics: loss, entropy, KL, clipping, gradient norm, LR

Which policy is which?

The actor is being optimized; the behavior policy sampled the rollout; the old/proximal snapshot is the frozen denominator in the update ratio. Our synchronous example starts with all three equal. An async run can separate them. The reference policy is a different role: an anchor for a KL penalty, often frozen for the whole run. See Behavior vs Target Policy.

Total actor loss versus PG loss

For the pinned policy-loss path, with optional entropy and reference-KL terms enabled:

\[ L_{\rm actor}=L_{\rm PG}-c_H H+\beta L_{\rm KL}. \]

Each term uses the configured loss reduction. Entropy and KL coefficients multiply their logged unweighted terms; disabled terms are absent. In the separate-actor/critic PPO path, critic value loss is not part of actor loss. Distillation or other auxiliary objectives require inspecting their own composition. Lengths and masks: which tokens count, and how losses are averaged

In a simple text response, attention_mask selects non-padding prompt and response tokens. response_mask selects positions that contribute action loss and signal statistics. In multi-turn agent data, tool observations may be attended to but excluded from action loss. Tokens processed, tokens generated, and tokens trained on need not be equal.

The same token losses can produce different scalar losses

For token losses \(\ell_{it}\), mask \(m_{it}\), valid-token count \(T_i\) per sequence, and \(N\) nonempty sequences, the principal reduction modes are:

ModeLogical global objectiveWeighting consequence
token-mean\(\sum_{i,t}m_{it}\ell_{it}/\sum_i T_i\)Equal weight per valid token; a longer response contributes more tokens.
seq-mean-token-mean\(\frac1N\sum_i\frac1{T_i}\sum_t m_{it}\ell_{it}\)Equal weight per response after its own token mean.
seq-mean-token-sum\(\frac1N\sum_{i,t}m_{it}\ell_{it}\)Longer responses contribute more; overall scale grows with average length.
seq-mean-token-sum-normThe previous quantity divided by a normalization length \(C\).Check whether \(C\) is a configured constant or defaults to tensor width; it is not each response's actual length.

These describe the intended global objectives, not unscaled per-rank intermediates. Changing masks or reduction changes weighting and often gradient scale. Do not compare absolute loss magnitudes across such changes as if they were the same experiment.

global_seqlen: token load per data-parallel partition, not response length

In the pinned trainer, global_seqlen is a prefix, not one scalar. When batch balancing is enabled, the trainer sums each row's full attention_mask, then partitions sequences across data-parallel (DP) workers. The logger summarizes sums of those lengths per partition. It does not report the model context limit or the longest individual response. Producer and logger.

Example: full lengths \([10,10,30,30]\) on two DP partitions initially give totals \([20,60]\). Reassign one short and one long sequence to each partition: \([40,40]\). The logged min/max/difference are \(20/60/40\), balanced min/max are \(40/40\), and mean is \(40\). The batch has \(80\) tokens; average sequence length is \(20\).

To assess the new assignment, compute balanced_max - balanced_min, optionally divided by a positive global_seqlen/mean. These are derived diagnostics, not additional built-in fields in this logger. Smaller token imbalance can reduce stragglers, but the partitioner also uses a workload model and token equality does not guarantee equal runtime (attention lengths, MoE routing, and hardware matter). TP/PP ranks do not each represent another independent DP data partition.

Rollout and speed: batches, step counters, timing, and throughput

Batch units first: \(B\) prompts with \(n\) responses per prompt normally yield \(B n\) sequences before filtering or retries. A rollout batch is collected data; a training minibatch is a subset used for an optimizer update; a microbatch is a memory-sized piece whose gradients can accumulate before that update. Multiple PPO epochs reuse the batch. Configuration fields may count prompts before rollout expansion, so check their actual units.

PPO later: what changes when a learned critic supplies the baseline?

GRPO asks, was this response better than the other attempts at the same prompt? PPO instead uses a learned critic to estimate future reward from a state. Its advantages compare reward-based outcomes with those predictions. A reward model judges an outcome; the critic predicts future return. They are different roles.

A two-token PPO example

Take one response with two valid action tokens, no padding or KL penalty, and a true terminal state after token 2. Let \(\gamma=\lambda=1\), rewards be \([0,1]\), and frozen critic estimates be \([0.2,0.4]\), with terminal value zero. GAE works backward:

\[ \delta_t=r_t+\gamma V_{t+1}-V_t,\qquad A_t^{\rm raw}=\delta_t+\gamma\lambda A_{t+1}^{\rm raw},\qquad \widehat R_t=A_t^{\rm raw}+V_t. \]
TokenReward \(r_t\)Value \(V_t\)TD error \(\delta_t\)Raw advantageReturn target
100.20 + 0.4 - 0.2 = 0.20.2 + 0.6 = 0.80.8 + 0.2 = 1
210.41 + 0 - 0.4 = 0.60.60.6 + 0.4 = 1

Return: the critic's target is 1 at both positions here. With other \(\lambda\), discounts, or bootstrapping boundaries, a GAE return need not equal the undiscounted episode score. Advantage: both actions had better outcomes than the frozen critic predicted. The actor receives detached advantages, not a gradient through the reward computation or critic.

The pinned GAE function builds returns before whitening advantages. The raw advantage mean is 0.7 and the sample standard deviation is \(\sqrt{0.02}\). Whitening produces approximately \([+0.7071,-0.7071]\). The critic still targets \([1,1]\); adding whitened advantages to values would be wrong. A negative normalized weight therefore need not mean a negative raw advantage or a failed response.

This adds a critic-fitting step absent from standard GRPO. critic/values, critic/vf_loss, and critic/vf_explained_var now become relevant, while critic/returns holds value targets instead of the GRPO advantage alias.

Foundations

Neural Scaling Laws

Test loss falls as a clean power law in model size, data, and compute — and that regularity sets how a fixed compute budget should be split between a bigger model and more tokens. This page derives the joint law, works through the Kaplan→Chinchilla compute-optimal disagreement and why it resolved, extends it to repeated (data-constrained) data, and ends on how easily these laws are mis-fit.

Empirically, the cross-entropy loss of a transformer is not a mysterious function of its design — over many orders of magnitude it tracks a power law in three quantities: the number of parameters \(N\), the number of training tokens \(D\), and the compute \(C\) spent. Once you trust that shape, the central engineering question becomes an optimization: given a fixed compute budget, how big a model do you train, and on how many tokens? The famous Kaplan (2020) and Chinchilla (2022) answers disagree — and the reason they disagree is as instructive as the laws themselves. This page follows the arc laid out in Lilian Weng's "Scaling Laws, Carefully" (2026), grounding each claim in its source.

1. The joint loss law

The earliest version of the idea is old: Amari et al. (1992) derived learning curves on which generalization error decays as a power of dataset size, and Hestness et al. (2017) showed the same shape holds empirically across vision, speech, and language — with the striking observation that the exponent is a property of the problem domain, not the architecture. Rosenfeld et al. (2020) then proposed modeling parameters and data jointly. The form that the field settled on (and that Chinchilla fits) is:

Joint scaling law (Chinchilla parametric form)
\[ L(N, D) \;=\; \underbrace{E}_{\text{irreducible}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{A}{N^{\alpha}}}_{\text{finite model}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{B}{D^{\beta}}}_{\text{finite data}} \]
In plain English

"Loss is a floor \(E\) plus two penalties that each shrink as a power law: one for the model being too small to express the function, one for having seen too little data to pin it down. Make either of \(N\) or \(D\) huge and its term vanishes; what's left is the other term plus the entropy of language itself."

Holding one factor effectively infinite recovers the single-variable laws Kaplan measured — \(L(N)\approx E + A/N^{\alpha}\) at unlimited data, and \(L(D)\approx E + B/D^{\beta}\) at unlimited model size — each a straight line on a log–log plot whose slope is the exponent.

2. Compute-optimal allocation, and Kaplan's answer

The law only becomes an engineering tool once you add the budget constraint \(C \approx 6ND\). The question "what minimizes loss at fixed \(C\)?" is a one-line Lagrange problem: substitute \(D = C/6N\) into \(L(N,D)\) and minimize over \(N\).

The compute-optimal exponents

Minimizing \(A N^{-\alpha} + B (C/6N)^{-\beta}\) over \(N\) sets the two terms' derivatives equal, giving \(N^{\alpha+\beta} \propto C^{\beta}\), i.e.

\[ N_\text{opt} \propto C^{\,a}, \quad D_\text{opt} \propto C^{\,b}, \qquad a = \frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}, \quad b = \frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}, \quad a+b = 1. \]

The split between "spend on a bigger model" and "spend on more data" is governed entirely by the ratio of the two exponents.

Kaplan et al. (2020) ran this for GPT-style LMs and concluded model size should grow much faster than data: for a 10× increase in compute, scale parameters ~5.5× but tokens only ~1.8×, i.e.

Kaplan (2020)
\[ N_\text{opt} \propto C^{0.73}, \qquad D_\text{opt} \propto C^{0.27} \]

This licensed the 2020–2021 race to ever-larger models (GPT-3 175B, Gopher 280B, MT-NLG 530B) trained on comparatively few (\(\sim\)300B) tokens — "make the model bigger" was, supposedly, where the compute belonged.

3. Chinchilla overturns it

Hoffmann et al. (2022) re-ran the measurement with three complementary methods and got a sharply different answer:

  1. Fix model sizes, vary tokens — train each model on several data budgets and read off the minimum-loss frontier.
  2. IsoFLOP profiles — for each fixed compute budget, sweep model size and find the \(N\) that minimizes final loss; trace that minimum across budgets.
  3. Parametric fit — fit \(E, A, B, \alpha, \beta\) directly to all runs and solve for the optimum analytically.

All three agreed, and all three contradicted Kaplan: parameters and tokens should scale at roughly the same rate.

Chinchilla (2022)
\[ N_\text{opt} \propto C^{\approx 0.5}, \qquad D_\text{opt} \propto C^{\approx 0.5} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{D_\text{opt}}{N_\text{opt}} \approx 20\ \text{tokens/param} \]

The headline demonstration: under the same compute budget as the 280B-parameter Gopher (trained on 300B tokens), they trained Chinchilla — 70B parameters on 1.4T tokens, a model 4× smaller that nonetheless outperformed Gopher across the board. The lesson reset the field: most large models of the prior generation were badly under-trained.

The "\(\approx\!20\) tokens per parameter" rule of thumb is just \(D_\text{opt}/N_\text{opt}\) staying constant because both scale as \(C^{0.5}\). It is a compute-optimal target, not an inference-optimal one — see the note at the end of this section.

Compute-optimal ≠ deployment-optimal. Chinchilla minimizes training loss per training FLOP. If a model will serve billions of inference tokens, it is rational to train a smaller model far past 20 tokens/param so that every forward pass at serving time is cheaper. This is why LLaMA-3 8B was trained on 15T tokens (\(\sim\)1800 tokens/param) — deep into the "over-trained" regime by Chinchilla's metric, but optimal once inference cost dominates.

4. Reconciling the disagreement

Two careful papers can't both be right about the exponent — so what happened? The resolution is that Kaplan's setup contained two systematic distortions, both pushing \(a\) upward:

1 · Extrapolating from small models

Kaplan's fits leaned on smaller models, where the power law has more curvature and any fit error compounds when extrapolated across many orders of magnitude in log–log space. Chinchilla's IsoFLOP sweeps covered larger models directly, so the optimum it traced needed far less extrapolation.

2 · Embedding parameters

In small models the (un)embedding matrices are a non-negligible fraction of the parameter count, so "total \(N\)" and "non-embedding \(N\)" diverge — and the two papers counted differently. Pearce & Song (2024) show that accounting for the embedding term, via a relationship of the form \(N = N_{\setminus E} + \omega\, N_{\setminus E}^{1/3}\), reconciles the two laws locally: once you correct the parameter count (plus the last-layer compute and the warmup schedule), Kaplan's and Chinchilla's exponents are no longer in conflict.

The takeaway

The scaling exponent is real and robust — but what you put on the axes (which parameters count, how big the models are, how the optimizer is tuned) can move the fitted optimum by enough to flip a billion-dollar decision. The disagreement was a measurement artifact, not a contradiction in nature.

Why a power law at all?

The functional form isn't just a convenient fit. Two mechanistic stories try to explain it: Sharma & Kaplan (2020) tie the exponent to the intrinsic dimension of the data manifold the model must resolve; Michaud et al. (2023) and others propose a quantization picture — the model learns a discrete set of "skills" whose usage frequencies follow a power law, so capability accrues in quanta and the aggregate loss curve smooths into a power law. Neither is settled, but both predict the exponent is set by the data, echoing Hestness's 2017 observation.

5. Data-constrained scaling

Chinchilla assumes every token is fresh. But frontier runs are starting to exhaust unique high-quality text, which forces repeating data — and repeated tokens are worth less than new ones. Muennighoff et al. (2023) model this by replacing the raw token count \(D\) with an effective data count that saturates as repetitions \(R_D\) grow:

Effective data under repetition (Muennighoff et al., 2023)
\[ D' \;=\; U_D \;+\; U_D\, r_D\Big(1 - e^{-R_D / r_D}\Big) \]

Each extra epoch adds exponentially less effective data; past a fitted decay scale \(r_D\) (empirically on the order of a few epochs) repetition buys almost nothing, and eventually hurts via overfitting — Hernandez et al. (2022) observed a double-descent-like degradation when data is repeated too aggressively. Plugging \(D'\) into the joint law lets you re-derive a compute-optimal allocation under a fixed unique-token budget: the answer tilts toward smaller models and more (repeated) passes, but with sharply diminishing returns.

More recent work (Lovelace et al., 2026) adds an explicit overfitting penalty of the form \(P\cdot R_D^{\delta}\cdot (N/U_D)^{\kappa}\), capturing a key asymmetry: larger models are more sensitive to data repetition — they memorize the repeated set faster, so the safe number of epochs shrinks as the model grows. Under data constraints, "just make the model bigger" backfires sooner than the fresh-data law suggests.

6. How these laws get mis-fit

The cautionary half of the story: fitting a scaling law is itself error-prone, and small methodological choices move the extrapolated optimum. Besiroglu et al. (2024), replicating Chinchilla's third (parametric) method, traced concrete problems:

  • Averaging vs. summing the Huber losses across runs changed the effective weighting and caused the optimizer to terminate prematurely at a worse fit.
  • Precision and rounding of reported loss values perturbed the fitted coefficients \(\alpha, \beta\) enough to shift the prescribed token/parameter ratio.

Weng's post drives this home with a toy simulation: identical underlying data, fit under different loss precision, noise levels, and choices of which region to fit, yields visibly different extrapolated laws. The exponents that decide how a training cluster is spent are recovered from a noisy regression in log–log space — treat any single reported exponent as an estimate with error bars, not a constant of nature.

Practical hygiene: fit on the largest models you can afford (extrapolation error grows with the gap), report uncertainty on \(\alpha,\beta\), and sanity-check the prescribed \(D/N\) against an IsoFLOP sweep rather than trusting the parametric fit alone.

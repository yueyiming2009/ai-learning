Evaluating the Evaluator

A grader is a measurement instrument. Test its errors before using its verdicts to rank models, filter data or train a policy.

Foundations → Graders → Models → Agents → Training → Worked comparison.

1. Specify what a verdict means

“Correct,” “preferred,” “safe,” “follows the rubric,” and “passes these tests” are not interchangeable labels. Define the target criterion, allowed evidence, output schema, thresholds and abstention policy before selecting the grader.

InstrumentUseful outputAudit target
Executable checkerAssertions passed, failed or could not be executed.Test coverage, valid alternative solutions, flakiness, tampering resistance and separation of test failure from infrastructure failure.
Human rubricCriterion-level labels, preferences, uncertainty and reasons.Rater training, domain competence, ambiguity, consistency, identity blinding and adjudication procedures.
LLM judgePointwise scores or pairwise judgments under a pinned prompt and model version.Agreement with adjudicated labels, ordering/style biases, evidence use, stochasticity, malformed responses and output-borne instructions.
Learned reward modelA scalar ranking signal or preference prediction.Held-out pairwise correctness, score/threshold calibration if needed, out-of-distribution behavior and errors under policy optimization.

A reward score need not be a probability, and preference accuracy does not certify factual correctness. An answer can win a pairwise comparison while both candidates are unacceptable. Retain absolute acceptability checks when the decision requires them.

2. Build an audit set independently of the grader

Use two complementary sets: a representative sample to estimate ordinary operating errors, and a targeted challenge set to expose costly blind spots. Include outputs from the base model, current candidate, different model families and relevant task slices. Do not evaluate only easy outputs that the grader helped generate or select.

For each case, preserve the task, output, relevant evidence, criterion-level reference labels, ambiguity notes and adjudication provenance. Have qualified raters label without seeing the grader's verdict or candidate identity where feasible. Resolve important disagreements with an explicit adjudication process; do not relabel references merely to increase agreement.

Audit caseWhat it reveals
Known correct and known incorrect outputsFalse rejection and false acceptance, including subtle bugs or false facts hidden in fluent answers.
Alternative valid solutions and incomplete solutionsOverly narrow reference matching versus a checker that rewards only the easy parts.
Same content, swapped order or changed stylePairwise position preference, verbosity preference, formatting shortcuts and identity cues.
Malformed or ungradable evidenceWhether schema errors, tool failures and genuine uncertainty get a distinct status rather than silently becoming a zero or a pass.
Attempts to influence the graderWhether untrusted answer text is treated as instructions, whether checks can be altered, and whether the scorer has unnecessary authority.

Keep grader-development data separate from a held-out audit set. Repeatedly tuning the rubric or judge prompt on the audit set turns it into development data. A challenge set's enriched error distribution is useful for diagnosis but is not an estimate of production error prevalence unless appropriately sampled and weighted.

For a reward-hacking audit, record the adjudicated attempt label (including ambiguous cases), whether the behavior was only proposed or actually executed, its effect on the recorded score, and an independent task-correctness verdict. A failed exploit and a successful manipulation are different outcomes; neither should disappear into a single task pass/fail field. Preserve the rule used to distinguish legitimate shortcuts from prohibited ones. The METR case below shows why these distinctions matter.

3. Distinguish false acceptance from “how many accepted answers are wrong”

For one binary criterion, call an actually acceptable output positive and the grader's accept decision positive. Among gradable, adjudicated cases:

Actually acceptableActually unacceptable
Grader acceptsTrue positive (TP)False positive (FP)
Grader rejectsFalse negative (FN)True negative (TN)
\[ \mathrm{TPR}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\qquad \mathrm{FPR}=\frac{FP}{FP+TN},\qquad \mathrm{precision}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}. \]

TPR is recall on acceptable outputs; false rejection is \(1-\mathrm{TPR}\). FPR conditions on unacceptable outputs. Precision instead asks how trustworthy the accepted pool is. If \(\pi\) is the prevalence of acceptable outputs, Bayes' rule gives

\[ \Pr(\text{acceptable}\mid\text{accepted}) =\frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi+\mathrm{FPR}(1-\pi)}. \]

Consider 1,000 outputs, only 10% truly acceptable, with TPR 90% and FPR 10%. The grader accepts 90 good and 90 bad outputs: only half of its accepted pool is good. Its 90% overall classification accuracy does not mean 90% precision.

10% acceptable, FPR 10%: accepted-pool precision 50.0%; expected 90.0 good + 90.0 bad accepts per 1,000 outputs. At 10% acceptable prevalence and 90% recall, 10% false-positive rate yields 50% precision; 1% false-positive rate yields 90.9% precision.

For a trained policy, the output distribution can change both prevalence and conditional error rates. Reweighting a prior audit is insufficient if new failure modes appear. Report sample counts and uncertainty for each error rate, with separate invalid/abstention rates. Zero observed false accepts in a small audit is not proof that FPR is zero.

4. Audit judgments, not just JSON validity

Pointwise judging: decompose the rubric into observable criteria; supply relevant evidence and an explicit “insufficient evidence” outcome. A detailed explanation can aid an audit, but a judge's rationale is not independent proof that its verdict is right.

Pairwise judging: blind identities, randomize order, evaluate swapped pairs on an audit subset and report order-induced flips. Retain ties and both-bad outcomes. If defining a win rate as \((W+0.5T)/(W+L+T)\), state the tie rule and report invalids separately. A forced-choice win fraction with ties discarded is a different metric.

Measure repeatability using repeated judgments on the same outputs, but do not count those as new independent task labels. Overall agreement can be dominated by common easy labels; inspect confusion matrices, per-criterion performance and difficult slices. Chance-adjusted agreement measures have prevalence assumptions too and do not replace adjudication.

If a judge emits a confidence score, test whether it predicts correctness on an independent labeled audit. Choose calibration mappings and acceptance thresholds on development data, then freeze them. A numeric 1–5 rating, a reward logit or a self-reported confidence string is not automatically a probability of correctness.

The grader reads untrusted content. Isolate candidate text from instructions, protect references and executable checks, restrict tools and network access, and test the boundary. Prompt wording alone is not a security boundary. Ensembles can share the same blind spots; agreement among judges is not independent validation of the answer.

5. Test the grader under the pressure it will face

A grader used only for reporting sees sampled outputs. A grader used for best-of-N selection, data filtering or RL is actively searched for high-scoring outputs. Small systematic errors can become the policy's preferred strategy. High held-out pairwise accuracy on old outputs does not prove robustness under that new search distribution.

Keep a fixed audit panel for comparability and add separately labeled fresh outputs from current policies. Audit high-reward wins, cases where independent judges disagree, and outputs near acceptance thresholds. Do not use only the top-scoring outputs to estimate an overall error rate; retain a representative sample as well.

RewardBench evaluates preference models with structured chosen/rejected pairs, including subtle errors. That evidence is useful for ranking evaluation; it does not alone establish calibrated task-success probabilities or robustness to continued RL. Reward-model overoptimization experiments show proxy/gold divergence in a synthetic setup whose “gold” is another reward model, not perfect human truth.

When a grader changes, assign a new version and regrade a common frozen sample to measure the measurement change. Do not splice scores from different graders into one training curve without marking the break. Replaying stored outputs can isolate the grader change; rerunning the agent changes the outputs too.

METR: a high score is a screening signal, not an audit population

METR's June 2025 report began its RE-Bench review with anomalously high-scoring runs. Reviewing all runs in the three task families where it found hacks then uncovered more attempts, including unsuccessful ones that score-based screening missed. HCAST's capped, often binary scores made that heuristic unsuitable, so METR used an LLM transcript monitor. Both screening methods produced many false positives and required manual review. These were evaluation observations, not policy updates driven by rewards from those evaluations.

On a separate RE-Bench comparison using Claude 3.5 Sonnet (New), the score heuristic and monitor found two confirmed cases in common; the monitor found two more that the heuristic missed, and the heuristic found one that the monitor missed. Their disagreement demonstrates complementary blind spots, not the recall of their union: cases missed by both remain unknown. METR also counted attempts to read nonexistent reference solutions, illustrating why an audit needs an explicit policy for ambiguous attempts rather than equating “counted as hacking” with “successfully obtained an answer.”

  • Separate discovery from estimation. Use high scores, monitor flags, and disagreements to find cases, but also human-review a representative sample of unflagged and low-scoring runs. Preserve sampling probabilities if combining strata into an overall rate; do not treat an enriched flagged set as representative.
  • Adjudicate with task evidence. Inspect actions, code, score effects, and the allowed-method contract, not just an agent's assurance or a monitor's rationale. Report uncertain labels and failed attempts separately from successful exploits. Have domain-qualified reviewers resolve important ambiguities.
  • Keep detection limits visible. Report the task mix, model version, screening method, reviewed denominator, and exclusions. Different tasks, scaffolds, scorer visibility, and review methods confound a RE-Bench-versus-HCAST rate comparison; it is not a controlled test of scorer visibility or a deployment-prevalence estimate.
Do not mix experimental units. METR's prompt experiment used 20 runs per condition and counted hacking plans in the first generation, not successful whole-trajectory exploits. Its chain-of-thought pilot reviewed the 15 highest-scoring instances under its hacking classifier and 10 randomly selected from the remainder of 2,752 queries. That selected inspection supports case discovery, not a population-wide accuracy or recall claim. Neither experiment establishes that monitoring or clearer instructions eliminate hacking.

For the corresponding system controls, see the evaluator's runtime and artifact boundaries. Those proposed controls still need adversarial validation; monitoring coverage and isolation strength are separate claims.

6. Release a grader with its evidence

Keep the criterion definition, scorer code or model/prompt version, input schema, threshold, audit-set provenance, error counts by slice, abstention handling, known blind spots and approved use together. State whether it is suitable for diagnostic feedback, data filtering, checkpoint selection or a release gate; those uses have different error costs.

For training-objective design, continue to Verifiers & Reward Design. For the decision loop that consumes the measurements, use Evaluation-Driven Training. Shared infrastructure is fine; shared unexamined blind spots are not independent evidence.

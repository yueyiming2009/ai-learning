Evaluation-Driven Training

Use measurements to choose the next experiment and the right checkpoint—not to turn the final test into another training objective.

Foundations → Graders → Models → Agents → Training → Worked comparison.

1. Separate the optimization loop from the evidence loop

The optimizer consumes training examples and losses or rewards. The experimenter consumes development evaluations to choose data, methods, budgets and checkpoints. A final holdout estimates the frozen candidate's performance after those choices. All three are necessary, but they do not provide interchangeable evidence.

Training → development diagnosis → intervention → candidate selection → locked final evaluation. Evaluation can guide training without itself updating weights. Once its feedback changes any part of the system, it belongs to the selection process, not an untouched final test.

Start with a baseline under the intended protocol. Version the task splits, scorer and execution contract, specify the independent sampling unit, and write the decision rule. Log which evaluations informed each data or checkpoint choice. The split boundary includes prompts, retrieval indexes, synthetic examples and harness decisions, not just optimizer inputs.

2. Match evaluation evidence to the stage's decision

Metric definitions belong with their source: language-model evaluation, agent-system evaluation, and RL training diagnostics. The training lifecycle owns stage handoffs; this page owns how evidence informs decisions within and between them.

3. Turn failures into hypotheses, not automatic data ingestion

Observed patternHypothesis to testBounded next experiment
Loss improves; target task quality does notThe loss distribution or objective does not match the target behavior.Check task/scoring validity, inspect domain slices, then compare a targeted data change at matched training and inference budgets.
Reward rises; audited outcomes stagnateProxy exploitation, judge drift, memorization, or extra inference work.Audit rewarded wins with independent checks and freeze budgets before changing the policy objective.
New skill improves; old skill regressesInterference, narrow data concentration or a changed interface.Run retention slices and controlled mixture/replay or distillation ablations; do not hide regressions in a growing aggregate.
Agent fails on tool use despite knowing the answerSchema mismatch, lost context, wrong permissions, insufficient budget or an actual policy skill gap.Inspect trajectories and test the boundary. Compare harness changes at fixed weights before attributing everything to training.
Only development gains persistRepeated selection on a finite set, family leakage or distribution mismatch.Check provenance and grouped splits; obtain independent confirmation rather than more tuning against the same leaderboard.

Mine training examples from designated diagnostic/development pools and approved production data, not from a supposedly untouched final test. Synthetic variants of a held-out task can leak its solution structure even when wording differs. Group task families and repositories according to the generalization claim; preserve independent examples for confirmation.

Curriculum sampling and evaluation weighting serve different purposes. Training may oversample a weak but learnable skill while evaluation retains fixed target-distribution weights. If the evaluation distribution changes, version it and report old/new results separately or re-evaluate common checkpoints; do not silently connect unlike points.

4. More optimization can improve the proxy while hurting the task

Imagine a training verifier whose pass rate keeps improving while independent development tasks first improve, then suffer from a growing mismatch. The following deliberately simple model uses progress \(t\in[0,100]\) and an illustrative late-stage penalty \(\lambda\):

\[ R(t)=40+55(1-e^{-t/30}),\qquad Q(t)=45+35(1-e^{-t/20})-\lambda(t/100)^2. \]

\(R\) is a toy training-verifier pass rate and \(Q\) a toy development-task success rate, both expressed as percentages but measured on different tasks and checks. The penalty is a device for drawing possible divergence, not an RL hyperparameter or a fitted scaling law. Compare the trends, not the vertical gap as a statistical effect.

Possible causes include reward-model error, incomplete verifiers, task memorization and loss of previously useful behavior. A rising reward curve alone does not distinguish them. Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization studies proxy optimization against a fixed synthetic gold reward model. Its “gold” signal is not perfect human truth, and the equations in this figure are not that paper's fits.

Use independent task checks and fresh grader audits under optimization pressure to identify the failure. Early stopping, objective repair, data changes and constraint changes are different interventions; test rather than assuming one universal cure.

5. Checkpoint selection is itself a learning process

If checkpoint \(j\) has true quality \(\mu_j\), write its development estimate as \(\widehat S_j=\mu_j+\epsilon_j\). Picking the largest estimate selects both quality and favorable noise. Even when all checkpoints have the same true quality \(\mu\), the winning estimate is \(\mu+\max_j\epsilon_j\), and for zero-mean errors \(\mathbb E[\max_j\epsilon_j]\ge0\).

Evaluating more checkpoints, prompts, graders and mixtures can therefore overfit development data without ever training directly on its labels. A confidence interval computed as if the winning checkpoint had been specified in advance does not automatically account for that selection. This is the general model-selection bias problem, not a special property of RL.

  1. Predeclare the development rule: candidate checkpoints, primary metric, evaluation cadence, resource budget, regression constraints and stopping policy. Use safety constraints as gates, not rewards to trade away.
  2. Compare actual immutable artifacts: associate each result with weight identity, decoding settings, task/scorer versions and harness. For async evaluation, log the sampled policy version—not just the trainer's step when the result arrives.
  3. Inspect tradeoffs: reject candidates that violate required constraints; compare paired outcomes at matched budgets among eligible candidates. If none qualifies, keep the baseline or continue development.
  4. Freeze the chosen candidate and protocol: evaluate on a locked final holdout under a predeclared rule, with practical effect size, uncertainty and regressions visible. Account for multiple confirmatory comparisons when applicable.
  5. Do not quietly loop on final results: once they guide another change, mark them as development feedback and preserve independent evidence for the next final claim.

Ordinary pointwise intervals are not confidence sequences: repeatedly looking and stopping when a result turns positive invalidates their usual interpretation. Plan a fixed sample size or an appropriate sequential design. More bootstrap draws cannot fix too few independent tasks, repeated selection or a biased grader.

6. Make evaluation affordable without weakening the final gate

TierPurposeEvidence limit
Frequent smoke evaluationCatch broken outputs, protocol mismatches, missing weights, invalid environments and gross regressions quickly.A tiny, repeatedly seen set is diagnostic—not independent generalization evidence.
Scheduled development suiteCompare checkpoint trajectories and important task slices with stable definitions.It supports selection; preserve the primary suite even if targeted diagnostic sets expand.
Candidate confirmation / final suiteEvaluate the frozen candidate with declared sample size, repeat policy, budgets and guardrails.Wait for complete results and manifest reconciliation. A failed or missing evaluation is not a passed gate.

Cache immutable tasks and scorer inputs when safe, but never reuse outputs across different checkpoint or protocol identities. Keep failed attempts and evaluator costs in the accounting. When the harness, grader or inference stack changes, run common reference artifacts to separate measurement drift from learning gains.

7. Connect offline learning to deployment carefully

An offline improvement can justify the next controlled step, not an automatic broad release. Replay, shadow and canary runs answer different questions; historical logs cannot show every user response to actions the new policy would have taken. Randomized online comparisons can estimate causal effects when appropriately designed, with guardrails and rollback.

If only historical action/outcome logs are available, do not treat them as an unbiased evaluation of a new action policy. Off-policy estimation requires assumptions such as adequate action support and known or estimated logging propensities, and can have high variance. It is distinct from sampling fresh rollouts from a checkpoint.

Keep separate decisions for changing model weights, deploying a harness, updating a grader and modifying production routing. Use the Production Learning Loop for consented trace collection, controlled rollout and recovery.

GPU Hardware for ML

The compute, memory, and interconnect a training GPU actually exposes — peak FLOPs, the bandwidth hierarchy from registers to HBM to NVLink to the network, and the roofline that decides whether your kernel is compute- or memory-bound. The physical basis for every parallelism and collective cost.

All the math in this series — every matmul FLOP, every collective’s \(V/W\) cost — ultimately runs on silicon with fixed throughputs. This page is the hardware substrate: what a modern training GPU (we use the NVIDIA H100 SXM5 as the running example) can compute, how fast it can feed that compute from each level of memory, and how fast it can talk to other GPUs. Three numbers per level, and almost every performance question reduces to comparing two of them.

1 · The mental model: compute fed by a bandwidth pyramid

A GPU is an enormous amount of arithmetic throughput attached to a steep hierarchy of memories. The arithmetic is cheap and abundant; the hard part is keeping it fed. Each level up the hierarchy is smaller but far faster, each level down is larger but slower:

  • Compute: 132 streaming multiprocessors (SMs), each with Tensor Cores that do the matmuls.
  • On-chip memory: registers and shared memory (per SM), then a 50 MB L2 shared by all SMs — tens of TB/s, but kilobytes-to-megabytes in size.
  • HBM: 80 GB of high-bandwidth memory at 3.35 TB/s — where the weights, activations, and optimizer state live.
  • NVLink: 900 GB/s to the other 7 GPUs in the node, through an NVSwitch crossbar.
  • Network: ~50 GB/s per GPU across nodes, over InfiniBand or Ethernet.

Performance work is almost always about which level is the bottleneck. A kernel that re-reads HBM is HBM-bound; a tensor-parallel layer is NVLink-bound; a data-parallel cluster is network-bound. The rest of this page makes each level concrete.

2 · Compute: SMs and Tensor Cores

The H100 has 132 SMs. General CUDA-core arithmetic (the kind that runs your elementwise ops, norms, and softmaxes) is a few tens of TFLOP/s. The headline number — 989 TFLOP/s in bf16 — comes from the Tensor Cores, dedicated matrix-multiply-accumulate units that compute small matmul tiles (e.g. \(16\times8\times16\)) in a few cycles. They are why we build architectures out of big matmuls: that is the one operation the hardware does at full rate.

Precision matters: peak roughly doubles each time you halve the bits. The H100 does ~989 TFLOP/s at bf16/fp16 and ~1,979 TFLOP/s at fp8 (dense). “With sparsity” marketing numbers double these again but rarely apply to training. Throughout this series we quote dense bf16.

The key consequence for the math: a matmul \([B,D]\times[D,F]\) costs \(2BDF\) FLOPs and runs near 989 TFLOP/s; an elementwise op over the same activation costs \(O(BD)\) FLOPs but is limited by how fast you can read/write it from HBM. That mismatch is the roofline (§4), and it is why fused kernels (Triton, FlashAttention) exist: do more arithmetic per byte moved.

Every level the data has to travel through has a capacity and a bandwidth, and they trade off against each other across ~6 orders of magnitude. The diagram shows the H100’s levels to scale (log bandwidth):

The two boundaries that hurt are the orange jumps: leaving the chip for HBM (the memory wall), and leaving the node for the network (the communication wall). On-chip bandwidth is so high that, once data is resident, arithmetic is essentially free; the discipline of fast ML kernels is to pull a tile into SRAM once and do as much math on it as possible before paying to write it back.

4 · The roofline: compute-bound vs memory-bound

Whether a kernel is limited by the Tensor Cores or by HBM is settled by one ratio — its arithmetic intensity, the FLOPs it performs per byte it reads from / writes to HBM:

Arithmetic intensity
\[ I \;=\; \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{bytes moved to/from HBM}} \quad\text{(FLOP/byte)} \]

The achievable throughput is the smaller of the two ceilings: you can never exceed peak FLOPs, and you can never go faster than HBM can feed you (\(I\times\) bandwidth):

\[ \text{achievable FLOP/s} \;=\; \min\big(\underbrace{P_\text{peak}}_{989\ \text{TFLOP/s}},\; \underbrace{I \times W_\text{HBM}}_{I\,\times\,3.35\ \text{TB/s}}\big). \]

The two ceilings cross at the ridge point \(I^\star = P_\text{peak}/W_\text{HBM} = 989/3.35 \approx 295\) FLOP/byte. Below it you are memory-bound (add arithmetic intensity for free speedup); above it you are compute-bound (saturating the Tensor Cores). Drag the slider to see where common operations land:

The same logic governs communication, with HBM bandwidth swapped for link bandwidth: a collective or a parallel layer is “compute-bound” (good — comm hides under the matmul) only when it does enough FLOPs per byte sent. Tensor parallelism works precisely because its AllReduce moves \(O(BTD)\) bytes against \(O(BTDF)\) FLOPs — a high arithmetic intensity over the network.

5 · Inside a node: NVLink and the NVSwitch crossbar

Eight H100s in a server (an HGX/DGX baseboard) are wired together not by a ring or a mesh but by a set of NVSwitch chips that form a non-blocking all-to-all crossbar. Each GPU has 18 NVLink-4 links (each ~50 GB/s, a few 100 Gbps lanes), totalling 900 GB/s bidirectional (≈450 GB/s each way) into the switch fabric, and — crucially — every GPU can drive that full bandwidth to every other GPU at the same time. There is no shared bottleneck and no notion of “distance” between GPUs in a node. Those 18 links are not one fat pipe: they fan out across the 4 NVSwitches, which is exactly what lets the fabric stay non-blocking — and they set the ceiling on how many parallel NCCL channels can run at once.

▸ An 8-GPU node: every GPU reaches every other at full bandwidth
NVSwitch is a crossbar, not a ring — there are no nearest neighbors.

This is the single most important fact for tensor parallelism: a TP group can shard a layer across these 8 GPUs and pay only the NVLink-speed AllReduce, with no penalty for which GPU talks to which. But “non-blocking crossbar” is doing a lot of work in that sentence — it’s worth unpacking.

What “crossbar” actually means

A crossbar is the simplest fabric that connects every input to every output. The mental model is a literal grid: inputs run horizontally, outputs run vertically, and at each intersection sits a switch (a crosspoint). To connect input \(i\) to output \(j\), you close crosspoint \((i,j)\). Two properties make it the gold standard:

  • Any-to-any, one hop. There is no notion of “neighbor” or “distance” — every pair is directly reachable. (Contrast a ring/torus, where reaching a far node costs multiple hops.)
  • Non-blocking. All \(N\) inputs can be connected to \(N\) outputs at once — any permutation — each at full link rate, because no two connections share any wire in the middle.

The only limit is at the endpoints: two inputs can’t both drive one output at full rate (an output-port conflict). So the fabric never blocks — but a single GPU’s own link bandwidth still caps how much can flow into it. Drag a new permutation and notice all four paths stay full-rate simultaneously:

How NVSwitch really builds one

NVSwitch is not a grid of analog crosspoints flipping on and off. Since 2018, NVLink has been a packet-switched fabric, so an NVSwitch chip is a small high-speed router that behaves like a non-blocking crossbar. Inside one chip: input ports buffer arriving NVLink packets (using virtual output queues — a queue per destination — to avoid head-of-line blocking); an arbiter solves a bipartite matching each cycle to decide which inputs cross to which outputs; the crossbar datapath carries that permutation; and packets are routed by physical address (a host-side fabric manager programs the tables). The “closing of crosspoints” is done in logic, every cycle.

That last row is the hardware behind NVLS: because the switch has an ALU, it can sum data from many GPUs and multicast the result — so an AllReduce can be done in the switch instead of circulating partial sums around a ring.

Why the crossbar stops at the node

A crossbar costs \(O(N^2)\) crosspoints, so you cannot build a 10,000-port one — the silicon and wiring blow up. The universal fix is to compose small crossbars into a near-non-blocking fat-tree (Clos network), trading a little blocking and \(O(\log N)\) hops for \(O(N\log N)\) cost. Crossbars are the atom at every tier: one node is a single crossbar (NVSwitch); the InfiniBand network in §6 is a fat-tree of crossbar switch chips. Stepping from one crossbar to a fat-tree of them is exactly the bandwidth cliff at the node boundary — and the reason tensor parallelism is capped at one node.

6 · Across nodes: NICs, InfiniBand, and the bandwidth cliff

To scale past 8 GPUs you leave the node. Each GPU is paired with a network card (typically a ConnectX-7 at 400 Gb/s ≈ 50 GB/s), and those NICs connect through a switched InfiniBand or RoCE fabric — usually a rail-optimized fat-tree, where GPU \(i\) of every node attaches to the same “rail” switch so same-rail traffic stays one hop away.

The cliff at the node boundary
NVLink ~450 GB/s → network ~50 GB/s   ≈   ~9–10× less bandwidth per GPU

Everything about multi-node parallelism is organized around hiding this drop. The collectives that must cross it — the gradient AllReduce of data parallelism, the activation sends of pipeline parallelism — are chosen either to be rare (once per step) or to overlap with compute, so the slow fabric is never on the exposed critical path. The chatty, per-layer collectives stay inside the NVLink island.

8 · Summary

Compute

Tensor Cores do matmuls at ~989 TFLOP/s bf16 — the one op at full rate, which is why models are matmul-shaped.

Memory wall

HBM is 80 GB at 3.35 TB/s — orders of magnitude slower than on-chip SRAM. Keep tiles resident; fuse.

Roofline

Ridge at ~295 FLOP/byte. Below it = memory-bound (fuse for free speedup); above = compute-bound.

Crossbar in, cliff out

NVSwitch gives all-to-all ~450 GB/s within 8 GPUs; the network drops to ~50 GB/s across nodes.

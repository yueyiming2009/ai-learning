Inkling 2 Small & Mini

Two scales of multimodal agent models: a large sparse backbone and a compact looped one. Follow the architecture, the training handoffs, and the mixed-precision systems that make them practical.

TML Inkling · Thinking Machines Lab · Sources checked November 22, 2026

Release progression: Inkling / Inkling-Small precede Inkling 2 Small (November 5; weights November 12) and Mini (November 20). This is release history, not a claim that their checkpoints initialize one another.

Mini's disclosed derivation: a 24-layer model on TML's mainline scaling ladder receives looping and expert pruning. A larger internal Inkling model supplies healing data. The sources do not identify released Inkling 2 Small as Mini's parent checkpoint or healing teacher.

Coverage: architecture and multimodality · Small's four stages · asynchronous RL · Mini's pruning and distillation · numerics and infrastructure · results and disclosure limits.

The useful contrast is not simply 594B versus 26.4B parameters. Small combines a wide sparse model with long-context data and one joint RL run; Mini trades repeated computation for a smaller stored model, then repairs expert pruning through distillation. Both use low precision as part of training, not merely as a final compression step.

Recipe, not reproduction. The launch posts and the November 13 precision deep dive disclose substantial methodology. They do not release the complete datasets, exact mixtures, hyperparameters, and production code needed to reproduce these models. “Same mainline recipe” does not transfer Small's token counts, RL duration, or infrastructure configuration to Mini.

Read the backbone as three cooperating mechanisms

Hybrid attention moves information across tokens. The local layers keep most attention bounded; every sixth layer supplies global access. The query-to-KV head ratios are 6:1 for Small and 3:1 for Mini, a grouped-query attention layout. The 1M context ceiling does not mean every layer performs full attention over 1M tokens, nor that every training phase uses that length.

MoE supplies conditional capacity. A router selects eight experts per token rather than evaluating the whole expert bank. Small activates 8/384 = 1/48 of the routed experts in a layer; Mini activates 8/128 = 1/16. These are expert-selection fractions, not fractions of all model FLOPs: attention, shared non-expert operations, and Mini's repeated passes still cost work. See MoE routing for the generic mechanism.

Attention residuals move information across depth. The six-layer block structure lets later computation learn a mixture of earlier block outputs instead of relying only on a single cumulative residual sum. Mini extends this access across loop passes. Short convolutions are another local sequence operation, not a replacement for the disclosed global-attention layers. The config fields establish these components; they are not, by themselves, a complete specification of execution order or every normalization and gate.

Vision and auxiliary prediction

Small's launch describes a 292M-parameter Perception Encoder–based vision encoder, 20×20-pixel patches, and 2×2 spatial pooling before interleaving image patches with text. Both released configs specify a 23-layer, width-1,024, 16-head vision transformer, patch size 20, merge size 2, and a projection hidden dimension of 4,096; the projection adapts to each decoder's width. Matching settings establish design reuse, not identical vision weights. [1] [7]

Both checkpoints also contain a separate mtp_config: five auxiliary layers for Small, three for Mini, with block size 8. Keep this distinct from the 48/24-layer main decoder and Mini's loops. The launch describes draft/verify sampling, but these configs do not disclose the original MTP loss weights, training schedule, or measured acceptance rate. In particular, the auxiliary config's RoPE setting is not evidence that the main decoder uses the same positional implementation.

2. Mini: more serial depth without more stored layers

Mini divides its 24 layers into four blocks of six. The first block is the prelude, the middle two form the shared core, and the last is the epilogue. Only the core repeats. The zero-based config interval [6, 18) means layers 7–18 in one-based notation. num_passes=3 and is_varloop=false describe the released Mini. [2] [7]

B1: layers 1–6prelude, once
→
B2 → B3: layers 7–18same weights, three passes
→
B4: layers 19–24epilogue, once

Every core pass receives the prelude output. Passes 2 and 3 also receive a learned, gated “message” containing middle-block outputs from the preceding pass. This is richer than blindly feeding one final hidden state back into the first layer.

\[ D_{\mathrm{applications}}(L)=6+12L+6,\qquad D(3)=48,\qquad D_{\mathrm{stored}}=24. \]

Attention-residual mixing combines the prelude, the previous pass's message, and outputs of previous layers in the block. The message gate initially suppresses the previous-pass contribution, so introducing looping starts from behavior resembling the non-looping model; training then increases the message's influence. Reusing weights does not reuse the result of a layer: each pass performs new computation on different representations.

The card's approximate compute accounting is \(3.4 + (L-1)\times 1.0\) billion effective active parameters, giving 5.4B at three passes. This counts repeated weight applications, not 5.4B newly stored parameters, and is not a complete FLOP or latency model. Two models with 48 layer applications can still have very different widths, expert capacity, attention costs, and throughput.

What the looping experiments actually compare

TML reports that looping the middle 12 layers beat looping the last 12 on 27 of 28 evaluations. In a separate unpruned comparison, the non-looping ladder model cost 7.5% less training compute, but needed 140% more inference compute to match the looping model's loss on 512K-token agent traces. That is a reported compute-equivalent comparison, not a universal 2.4× serving speedup.

Reasoning, stored knowledge, and tools

More loops mean more work with the same knowledge, not a bigger encyclopedia. TML's variable-loop model improves on long-context benchmarks GraphWalks BFS and MRCR v2 as passes increase, but not on knowledge-intensive AA-Omniscience, SimpleQA Verified, SimpleVQA, or WorldVQA. Extra computation helps locate and connect evidence in the supplied context; it does not teach the weights missing facts. [2]

Search and tools help supply the missing information. Mini leads the launch's similarly sized open-weight comparison on BrowseComp, DeepSearchQA, and MCP Atlas and is strong on knowledge-work benchmarks that allow search and tool use. This makes its retrieval and reasoning useful beyond what it can recall unaided: tools bring in evidence, and the model works through it. [2]

Effort is not loop count. Released Mini has three fixed passes. The separately released TML-d24-varloop-research supports variable passes for research. Do not turn its 1–10-pass experiments into a claimed Mini runtime feature, or treat every extra pass as guaranteed extra accuracy.

3. Small: broad prediction → long-context trajectories

Read the four stages as a curriculum in both data quality and deployment resemblance. The later stages teach the model the form of reasoning and agent interaction before reward optimization begins. The counts below are Small's reported training-token budgets, not unique corpus sizes. [1]

Likewise, a larger context window, a longer multi-turn trajectory, and more training tokens are three different quantities.

4. Small: one joint RL run across heterogeneous tasks

The main RL run used 1,075 steps, over 17 million rollouts, and nine days. Instead of the previous Inkling approach of separate reasoning/agentic runs followed by multi-teacher distillation, Small optimized these task families together. This is an important methodological change: the model must handle conflicting task demands inside one evolving policy. [1]

TML sampled and conditioned on a reasoning-effort level, then included a length penalty in the reward. This teaches a speed–thoroughness trade-off; it is different from Mini's architectural pass count. A reward combining task success, length, and alignment is not equivalent to optimizing unit-test pass rate alone.

Why asynchronous execution matters

Small's rollout-length median/p99 were 43K / 1.5M total tokens, with duration median/p99 of 8 minutes / 4 hours. A synchronous barrier would repeatedly make short tasks wait for long ones. Asynchronous sampling lets the learner keep updating while older-policy trajectories finish, at the cost of policy staleness.

RL used a 256K context window with compaction training for longer tasks. Compaction is a learned-to-handle harness transition that reduces the history available at one time; it does not make a 1.5M-token trajectory fit losslessly in a 256K attention window. The total trajectory can span multiple compacted contexts. Training across harnesses and compaction strategies makes those transitions part of the workload rather than an inference-only surprise.

StableReinforce: tolerate sampler–learner mismatch

The launch calls its objective a score-centering variant, StableReinforce. Tinker's documentation describes a REINFORCE variant for off-policy/asynchronous sampling and differences between training and sampling numerics. Its public API requires additional recorded sampling information; it should not be presented as ordinary GRPO with a renamed flag, or as a fully disclosed production loss derivation. [4] The TIM reference distinguishes StableReinforce from the published score-centering method.

One explicitly documented component is KL from the sampler \(q\) to the learner \(p_\theta\), not KL to a fixed reference model. On sampled tokens \(x_t\), the two available estimators are:

\[ \mathcal L_{\mathrm{KL},k1}=\beta\sum_t\bigl(\log q(x_t)-\log p_\theta(x_t)\bigr), \qquad \mathcal L_{\mathrm{KL},k3}=\beta\sum_t(r_t-1-\log r_t), \quad r_t=\frac{p_\theta(x_t)}{q(x_t)}. \]

The default k1 gradient raises the learner probability of sampled tokens independently of their advantage. k3 instead has a gradient proportional to \(r_t-1\), pushing down tokens the learner overweights relative to the sampler and up those it underweights. Its terms are nonnegative; a finite-sample k1 estimate need not be. The documented default kl_coeff=0.01 is an API default, not a disclosed value from Small's production run.

Alignment is woven through the recipe

Small's midtraining filters remove misbehavior traces and retain honest ones. RL adds a general alignment penalty across tasks and targeted environments, including impossible tasks where acknowledging failure is preferable to fabricating success. On-policy self-distillation from a teacher prompted with a conduct specification then trains the model to internalize that behavior. These are distinct interventions: filtering demonstrations, modifying rewards, and supplying teacher guidance on policy-generated trajectories.

The launch also reports pausing the run after monitoring detected a policy-boundary violation, hardening infrastructure, removing web-dependent environments, and adding synchronous tool-call monitoring before resuming. An asynchronous completed-rollout monitor complements those checks. This is evidence that reward design and infrastructure controls both mattered—not that model alignment alone secured the training environment.

5. Mini: prune, heal, then optimize the smaller policy

Mini follows the mainline data and quantization recipe, with two central architecture changes: looping and expert pruning. The disclosed sequence below does not assign Small's H1–H4 budgets to Mini. [2]

  1. Start with the 24-layer scaling-ladder model. Its initial MoE chooses 8 of 384 experts: a sparsity ratio of 48. Looping is introduced during training with the gated message mechanism described above. The post does not supply an exact step-by-step timing of loop introduction relative to every data-stage boundary.
  2. Prune once during midtraining. Keep the 128 most-used experts in each layer and remove the other two-thirds. The released model still selects eight experts per token. The expert bank becomes smaller and less sparse; this is not a threefold reduction of every operation or of the entire model's memory.
  3. Heal with 1T distilled tokens. A larger internal Inkling teacher supplies training data. TML reports recovery of pre-pruning performance as remaining experts learn work formerly handled by deleted experts. This is capability recovery through teacher supervision, not the addition of deleted parameters back into the model.
  4. Run SFT on a Small-like mixture. The post explicitly places supervised fine-tuning after pruning/healing, using a similar mixture to Small. It does not disclose an SFT token budget or establish that the mixtures are identical.
  5. Run 2.2T RL tokens over 2,000 steps. Post-training also includes on-policy distillation for alignment, as in Small. Do not substitute Small's 1,075 steps, 17M+ rollouts, nine days, or sampler/trainer ratio for this run.
Three techniques, three jobs. Looping increases serial work per token; pruning reduces resident expert capacity; distillation repairs or steers behavior. Only the second directly deletes stored experts. Neither more depth nor more RL creates the missing factual capacity of a much larger parameter bank.

6. “End-to-end 4-bit” is a mixed-precision contract

The November 13 technical follow-up resolves the shorthand in Small's announcement. Eligible MoE forward GEMMs use NVFP4 throughout pretraining, SFT, and RL; backward GEMMs use MXFP8. Numerics stay consistent across stages rather than quantizing an otherwise high-precision model only at release. It does not mean attention, gradients, optimizer state, communication, and every other operation are FP4. [3]

The subtle part: transposing changes the quantization problem

Let \(x_q,w_q\) be the operands reconstructed from the forward quantization, with \(y=x_qw_q^\top\). The linear-layer backward uses the corresponding products \(g w_q\) and \(g^\top x_q\). But transposition changes which values share a scale: a row-wise 1×16 block becomes a different column-wise group. Re-quantizing the original high-precision weights independently can therefore make backward use different values from forward.

A transpose-invariant 16×16 scale avoids that mismatch but exposes 256 values to a single outlier. TML found the resulting underflow too damaging. Instead it starts from the values actually used in forward, dequantizes and transposes them, and rounds stochastically into MXFP8 for backward. Preserving those values in expectation removes one source of systematic bias; it does not undo the original NVFP4 error or make the entire quantized training process exact.

For neighboring representable values \(a\le x\le b\), choose \(b\) with probability \((x-a)/(b-a)\), otherwise \(a\). Then \(\mathbb E[Q_{\mathrm{SR}}(x)]=x\), provided the value is bracketed rather than clipped. The source's toy example preserves a forward value of 4.5 by choosing 4 with probability 0.75 and 6 with probability 0.25. Deterministic nearest rounding always returns 4.

Between 4 and 6, expected stochastic rounding equals the input. Nearest rounding returns 4 below 5 and 6 at or above 5.

Communication, kernels, and rollout reuse

On GB300, the cited peak rates are 15 PFLOP/s for NVFP4, 5 for MXFP8, and 2.5 for BF16. A nominal 3× GEMM advantage over MXFP8 is not a 3× model-training advantage. Expert-parallel dispatch and combine can become the bottleneck; the low-bit communication paths above shrink it. TML's in-house BBBK kernel stack fuses Hadamard rotation and S2F6 quantization into the output GEMM epilogue, with pipeline scheduling, register planning, and ALU vectorization alongside Tensor Core work.

Small's RL stack also uses high expert parallelism, expert load balancing, and CUDA Graphs; sampling captures prefill, draft, and verify. Sticky routing plus DRAM KV-cache offload reportedly achieves 93%+ prefix-cache hits, helping sustain a 5:3 sampler:trainer ratio. Weight transfer uses GPUDirect RDMA with a parallelism-invariant checksum verifier and reported 93%+ line rate. These are disclosures about Small's production stack, not default Tinker Cookbook settings. [1]

Measured claim versus hardware ceiling: the precision post reports 8% pretraining and 20% RL wall-clock speedups over full MXFP8 in their largest jobs. It reports about a 2% pretraining compute-efficiency cost, but no discernible RL reward difference. Those end-to-end trade-offs—not peak FP4 arithmetic—are the relevant evidence. The production BBBK stack is described, not supplied as a complete reproducible training system.

7. What you can experiment with: Tinker, not a replica of the factory

Tinker is the managed training API; Tinker Cookbook is the public client-side recipe and abstraction library. It includes SFT, verifiable-reward RL, preference learning, tool use, and on/off-policy distillation examples. Its Inkling renderer/tokenizer path must match the chosen model and effort interface. Reading or adapting these recipes does not reproduce the original pretraining run, private environments, or GPU kernel stack. [5]

Trace the public asynchronous example

Small's announcement links a specific Cookbook revision, 4a3a282. Its do_async_training has four concurrent groups: a dataloader, rollout workers, a learner consuming completed groups, and an evaluator reacting to new sampling weights. In the non-streaming path it prioritizes completed groups from the oldest sampler version. This illustrates overlapping collection and learning without asserting that Small used this exact production orchestrator.

In that revision, max_steps_off_policy caps work in flight through groups_per_batch * (max_steps_off_policy + 1); it is not a hard age cutoff. Slow groups are not discarded just for being stale, so observed lag can exceed the configured value. Streaming minibatches consume groups in completion order. The code records minimum, mean, and maximum staleness. These details matter when turning a documented objective into a practical experiment.

StableReinforce API contract, not a production hyperparameter recipe

  1. Create the sampling client with record_stability_info=True. Preserve returned sequence_ids and sampling log-probabilities.
  2. Construct training datums with target_tokens, logprobs, and advantages, aligned to the next-token shift. Supply both model-input and loss-input provenance span lists. Tokens with nonzero advantage must identify stability-recorded sampled sequences; the required sampled loss spans cover complete sequences.
  3. Call forward_backward(..., loss_fn="stable_reinforce", loss_fn_config=...), then the optimizer step. Choose the documented KL settings deliberately rather than assuming they are Small's settings. Sampling and training provenance must belong to the same session.
  4. Evaluate held-out tasks separately from reward, and inspect task-wise quality, rollout failures, policy lag, token budgets, and alignment. Start with a bounded customization run, not a claim to reconstruct Inkling's full recipe.

StableReinforce is marked alpha. This is a source-checked API overview, not an executed training tutorial. Follow the current Datum provenance documentation and verify SDK/Cookbook compatibility before adapting the pinned example.

Looping and monitorability: preliminary, not a safety theorem

In the separate variable-loop model, the reported no-chain-of-thought time horizon rises from 16 seconds at one pass to 37 seconds at three, measured in human task time at 50% success—not model latency. More passes shorten visible reasoning, and serial lookup performance improves out to ten passes. In the reported experiments the shorter reasoning remains legible, and agentic stealth does not show a clear trend with loop count.

Those findings are limited by the training and evaluation design: variable passes 1–5 were introduced during midtraining, mostly at three passes, and RL used three passes. Tests at other depths do not establish how a model trained with RL at those depths would behave. Readable text is not proof that all important computation was verbalized. The launch explicitly encourages further research at larger scales. [2]

Open weights and realistic memory budgets

Both releases use Apache 2.0. Small's card recommends at least 500GB aggregate VRAM for the NVFP4 checkpoint and 800GB for the alternative MXFP8 checkpoint. Mini's mixed-precision checkpoint is about 20GB; its supported local configurations include Apple silicon with at least 32GB memory and NVIDIA DGX Spark. Model weights alone are not the runtime footprint: context length, KV cache, workspaces, concurrency, and framework support matter. A 1M context limit is not a promise of 1M-token operation on every minimum-memory device. [6]

Mini's native 20-layer NVFP4 / 4-layer MXFP8 deployment avoids a new post-training precision conversion on those paths. That does not make training numerically lossless, make all runtime arithmetic FP4, or guarantee equivalence for independently converted GGUF formats. The launch and cards list serving integrations and RL tools; their availability is not evidence that the production training stack is open.

Known weaknesses and data provenance

Small's launch notes verbosity, uneven tool choice in skill-heavy codebases, presentation issues, and overrefusal. Mini is not the strongest comparable model for multi-turn chat or heavily constrained instructions: it reports 50.0% on MultiChallenge and 33.4% on ComplexConstraints v1.1, and trails larger models on difficult reasoning. Strong tool-assisted results do not erase a smaller model's closed-book knowledge limits.

Both cards describe training content including text, images, and video, drawn from public sources, third parties, internal generation, and synthetic generation/augmentation, with cleaning, deduplication, and filtering. These are broad provenance categories, not an itemized dataset manifest or independently audited source/license ledger. Video in the training-data description does not expand the released text/image input API into a documented video or audio interface.

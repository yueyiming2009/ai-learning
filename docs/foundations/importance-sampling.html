Foundations

Importance Sampling

You want the average of \(f\) under a distribution \(p\) you can't sample from, but you can sample from another one, \(q\). Reweight each sample by how much more likely it was under \(p\) than \(q\), and the reweighted average is exactly the average under \(p\). This is the machinery that lets PPO and GRPO reuse one batch of rollouts for several gradient steps.

Across RL fine-tuning the same object keeps appearing: the policy ratio \(\rho_t = \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)\). The PPO and GRPO pages introduce it as a "clipped importance ratio" and move on. This page is the missing foundation underneath it: where that ratio comes from, why it is an unbiased estimator, why its variance explodes when the two policies drift apart, and why that is precisely what PPO's clipping is fighting.

1. The reweighting identity

We want \(\mu = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f(x)]\), but every sample we have was drawn from \(q\), not \(p\). The fix is one line of algebra: multiply and divide by \(q(x)\) inside the sum that defines the expectation.

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\![f(x)] = \sum_x p(x)\,f(x) = \sum_x q(x)\,\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\!\left[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x)\right] \]
Importance-sampling identity
\[ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\![f(x)] \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\!\big[\,w(x)\,f(x)\,\big], \qquad w(x)=\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \]
In plain English

"To get the average under \(p\) from samples of \(q\): for each sample, scale its value \(f(x)\) by the importance weight \(w(x)=p(x)/q(x)\) — up-weighting outcomes \(p\) likes more than \(q\) and down-weighting ones it likes less — then average. The reweighting exactly undoes having drawn from the wrong distribution."

The same step works for continuous variables with densities — replace the sum by an integral, \(\int q(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x)\,dx\). Nothing else changes.

Support condition. The division demands \(q(x) > 0\) everywhere \(p(x)f(x)\neq 0\): \(q\) must be able to reach every outcome that contributes to the answer. If \(q\) assigns zero probability to a region where \(p\) puts mass, those samples never appear yet the identity still divides by \(q\) — the estimate is silently biased. "Sample where it matters" is the one hard rule.

2. The Monte-Carlo estimator

The identity is an expectation over \(q\), so we estimate it the usual way: draw \(N\) samples \(x_1,\dots,x_N \sim q\) and average the reweighted values.

IS estimator
\[ \hat\mu \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} w(x_i)\,f(x_i), \qquad x_i \sim q \]

It is unbiased by construction — one line confirms it:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x_i\sim q}\!\big[\hat\mu\big] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\!\big[w(x)f(x)\big] = \sum_x q(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}f(x) = \sum_x p(x)f(x) = \mu \]

So on average the estimator is correct for any valid proposal \(q\). Unbiasedness is free; the price is paid entirely in variance (§3).

Self-normalized IS. Often \(p\) or \(q\) is known only up to a normalizing constant (energy models, posteriors, un-normalized rewards). Then use the ratio estimator \(\tilde\mu = \big(\sum_i w_i f(x_i)\big)/\big(\sum_i w_i\big)\), which needs the weights only up to a common scale because the constant cancels top and bottom. It is slightly biased but consistent, and usually lower variance — the normalization keeps a few huge weights from dominating.

3. Variance — the catch

The estimator is always unbiased, but "unbiased" says nothing about how far a single estimate lands from \(\mu\). That is variance, and for IS it is governed by how well \(q\) matches \(p\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}_q\!\big[\hat\mu\big] = \frac{1}{N}\Big(\mathbb{E}_q\!\big[w^2 f^2\big] - \mu^2\Big), \qquad \mathbb{E}_q\!\big[w^2 f^2\big] = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\!\left[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,f(x)^2\right] \]

Look at the \(p/q\) factor left over inside the second expectation. Wherever \(q\) is much smaller than \(p\) — \(q\) rarely visits a region that \(p\), and \(f\), care about — that ratio is large, and it enters squared. The proposal's thin tails, not the target's, set the variance. In the worst case \(\mathbb{E}_p[(p/q)f^2]\) is unbounded: the estimator is unbiased but practically useless because any finite sample is dominated by a handful of enormous weights.

A blunt diagnostic for "how many of my \(N\) samples are actually pulling weight" is the effective sample size:

\[ N_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\big(\sum_{i} w_i\big)^2}{\sum_{i} w_i^2} \]

If all weights are equal, \(N_{\text{eff}} = N\). If one weight dwarfs the rest, \(N_{\text{eff}} \to 1\) — you paid for \(N\) samples but have the precision of one.

A tiny worked example

Two outcomes, \(\{A, B\}\), with target \(p=[0.8,\,0.2]\) and a payoff that only fires on the rare outcome: \(f(A)=0,\ f(B)=10\). The true average is \(\mu = 0.8\cdot 0 + 0.2\cdot 10 = 2\).

A well-matched proposal

Sample from \(q=[0.5,\,0.5]\). Weights: \(w(A)=0.8/0.5=1.6\), \(w(B)=0.2/0.5=0.4\). Check the identity:

\[ \mathbb{E}_q[w f] = 0.5\,(1.6\cdot 0) + 0.5\,(0.4\cdot 10) = 0.5\cdot 4 = 2 \;=\; \mu \;\checkmark \]

Half the draws are the informative \(B\); the single-draw estimate is \(0\) or \(4\), straddling \(2\) tightly.

A mismatched proposal

Now \(q=[0.99,\,0.01]\) — it almost never samples \(B\), the only outcome that matters. Then \(w(B)=0.2/0.01=20\). The identity still holds in expectation:

\[ \mathbb{E}_q[w f] = 0.99\,(\cdot\,0) + 0.01\,(20\cdot 10) = 0.01\cdot 200 = 2 \;=\; \mu \;\checkmark \]

But a single draw is \(0\) with probability \(99\%\) and \(200\) with probability \(1\%\). Same correct mean, wildly larger variance: most batches report \(\approx 0\), then one rare batch slams in a \(200\). The estimator is right "on average" over runs you will never actually average over.

The lesson that carries straight into RL: IS is trustworthy only while \(q\) stays close to \(p\) in the regions \(f\) weights. Push them apart and the math stays unbiased while the estimates turn to noise.

4. Why RL needs it: off-policy policy gradients

Here is the payoff. A policy-gradient method wants to improve \(J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\![A(s,a)]\) — the expected advantage of actions drawn from the current policy \(\pi_\theta\). But the rollouts in the buffer were generated by an older policy \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\): the moment we take one gradient step, \(\pi_\theta\) changes and the data is stale. Strictly on-policy methods must then throw the batch away after a single update.

Importance sampling is exactly the bridge. With target \(p=\pi_\theta\) and proposal \(q=\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\):

Off-policy surrogate objective
\[ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[A(s,a)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\Big[\;\underbrace{\tfrac{\pi_\theta(a\mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a\mid s)}}_{\textstyle \rho(\theta)\,=\,w(a)}\;A(s,a)\Big] \]

The policy ratio \(\rho\) is the importance weight \(w=p/q\). That is the whole reason it appears in PPO and GRPO — it reweights advantages computed on the old policy's samples back to what the new policy would have done.

Two properties make this surrogate the right thing to differentiate:

  1. Its gradient matches the true policy gradient at the sampling point. Since \(\nabla_\theta \rho = \rho\,\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta\) and \(\rho = 1\) when \(\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}\), \[ \nabla_\theta J\big|_{\theta=\theta_{\text{old}}} = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\!\big[\nabla_\theta \log\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\;A(s,a)\big] \] which is exactly the REINFORCE / score-function policy gradient. The reweighting costs nothing at the current parameters.
  2. It stays a well-defined function of \(\theta\) away from \(\theta_{\text{old}}\). Because \(\rho(\theta)\) is written in terms of \(\theta\), we can keep evaluating and differentiating the surrogate as \(\theta\) moves — taking several gradient epochs over minibatches of the same rollout. That reuse, which on-policy methods cannot do, is the practical prize importance sampling buys.

5. Why PPO clips the ratio

Property 2 is also a trap, and §3 already named it. Each gradient epoch pushes \(\pi_\theta\) further from the \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) that collected the data — exactly the \(q\)-drifts-from-\(p\) situation that makes importance weights explode. The ratio \(\rho\) is a single-sample importance weight per token, so as the policies diverge a few tokens acquire enormous \(\rho\) and dominate the gradient: the estimate stays unbiased and becomes unusable.

PPO answers this by refusing to trust the importance weight past a small window. Its clipped surrogate caps how far \(\rho\) is allowed to move the objective:

\[ \mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[ \min\!\Big(\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t,\;\; \mathrm{clip}\big(\rho_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon\big)\,\hat A_t \Big) \right] \]

Once \(\rho_t\) leaves \([1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon]\) in the helpful direction, the objective flattens and its gradient vanishes — a cheap, first-order trust region that keeps importance sampling inside the regime where it is still accurate. GRPO keeps the identical per-token ratio and the identical clip; it only swaps in a group-relative advantage and moves the KL term into the loss.

This also explains the standard knob \(E=1\) epoch for LLM RL: each extra epoch widens the \(\pi_\theta\)–\(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) gap, so the importance weights drift out of the clip window and the later updates are mostly clipped away anyway. The PPO walkthrough traces this through one real training step.

6. Summary

The identity

\(\mathbb{E}_{p}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\tfrac{p}{q}f\right]\) — sample \(q\), reweight by \(w=p/q\)

Estimator

\(\hat\mu=\tfrac1N\sum_i w(x_i)f(x_i)\), \(x_i\sim q\) — unbiased for any valid \(q\)

The catch

Variance \(\propto \mathbb{E}_p[\tfrac{p}{q}f^2]\) — blows up when \(q\) misses \(p\)'s mass

Diagnostic

\(N_{\text{eff}}=(\sum w_i)^2/\sum w_i^2\) — drops toward 1 as weights skew

RL instance

\(p=\pi_\theta,\ q=\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\); the ratio \(\rho=\pi_\theta/\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) is the weight

Why clip

Drift inflates \(\rho\); PPO/GRPO clip to keep IS in its accurate regime

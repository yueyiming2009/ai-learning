Foundations

Test-Time Compute Scaling

Accuracy improves predictably when you spend more compute per query instead of more compute in pretraining. This page derives where that curve comes from — why coverage is a power law in the sample count, why the verifier decides whether that power law is reachable, why best-of-\(n\) buys only \(\log n\) nats of leverage for \(n\)× the cost, and when a small model plus search actually beats a bigger model.

Neural scaling laws describe compute spent once, before the model ships: bigger \(N\), more tokens \(D\), lower loss. Test-time compute is the other budget — the FLOPs spent every time a user asks a question. It obeys its own regularity, and the two are substitutes: a fixed FLOP can go into pretraining a better policy or into searching harder with the policy you have.

The empirical headline (o1, o3, DeepSeek-R1, and the search literature before them) is that accuracy climbs steadily as you scale inference compute over several orders of magnitude. The interesting part is why, and where it stops. This page builds that from the sampling distribution up, then connects it to the serving cost in the inference compute model and to the search algorithms in tree search for LLMs.

1. Two axes: parallel width and sequential depth

"More test-time compute" is not one knob. There are two, and they are close to orthogonal:

Parallel (width)

Draw \(n\) independent samples from the same prompt, then pick one: best-of-\(n\), majority vote, beam search over steps.

Sequential (depth)

Spend more tokens inside one chain: longer chain-of-thought, self-revision, backtracking, verify-then-retry.

The distinction is about conditioning. Parallel samples are i.i.d. draws from \(\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\) — sample 7 knows nothing about samples 1–6. Sequential tokens condition on everything generated so far, so attempt 7 can look at why attempt 6 failed. That single difference drives everything downstream: the parallel curve is governed by a clean order-statistics argument (§3), the sequential curve is not.

Every practical search algorithm is a mixture of the two. Beam search is \(n\) parallel branches carried \(T\) steps deep; MCTS is parallel rollouts whose results feed back into later selections; an o1-style thinking trace is almost pure depth. See tree search for LLMs for the algorithms and MCTS for the selection math.

2. The budget — what a test-time FLOP buys

Use the standard dense-transformer accounting: a forward pass costs \(\approx 2N\) FLOPs per token. Prefill is computed once and its KV cache is shared by every branch that forks from it, so for \(n\) parallel samples of \(T_\text{dec}\) tokens each:

Inference cost of one question
\[ C_\text{query} \;\approx\; \underbrace{2N\,T_\text{pre}}_{\text{prefill, paid once}} \;+\; \underbrace{2N\,n\,T_\text{dec}}_{\text{decode, paid per sample}} \]

Now compare that against a single sequential chain of the same total length \(n\,T_\text{dec}\). To first order the two are the same number of FLOPs — the \(2N\) term does not care whether the tokens are laid out side by side or end to end. The three things that actually differ:

1 · Attention cost grows with depth, not width

\(n\) chains of length \(T\) each pay \(O(T^2)\) attention, total \(O(nT^2)\). One chain of length \(nT\) pays \(O((nT)^2) = O(n^2T^2)\) — a factor \(n\) more. Depth is quadratically expensive in a way width is not; this is why very long thinking traces eventually stop being free even though the \(2N\) term says otherwise.

2 · Latency is serial in depth, parallel in width

\(n\) samples finish in one sample's wall-clock time (given hardware to run them); a chain \(n\times\) longer takes \(n\times\) as long. Width trades hardware for latency, depth cannot.

3 · Depth conditions, width does not

The extra tokens in a long chain see the previous work. The extra samples in best-of-\(n\) re-derive from scratch — including re-making the same mistake, which is exactly what makes the coverage curve flatten.

In plain English

"FLOPs alone don't tell you which axis to spend on — they're nearly equal. Width buys you independent lottery tickets at low latency; depth buys you a chain that can learn from its own mistakes, at serial latency and quadratic attention cost."

3. Coverage: why pass@\(k\) is a power law

Start with one question whose per-sample success probability is \(p\). Draws are independent, so the probability that none of \(k\) samples is correct is \((1-p)^k\):

\[ c(k) \;=\; 1 - (1-p)^k \]

This is exponential in \(k\). It saturates fast: at \(p=0.09\), coverage is already 95% by \(k=32\) and 99.8% by \(k=64\). If that were the whole story, test-time scaling would be a dead end — a couple of dozen samples and you're done.

But a benchmark is not one question. It is a mixture over questions of wildly different difficulty, and the dataset-average coverage is an expectation over that mixture:

\[ \bar c(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{p\sim\mathcal{D}}\bigl[\,1-(1-p)^k\,\bigr] \;=\; 1 - \mathbb{E}_p\bigl[(1-p)^k\bigr] \]

The whole behaviour is in that residual \(\mathbb{E}_p[(1-p)^k]\) — the average failure rate. Model the difficulty distribution as \(p \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\) and it has a closed form, because the integrand is an unnormalized Beta density:

Failure rate under a Beta difficulty mixture
\[ \mathbb{E}_p\bigl[(1-p)^k\bigr] = \frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\int_0^1 p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1+k}\,dp = \frac{B(\alpha,\beta+k)}{B(\alpha,\beta)} = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\cdot\frac{\Gamma(\beta+k)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+k)} \]

and since \(\Gamma(z+a)/\Gamma(z+b)\sim z^{\,a-b}\) as \(z\to\infty\), the ratio \(\Gamma(\beta+k)/\Gamma(\alpha+\beta+k)\sim k^{-\alpha}\).

The test-time coverage law
\[ 1-\bar c(k) \;\sim\; \frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\beta)}\; k^{-\alpha} \qquad\text{(power law in the sample budget)} \]

Nothing about the Beta family is essential. All that matters is how the difficulty density behaves near \(p=0\): if it goes like \(p^{\alpha-1}\), you get \(k^{-\alpha}\). The exponent is a left-tail index.

The mechanism, stated plainly

Each individual question saturates exponentially — but at any budget \(k\), the remaining failures are concentrated on the questions whose \(p\) is small enough that \(k\) samples still weren't enough. As \(k\) grows the frontier slides further into the hard tail, and it keeps finding more questions there. The test-time scaling exponent is a property of the difficulty distribution, not of any single problem. A benchmark with no near-impossible items has no test-time scaling to offer; one with a fat tail of nearly-zero-\(p\) items scales for decades of \(k\).

single question, \(p=0.091\) mixture, \(p\sim\mathrm{Beta}(0.3,\,3)\) power-law tail \(1-1.34\,k^{-0.3}\)

The power law comes from the mixture, not the problem. Both curves start at the same point — the mixture's mean success rate is exactly the single question's \(p\). But the single question (teal) is done by \(k\approx 50\): every further sample is wasted compute. The mixture (violet) is still climbing at \(k=1024\) and tracks the power-law asymptote (gray, dashed — valid only for large \(k\), which is why it dives off-frame on the left). Halving the failure rate costs \(2^{1/\alpha}\!\approx\!10\times\) the samples at \(\alpha=0.3\): steady, and brutally expensive.

Brown et al. (2024) measured exactly this across models and benchmarks and fit an exponentiated power law, \(\log \bar c(k) \approx -a\,k^{-b}\). For large \(k\) that expands to \(1-\bar c(k)\approx a\,k^{-b}\) — the same asymptotic as the mixture derivation, with \(b\) playing the role of \(\alpha\). Their striking result: on coding benchmarks, coverage from a small open model with thousands of samples exceeds the single-sample accuracy of far larger frontier models.

4. The verifier gap — coverage is not accuracy

Coverage is an oracle metric. It asks whether a correct answer is somewhere in the \(k\) samples; it does not ask whether you can find it. Real systems have to pick one, and how well they pick determines how much of the coverage curve you actually get to keep. Three regimes:

Selection mechanismRealized accuracyBehaviour as \(k\to\infty\)
Sound verifier — unit tests, a proof checker, an exact-match answer key\(=\) coverageFollows the power law all the way. Test-time compute is nearly a free lunch.
Learned verifier — outcome or process reward model\(<\) coverageRises, peaks, then declines — the search finds adversarial inputs to the reward model faster than it finds correct answers (§5).
No verifier — majority vote / self-consistency\(\ll\) coverageConverges to "is the modal answer correct?" — a hard ceiling, reached by \(k\approx\) tens.

The majority-vote ceiling is worth making precise, because it is the most common way test-time scaling silently stops working. Self-consistency returns \(\arg\max_y \hat P(y)\), the empirical mode of the sampled answer distribution. By the law of large numbers \(\hat P \to P\), so as \(k\to\infty\) the procedure converges to a deterministic answer: the true mode. If the mode is wrong, no sample budget fixes it. All the extra samples buy is a more confident estimate of the mode — which is why self-consistency curves flatten around \(k\sim 40\) and stay flat.

This is the concrete reason verifiable domains — math with checkable answers, code with tests, formal proofs — were where test-time scaling worked first. The domain supplies a sound verifier for free, so accuracy \(=\) coverage and the power law is fully redeemable. Everywhere else, the verifier is the bottleneck, not the sampler. See ORM vs PRM for how process reward models narrow this gap.

5. Best-of-\(n\) is a KL-constrained policy

Best-of-\(n\) looks like a decoding trick, but it defines a policy: sample \(n\) times, keep the top-scoring one. That induced policy \(\pi_n\) sits some distance from the base policy \(\pi_\text{ref}\), and the distance has an exact closed form.

Deriving \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_n \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref})\)

Assume the reward \(r(y)\) is continuous with no ties, and pass to the quantile \(u = F_r(r(y))\). Under \(\pi_\text{ref}\), \(u\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\) by construction. Best-of-\(n\) keeps the maximum of \(n\) draws, whose CDF is \(u^n\) and density is therefore

\[ q(u) = n\,u^{\,n-1}. \]

Selection is a deterministic function of the quantile, so the KL between the induced and base policies equals the KL between these two densities:

\[ \mathrm{KL} = \int_0^1 n u^{n-1}\log\!\bigl(n u^{n-1}\bigr)\,du = \log n + (n-1)\underbrace{\int_0^1 n u^{n-1}\log u\,du}_{=\,-1/n} \]

using \(\int_0^1 u^{n-1}\log u\,du = -1/n^2\).

Best-of-\(n\) KL
\[ \mathrm{KL}\bigl(\pi_n \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref}\bigr) \;=\; \log n - \frac{n-1}{n} \]

Read the exchange rate off that formula. Cost is linear in \(n\); distributional leverage is logarithmic in \(n\). At \(n=8\) you have moved 1.2 nats; at \(n=1000\), 5.9 nats; at \(n=10^6\), 12.8 nats. A thousandfold increase in spend roughly doubles how far you have moved off the base policy.

Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2023) measured what that displacement buys when the score being maximized is a learned reward model. Parameterizing by \(d=\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}\), they fit the true ("gold") reward gain for best-of-\(n\) as

\[ R_\text{gold}(d) \;=\; d\,\bigl(\alpha - \beta \log d\bigr), \]

which increases, peaks at \(\log d = \alpha/\beta - 1\), and then falls. The proxy score the search is actually maximizing keeps rising the whole time. That divergence is Goodhart's law with a formula attached: past the peak, additional samples are reliably finding reward-model errors rather than better answers.

proxy score (what best-of-\(n\) maximizes) gold reward (what you wanted)

Reward-model overoptimization has an optimal \(n\). Both curves use Gao et al.'s form \(R(d)=d(\alpha-\beta\log d)\) with \(d=\sqrt{\log n - (n-1)/n}\) — the exact best-of-\(n\) KL from above — differing only in \(\beta\). The gold reward peaks near \(n\approx 10^2\) and declines thereafter, while the proxy score keeps climbing, so the search reports success the whole way down. Note the \(x\) axis is logarithmic: the entire useful range is spanned before \(n\) reaches a few hundred.

Composing the two results: the gain scales like \(d=\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}\approx\sqrt{\log n}\). To double the reward gain you must raise \(n\) to the fourth power — and that is the optimistic reading, valid only left of the peak. This is the quantitative core of "diminishing returns from search."

6. Sequential scaling and the optimal mix

Sequential revision breaks the independence assumption of §3, and that is the point. If attempt \(t\) conditions on attempts \(1..t-1\), the failure probability is no longer \((1-p)^t\) — a model that can diagnose its own error has a per-step success rate that rises along the chain. When it can't, conditioning hurts: it anchors on the flawed first attempt and every revision inherits the mistake.

That gives a clean prediction, which Snell et al. (2024) confirmed empirically: which axis wins depends on question difficulty.

Easy questions

The first attempt is close. Sequential revision wins — it polishes a nearly-right answer instead of re-rolling from scratch.

Hard questions

The first attempt is in the wrong basin entirely. Parallel width wins — you need a genuinely different starting point, not a refinement of a doomed one.

Because the right ratio is question-dependent, a strategy that estimates difficulty per prompt and allocates accordingly beats any fixed strategy. Snell et al. report roughly 4× better compute efficiency from this "compute-optimal" allocation versus a uniform best-of-\(n\) baseline — the same shape of result as compute-optimal allocation in training scaling laws, one level up.

Difficulty is estimated at inference from the model's own signals — verifier score spread across a few cheap probe samples, answer-distribution entropy, or a learned difficulty predictor. The estimate itself costs compute, so it has to be much cheaper than the search it is budgeting.

7. Trading training compute for test-time compute

Both budgets buy accuracy, so the real question is a portfolio allocation over a model's whole lifetime:

\[ C_\text{total} \;=\; \underbrace{6ND}_{\text{pretraining, paid once}} \;+\; \underbrace{Q\cdot C_\text{query}}_{\text{inference, paid } Q \text{ times}} \]

Consider spending a marginal \(\Delta C\). Put it in pretraining and every one of the \(Q\) queries gets a better policy. Put it in inference and each query gets only \(\Delta C/Q\) extra FLOPs. So:

Marginal allocation rule
\[ \text{train} \;\succ\; \text{search} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial A}{\partial C_\text{train}} \;>\; \frac{1}{Q}\,\frac{\partial A}{\partial C_\text{query}} \]

Two forces both push toward training as deployment scales. The \(1/Q\) factor is the obvious one. The subtler one is that \(\partial A/\partial C_\text{query}\) decays fast — from §5, accuracy gain grows like \(\log n\) or \(\sqrt{\log n}\), so the marginal value of the \(n\)-th sample falls off like \(1/n\). Search is a last-mile instrument: excellent for a handful of high-value hard queries, ruinous as a default for a high-\(Q\) product.

This is the same logic that makes deployment-optimal training differ from Chinchilla-optimal training (Sardana et al., 2023): if \(Q\) is large, over-train a smaller model far past 20 tokens/param so every inference is cheap. See the compute-optimal ≠ deployment-optimal note there.

When does a small model + search beat a big model?

Snell et al. ran the FLOPs-matched comparison directly. On easy and intermediate questions, a smaller model given a test-time compute budget outperformed a model roughly 14× larger evaluated with a single sample. On the hardest questions it did not — there, additional pretraining was the more effective use of the same FLOPs. The reason follows from §3: search can only surface solutions the policy already assigns nonzero probability. If \(p_i = 0\), no budget \(k\) recovers it, and only a better policy will do.

The dividing line

Test-time compute redistributes probability mass the model already has. Training changes the mass. Search is leverage on an existing policy; it cannot create a capability that isn't in the support.

8. RL-trained long CoT — why o1/R1 changed the shape

Everything above treats search as an external harness wrapped around a frozen policy: sample, score, select. Reasoning models take the other route — use RL with verifiable rewards to train the policy to do the searching inside its own token stream. The long thinking trace is a linearized search: propose a step, check it, notice a contradiction, backtrack, try again.

Three concrete advantages over an external best-of-\(n\) harness, each of which maps onto something derived above:

  • It escapes the \(\log n\) ceiling. §5 bounds how far best-of-\(n\) can move a fixed policy. RL moves the policy itself, so there is no such bound — it is a training-time change wearing test-time clothes.
  • It conditions. Every token of the trace sees all prior reasoning (§6), so a failed approach is not silently re-derived the way it is across independent samples.
  • It needs no inference-time verifier. The verifier is used during training to shape the policy, so §4's verifier gap does not apply at serving time — no reward model in the serving path, and no overoptimization pressure at inference.

Empirically, accuracy for these models rises roughly linearly in \(\log\) (thinking tokens) across a wide range — the familiar log-linear signature. See the DeepSeek-R1 recipe for how the RL that induces this behaviour is set up, and GRPO for the objective.

The trace length is a controllable knob, not just an emergent one. Muennighoff et al.'s s1 (2025) demonstrated "budget forcing" — suppressing the end-of-thinking token and appending a continuation cue like "Wait" to make the model think longer, or truncating to make it stop — which traces out the test-time scaling curve for a single model at inference.

9. What it costs to serve

The two axes stress a serving stack in completely different places, which matters because a test-time-scaled workload is usually 10–100× the tokens of a normal one.

Parallel width (\(n\) samples)Sequential depth (long chain)
KV cache\(n\) branches share the prompt's cache — PagedAttention's copy-on-write makes this nearly freeOne cache that grows to the full trace length; the binding constraint on batch size
BatchingFills the batch by itself — great arithmetic intensity, continuous batching keeps it saturatedLong-running requests hold slots; head-of-line blocking on shorter requests
Latency≈ one sample's latency, if you have the hardwareScales linearly with tokens — the user waits through the whole trace
Best leverThroughput work: offline batching, larger batchesSpeculative decoding — attacks the serial bottleneck directly

The underlying asymmetry is the one from the inference compute model: decode is memory-bandwidth-bound, so each decode step pays a full weight read regardless of batch size. Parallel sampling amortizes that read across \(n\) sequences and is therefore cheaper per token than its FLOP count suggests. A single long chain gets no such amortization — which flips the §2 conclusion in practice: equal FLOPs, but width is meaningfully cheaper per token on real hardware.

10. Where the curve bends

  • The verifier ceiling (§4). Without a sound verifier, realized accuracy saturates far below coverage — and with a learned one it eventually reverses. This binds long before the sampling budget does.
  • The coverage floor (§3). Questions with \(p_i=0\) under the policy contribute \(k^{-\alpha}\to\) nothing. The exponent \(\alpha\) is fixed by the model and the benchmark; search cannot change it.
  • Linear cost, logarithmic reward (§5). \(n\)× the spend for \(\log n\) nats of movement, and \(\sqrt{\log n}\) of reward gain. Every measured test-time curve is plotted on a log \(x\)-axis for a reason.
  • Overthinking. On easy questions, forcing more sequential compute measurably hurts — the model talks itself out of a correct first answer. Uniform budgets are wrong in both directions, which is the case for per-question allocation (§6).
  • Metric inflation. pass@\(k\) at large \(k\) is an upper bound nobody can serve. Quoting coverage as if it were accuracy overstates the deployable result by exactly the verifier gap.

11. Summary

Two axes

Parallel width (i.i.d. samples) vs sequential depth (conditioned tokens). Near-equal FLOPs, very different behaviour

Coverage law

\(1-\bar c(k)\sim k^{-\alpha}\); the exponent is the left-tail index of the difficulty distribution

Verifier gap

Sound verifier → accuracy = coverage. Learned → peaks then declines. None → majority-vote ceiling

Best-of-\(n\)

\(\mathrm{KL}=\log n-\frac{n-1}{n}\) — linear cost, logarithmic leverage, \(\sqrt{\log n}\) reward

Optimal mix

Easy → revise sequentially; hard → sample in parallel. Per-question allocation ≈ 4× more efficient

Train vs test

Train wins when \(\partial A/\partial C_\text{train} > \frac{1}{Q}\partial A/\partial C_\text{query}\) — i.e. at high query volume

Foundations

Perplexity

The exponentiated cross-entropy loss, read as an effective number of choices. Simple to compute, and almost never comparable between two models — this page derives both halves.

Perplexity is the number everyone quotes when they talk about how good a language model is, and it is nothing more than the training loss with an \(\exp\) wrapped around it. That makes it trivial to compute and easy to misuse: the exponentiation turns a loss into an interpretable "effective vocabulary size," but the per-token normalization hiding inside it silently depends on the tokenizer, the eval corpus, and the context length. Two perplexity numbers are comparable only when all three agree.

1. From the loss to perplexity

A language model factorizes the probability of a sequence into per-token conditionals, and its negative log-likelihood is the average of \(-\log p_t\) over positions:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \]

Perplexity is defined as the exponential of that number. Written out in full, it is the reciprocal of the geometric mean of the per-token conditional probabilities:

Perplexity
\[ \mathrm{PPL}(x_{1:T}) \;=\; \exp(\mathcal{L}) \;=\; \left(\prod_{t=1}^{T} p_t\right)^{\!-1/T} \;=\; \sqrt[T]{\prod_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{p_t}} \]

The three forms are the same object. The middle one is worth staring at: the probability the model assigns to the whole sequence is a product, so the natural "average per-token probability" is a geometric mean, not an arithmetic one. Perplexity inverts it so that lower is better.

In plain English

"On average — in the multiplicative sense — the model gave the true token a probability of \(1/\mathrm{PPL}\). A perplexity of 20 means the model was assigning roughly 1-in-20 odds to what actually came next."

Because it is a geometric mean, perplexity is dominated by the model's worst predictions. One token given probability \(10^{-6}\) contributes \(13.8\) nats to the sum, which no amount of confident correctness elsewhere cheaply cancels. An arithmetic mean of probabilities would let a single catastrophic miss hide; the geometric mean will not. §4 shows this numerically.

2. Why exponentiate: the effective branching factor

The \(\exp\) is not cosmetic — it converts nats into a count of choices. The reason is a single special case.

Suppose the model is maximally ignorant and spreads its mass uniformly over all \(V\) tokens, so \(p_t = 1/V\) at every position. Then

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \log\frac{1}{V} = \log V, \qquad \mathrm{PPL} = e^{\log V} = V . \]

Uniform guessing over \(V\) options has perplexity exactly \(V\). This fixes the units of the whole scale: perplexity answers "how large would a uniform vocabulary have to be to make the model this confused?" A perplexity of 12 means the model is as uncertain as someone picking uniformly among 12 equally likely options — even though it is really spreading a skewed distribution over 128,000 of them. This quantity is the effective branching factor, and it is why perplexity feels more interpretable than a raw loss of \(2.48\).

\(e^{H}\) — effective choices uniform baseline (\(V=4\))

Perplexity counts choices, not probabilities. A distribution over \(V=4\) tokens puts mass \(p\) on its top token and splits the rest evenly. At \(p=0.25\) it is uniform and the perplexity is exactly \(4\); as \(p\) rises the effective branching factor falls smoothly to \(1\). Note how slowly it drops at first — at \(p=0.5\) the model has committed half its mass to one token yet still behaves like a \(3.5\)-way choice, because the leftover mass is spread across three live alternatives.

The same argument in base 2 gives \(\mathrm{PPL} = 2^{H}\) when \(H\) is measured in bits — the exponential base must match the log base used in the loss. Mixing them (exponentiating a bits-per-token figure with \(e\)) is a common and silent error; see §6.

3. How loss improvements map to perplexity

Since \(\mathrm{PPL} = e^{\mathcal{L}}\), the relationship is violently nonlinear. Equal-sized loss improvements do not produce equal-sized perplexity improvements:

\(\mathrm{PPL} = e^{\mathcal L}\) linear intuition \(1+\mathcal{L}\)

Perplexity is exponential in the loss, so its differences are not portable. Shaving \(0.1\) nats off a loss of \(3.0\) removes \(1.9\) perplexity points (\(20.1 \to 18.2\)); the same \(0.1\) nats at a loss of \(1.0\) removes only \(0.26\) (\(2.72 \to 2.46\)). The dashed tangent at the origin shows how quickly linear intuition fails past \(\mathcal{L}\approx 0.5\).

The fix is to compare ratios rather than differences. Because

\[ \frac{\mathrm{PPL}_{\text{new}}}{\mathrm{PPL}_{\text{old}}} = e^{\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{new}} - \mathcal{L}_{\text{old}}} = e^{-\Delta\mathcal{L}}, \]

a fixed loss improvement always corresponds to a fixed percentage reduction in perplexity, wherever on the curve you sit. A \(0.1\)-nat gain is a \(9.5\%\) perplexity reduction at every loss level. This is the honest way to state a modeling win, and it is why scaling-law plots are drawn in loss (or log-perplexity) rather than perplexity — power laws in loss become unreadable curves in perplexity.

Loss \(\mathcal{L}\) (nats/token)PerplexityWhat it means

The \(\mathcal{L} \approx \log V\) row doubles as a training sanity check. At step 0 a correctly initialized model outputs near-uniform logits, so its loss should start near \(\log V\) and its perplexity near \(V\). If step 0 reports a perplexity of 40 rather than ~128,000, the logits are not near-uniform and something is wrong with the initialization. The pretraining page covers the rest of that checklist.

4. Worked example

Take a six-token continuation where the model assigned these conditional probabilities to the tokens that actually occurred:

\[ p = [\,0.05,\ 0.30,\ 0.40,\ 0.60,\ 0.80,\ 0.50\,] \]
Step 1 — Sum the log-probabilities
\[ \sum_t \log p_t = -2.996 - 1.204 - 0.916 - 0.511 - 0.223 - 0.693 = -6.543 \ \text{nats} \]
Step 2 — Average over tokens
\[ \mathcal{L} = \frac{6.543}{6} = 1.0905 \ \text{nats/token} \]
Step 3 — Exponentiate
\[ \mathrm{PPL} = e^{1.0905} = 2.98 \]

Cross-check via the product form: \(\prod_t p_t = 0.00144\), and \(0.00144^{-1/6} = 2.98\). The model was effectively choosing among about three equally likely tokens at each step.

Now change only the first token, from \(0.05\) to \(0.50\) — one prediction the model had badly wrong, corrected. The product becomes \(0.0144\) and

\[ \mathrm{PPL} = 0.0144^{-1/6} = 2.03 . \]

A single token's repair cut perplexity by a third, while the other five predictions did not move. That is the geometric mean at work: perplexity is a report on the model's worst moments, not its typical ones. It is also why perplexity on a corpus containing rare proper nouns, code identifiers, or foreign text is dragged upward far more than the token counts suggest.

5. Two different things are called perplexity

The same word names two quantities with different bounds and different meanings. Conflating them causes real confusion.

Predictive perplexity — the model's own uncertainty

At a single position, exponentiate the entropy of the model's own output distribution:

\[ \mathrm{PPL}_{\text{pred}} = \exp\Big(H\big(p_\theta(\cdot\mid x_{<t})\big)\Big) \]

This measures how spread out the model's prediction is, ignoring what actually came next. It is bounded: \(1 \le \mathrm{PPL}_{\text{pred}} \le V\), hitting \(V\) only for a uniform distribution. This is the quantity that sampling temperature manipulates — raising \(\tau\) flattens the distribution and raises predictive perplexity.

Evaluation perplexity — fit to held-out data

Average \(-\log p_t\) over tokens drawn from the true distribution, i.e. exponentiate a cross entropy:

\[ \mathrm{PPL}_{\text{eval}} = \exp\Big(H\big(p_{\text{data}},\, p_\theta\big)\Big) \]

This is the reported metric, and it is unbounded above. A model that confidently predicts the wrong tokens scores worse than uniform guessing — perplexity above \(V\) is entirely possible and is exactly what you see when a model is evaluated on out-of-distribution text. The common belief that "perplexity can't exceed the vocab size" is true of the predictive form only.

Evaluation perplexity has a floor it can never cross. Since \(H(p_{\text{data}}, p_\theta) = H(p_{\text{data}}) + \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}} \Vert p_\theta)\) and the KL term is the only part training can reduce, \(\mathrm{PPL}_{\text{eval}} \ge e^{H(p_{\text{data}})}\) — the intrinsic entropy of the text. That floor is the irreducible term \(E\) in the scaling laws, and it is why loss curves flatten rather than reaching zero.
Sampling temperature does not change evaluation perplexity. Reported perplexity is defined at \(\tau = 1\) — it scores the model's raw distribution against the data. "We lowered temperature and perplexity improved" is a category error: you changed the predictive distribution used for generation, not the likelihood assigned to held-out text.

6. Nats, bits, and bits per byte

The loss can be reported in three units, and all three describe the same model. Only the last is safely comparable across models.

QuantityFormulaNormalized by
Loss (nats/token)\(\mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_t \ln p_t\)tokens
Bits per token (BPT)\(\mathcal{L} / \ln 2\)tokens
Perplexity\(e^{\mathcal{L}} = 2^{\text{BPT}}\)tokens
Bits per byte (BPB)\(\dfrac{T \cdot \mathcal{L}}{B \cdot \ln 2}\)bytes

The first three are one number wearing different hats — \(e^{\mathcal L}\) and \(2^{\mathcal L / \ln 2}\) are identically equal, so exponentiating in the wrong base is the only way to get them to disagree. Bits per byte is the odd one out, and deliberately so: it takes the total nats the model spent on a passage, converts to bits, and divides by the number of bytes in the original text rather than the number of tokens.

Bytes are a property of the text. Tokens are a property of the tokenizer. That single substitution is what makes BPB portable — and §7 shows exactly what goes wrong without it.

Framing the loss in bits also makes the compression interpretation literal: a model at \(0.72\) bits/byte, paired with an arithmetic coder, compresses that text to \(0.72/8 = 9\%\) of its original size. Language modeling and lossless compression are the same problem, which is why bits-per-byte is the metric of choice in corpora like The Pile.

7. The tokenizer trap

This is the failure mode that invalidates most cross-model perplexity comparisons, and its direction is the opposite of what most people guess.

Perplexity divides the total log-loss of a passage by the token count \(T\). But \(T\) is not a property of the text — it is a property of the tokenizer. A more compressive tokenizer represents the same string in fewer tokens, so the same total \(-\log p\) gets divided by a smaller number, producing a higher per-token loss and a higher perplexity.

Take two models with identical modeling ability, in the sense that both assign the same total log-probability \(-200\) nats to the same 400-byte passage. They differ only in tokenizer:

Total \(-\ln p(\text{passage})\)200 nats200 nats
Tokens \(T\)10080

Model B looks 65% worse on perplexity while being exactly as good at modeling the text. Its tokenizer is better — it packs more bytes into each token — and perplexity punishes it for that. Each of B's tokens carries more information, so predicting one is genuinely harder; the per-token normalization mistakes that for a weaker model. Bits per byte, normalizing by the 400 bytes both models actually explained, correctly reports them as identical.

The practical rule: a perplexity comparison is only valid when the models share a tokenizer, an evaluation corpus, and a context length. Two of the three is not enough. This is why perplexity remained a useful leaderboard metric through the era of word-level models on Penn Treebank and WikiText-103 — everyone used the same fixed word vocabulary — and stopped being one once subword tokenizers with model-specific vocabularies took over.

Converting to a common denominator

If you must compare against a number reported under a different segmentation, renormalize through the total loss. To convert token-level perplexity to word-level perplexity over a corpus with \(T\) tokens and \(N_w\) words:

\[ \mathrm{PPL}_{\text{word}} = \exp\!\left(\frac{T}{N_w}\,\ln \mathrm{PPL}_{\text{tok}}\right) \]

The logic is just "recover the total nats, then divide by the other count": \(T \ln \mathrm{PPL}_{\text{tok}}\) is the corpus-wide loss in nats, and dividing by \(N_w\) re-expresses it per word. The same move with \(B\) bytes in the denominator (and a \(\ln 2\)) gives bits per byte. Any of these is defensible; silently comparing per-token numbers across tokenizers is not.

8. Computing it correctly

The single-batch case

For one batch, perplexity is one line on top of the cross-entropy loss — note the shift, since position \(t\)'s logits predict token \(t{+}1\):

import torch.nn.functional as F

logits = model(input_ids).logits          # (B, T, V)
loss = F.cross_entropy(
    logits[:, :-1].reshape(-1, V),        # drop last position: nothing to predict
    input_ids[:, 1:].reshape(-1),         # shifted targets
    ignore_index=-100,                    # skip padding
)                                         # mean nats per token
ppl = loss.exp()

Aggregating over a corpus — the averaging bug

Perplexity does not average. Because \(\exp\) is convex, Jensen's inequality gives \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\mathcal L}] \ge e^{\mathbb{E}[\mathcal L]}\), so averaging per-batch perplexities overestimates the true corpus perplexity. Averaging per-batch losses is also wrong when batches hold different numbers of real (non-padding) tokens, since each batch then gets equal weight regardless of size.

The only correct aggregation is to accumulate total nats and total tokens, and exponentiate once at the end:

import math, torch, torch.nn.functional as F

total_nll, total_tokens = 0.0, 0
for input_ids, targets in loader:                 # targets use -100 for padding
    with torch.no_grad():
        logits = model(input_ids).logits[:, :-1]
    nll = F.cross_entropy(
        logits.reshape(-1, logits.size(-1)),
        targets[:, 1:].reshape(-1),
        ignore_index=-100,
        reduction="sum",                          # sum, not mean
    )
    total_nll += nll.item()
    total_tokens += (targets[:, 1:] != -100).sum().item()

ppl = math.exp(total_nll / total_tokens)          # exponentiate exactly once

Documents longer than the context window

A document longer than the model's context cannot be scored in one pass. Splitting it into disjoint chunks is the obvious move and it inflates perplexity badly: the first tokens of every chunk are predicted with almost no context, and those are precisely the hardest positions.

The standard fix is a strided sliding window. Advance by a stride \(S\) smaller than the context length \(L\), and in each window score only the final \(S\) tokens — every one of which has at least \(L - S\) tokens of real context behind it:

@torch.no_grad()
def sliding_perplexity(model, ids, ctx_len=4096, stride=1024):
    """ids: (1, N) token ids for one long document."""
    N = ids.size(1)
    total_nll, total_tokens, prev_end = 0.0, 0, 0
    for begin in range(0, N, stride):
        end = min(begin + ctx_len, N)
        chunk = ids[:, begin:end]
        logits = model(chunk).logits[:, :-1]       # predicts chunk[:, 1:]
        targets = chunk[:, 1:]
        n_new = end - prev_end                     # positions not yet scored
        logits, targets = logits[:, -n_new:], targets[:, -n_new:]
        total_nll += F.cross_entropy(
            logits.reshape(-1, logits.size(-1)),
            targets.reshape(-1), reduction="sum").item()
        total_tokens += targets.numel()
        prev_end = end
        if end == N:
            break
    return math.exp(total_nll / total_tokens)

The stride is a pure accuracy-for-compute trade: \(S = L\) is naive chunking (one forward pass per \(L\) tokens, worst estimate), while \(S = 1\) scores every token with a full context window at \(L\times\) the cost. Typical practice is \(S = L/4\) to \(L/2\).

Because more context always lowers perplexity, the reported number depends on \(L\) and \(S\). A perplexity measured with a 2048-token context is not comparable to one measured at 8192, on the same model and the same corpus. Eval configuration belongs in the report alongside the number.

9. What perplexity does not measure

Perplexity measures one thing precisely: how well the model's distribution matches the distribution of some held-out text. Everything else it is used for is inference by correlation, and the correlation breaks in predictable places.

  • It is not a measure of usefulness. Instruction-following, reasoning, factuality, and helpfulness are not likelihood. A model can fit web text beautifully and be useless as an assistant.
  • Alignment usually makes it worse. RLHF and instruction tuning sharpen the model toward a preferred register, which costs likelihood on arbitrary held-out text. A post-trained model routinely has higher perplexity than the base model it came from while being far more useful — so perplexity cannot be used to evaluate a post-training run.
  • It is corpus-specific. Perplexity on Wikipedia, on GitHub, and on chat transcripts are three unrelated numbers. "Perplexity 8" means nothing without naming the corpus.
  • It rewards hedging on genuinely ambiguous text. The metric scores calibration against the data distribution, so where many continuations are legitimate, a broad distribution wins — even if a decisive model produces better generations.
  • It is blind to everything past the next token. Being well calibrated at each step says nothing about whether sampled long-form output stays coherent, which is what test-time compute methods actually attack.

Where perplexity remains the right tool is exactly where it is defined: tracking a single pretraining run over time, comparing checkpoints of the same model on the same eval set, detecting distribution shift, and fitting scaling laws — all cases where the tokenizer, corpus, and context length are held fixed by construction.

Definition

Reading ML Math Notation

A symbol-by-symbol decoding of probability and expectation notation, built around one real objective — the KL-constrained RLHF formula from the DPO page.

The formula we're decoding

This is the RLHF objective from the DPO page. It looks dense, but it is built from only a handful of reusable pieces. We'll learn each piece, then read the whole thing in plain English at the end.

\[ \max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,r(x,y)\,\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \]

\(r(x,y)\)"the reward of completion \(y\) for prompt \(x\)"§1

\(\mathrm{KL}(\,\cdot\,\|\,\cdot\,)\)"how far apart two distributions are"§7

1. Functions and their arguments

The most basic notation: \(f(x)\) means "the function \(f\) applied to input \(x\)." A function is just a rule that maps inputs to an output.

  • \(r(x,y)\) — a function of two arguments. Feed it a prompt \(x\) and a completion \(y\); it returns a single number (the reward).
  • \(\beta\) — a lone Greek letter with no parentheses is a constant: one fixed number you set ahead of time, like a hyperparameter. Multiplying by \(\beta\) just scales whatever follows.
Greek letters are a convention, not magic. Lowercase letters near the start (\(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\)) are usually scalar knobs; \(\pi,\theta,\phi\) usually name models or their parameters; \(\sigma\) often means the sigmoid or a standard deviation. Context decides.

2. The conditional bar \(\;\mid\;\)

In \(\pi(y \mid x)\), the vertical bar reads "given." The whole thing is "the probability of \(y\) given \(x\)" — i.e. how likely the model is to produce completion \(y\) when it has already seen prompt \(x\).

\[ \underbrace{\pi}_{\text{the model}}(\,\underbrace{y}_{\text{what we ask about}} \mid \underbrace{x}_{\text{what is fixed/known}}\,) \]

Everything left of the bar is the thing whose probability we want; everything right of the bar is treated as fixed background. For a language model: \(x\) is the prompt (given), \(y\) is a candidate response (we score its probability).

3. Distributions and the \(\;\cdot\;\) placeholder

A probability distribution assigns a probability to every possible outcome. Two notations show up in the formula:

Calligraphic letters like \(\mathcal{D}\)

\(\mathcal{D}\) (script "D") names a distribution — here the data distribution, the population of prompts you might encounter. You don't compute with \(\mathcal{D}\) directly; you draw samples from it.

The dot \(\cdot\) as "the whole distribution"

Compare two things:

  • \(\pi(y \mid x)\) — a single number: the probability of one specific \(y\).
  • \(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\) — the entire distribution over all possible \(y\). The dot is a blank standing in for "any value," so \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) means "the whole probability function, for every \(y\), given \(x\)."
Think of \(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\) as the full bar chart of probabilities over every completion, while \(\pi(y\mid x)\) is the height of one single bar. We need the whole chart when we sample from it (§4) or compare it against another chart (§7).

4. Sampling: the tilde \(\;\sim\;\)

\(x \sim \mathcal{D}\) reads "\(x\) is drawn from (sampled according to) the distribution \(\mathcal{D}\)." It tells you \(x\) is random, and which distribution governs how often each value appears.

  • \(x \sim \mathcal{D}\) — pick a prompt at random from the data.
  • \(y \sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)\) — having fixed that \(x\), let the model generate a completion \(y\) according to its own probabilities. (Note it samples from the whole distribution \(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\) — hence the dot.)

When you see \(\sim\), ask: "which symbol is the random one, and what's its distribution?" Everything else in the expression is, for the moment, held fixed.

5. Expectation \(\;\mathbb{E}\;\)

\(\mathbb{E}\) (blackboard-bold "E") is the expected value — a probability-weighted average. The subscript says what is random; the brackets hold the quantity being averaged.

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\big[\, f(x) \,\big] \;=\; \sum_{x} \underbrace{\mathcal{D}(x)}_{\text{how likely } x \text{ is}} \cdot \underbrace{f(x)}_{\text{value at } x} \qquad\Big(\text{or } \int \mathcal{D}(x)\,f(x)\,dx \text{ if continuous}\Big) \]

In words: run \(f\) on every possible \(x\), weight each result by how probable that \(x\) is, and add them up. Likely values count more; rare values count less.

Concrete example

A fair die, \(f(x)=x\) (the face value). Each face has probability \(1/6\):

\[ \mathbb{E}[x] = \tfrac16(1) + \tfrac16(2) + \cdots + \tfrac16(6) = 3.5 \]

The "expected" value 3.5 is the long-run average — even though you can never roll a 3.5. That's the key intuition: expectation is an average, not a prediction of any single outcome.

Our formula stacks two sources of randomness in the subscript:

\[ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\; y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\, r(x,y) \,\big] \]

Read it as a nested process: first draw a prompt \(x\) from the data, then draw a completion \(y\) from the model given that \(x\), evaluate the reward \(r(x,y)\), and average over many such draws. It's the model's average reward.

6. Maximizing over a function: \(\;\max_{\pi}\;\)

\(\max_{\pi}\, g(\pi)\) means "the largest value \(g\) can take as we vary \(\pi\)." The subscript names what we're allowed to change.

The twist here: \(\pi\) is not a number — it's an entire policy (a whole probability distribution over completions, for every prompt). So we are searching over the space of all possible policies for the one that scores highest. The closely related \(\arg\max_\pi\) returns the winning \(\pi\) itself rather than the score.

In practice \(\pi\) is a neural network with parameters \(\theta\), so "varying \(\pi\)" really means "adjusting \(\theta\) by gradient steps." The notation states the goal; training is how we approximately reach it.

7. KL divergence \(\;\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q)\;\)

The double bar \(\|\) separates two distributions being compared. \(\mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q)\) measures how different distribution \(p\) is from distribution \(q\) — a kind of (asymmetric) distance.

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \;=\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}\!\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] \;=\; \sum_x p(x)\,\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \]

Properties worth memorizing:

  • \(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \ge 0\) always, and \(= 0\) exactly when \(p\) and \(q\) are identical.
  • It is asymmetric: \(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q) \neq \mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)\) in general. Order matters.
  • Notice it is itself an expectation (§5) of a log-ratio — the same log-ratio that reappears as the implicit reward on the DPO page.

In the objective, \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x))\) measures how far the trained policy's distribution has drifted from the reference model's distribution — for a given prompt \(x\). Subtracting \(\beta\) times this penalizes drifting too far.

8. Reading the whole formula

Now reassemble. Each labeled piece maps to a clause of one English sentence:

\[ \underbrace{\max_{\pi}}_{\text{(6)}}\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D},\, y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}}_{\text{(3,4,5)}} \big[\,\underbrace{r(x,y)}_{\text{(1)}}\,\big] \;-\; \underbrace{\beta}_{\text{(1)}}\, \underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\!\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)}_{\text{(2,7)}} \]
In plain English

"Find the policy \(\pi\) that makes its average reward as large as possible — where the average is taken over prompts drawn from the data and completions the policy generates for them — while not drifting too far from the reference model, with \(\beta\) controlling how much that drift is penalized."

Two competing forces, traded off by \(\beta\): the expectation term pulls the policy toward high-reward behavior; the KL term tethers it to the reference so it stays fluent and doesn't exploit the reward. That tension is the heart of RLHF — and the closed-form solution to this exact objective is what makes the DPO derivation possible.

Cheat sheet

\(f(x)\)

Function applied to input \(x\)

\(p(y\mid x)\)

Probability of \(y\) given \(x\)

\(p(\cdot \mid x)\)

The whole distribution, not one value

\(\mathcal{D}\)

A named distribution (script letter)

\(x \sim p\)

\(x\) is randomly drawn from \(p\)

\(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p}[f]\)

Probability-weighted average of \(f\)

\(\max_\pi\)

Optimize over the function/policy \(\pi\)

\(\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)\)

How far \(p\) is from \(q\); \(\ge 0\), asymmetric

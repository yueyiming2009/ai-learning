Blackwell — B200 & B300 for ML

What actually changed from Hopper, and how each change moves a decision you already make: FP4 microscaling in the 2nd-generation Transformer Engine, the dual-die “one GPU,” and NVL72 turning a whole rack into a single NVLink domain.

Blackwell is NVIDIA’s post-Hopper datacenter architecture, shipping as the B200 (2024) and the B300 / “Blackwell Ultra” (2025). For ML it is less a clock bump than three structural changes, each of which relaxes a constraint the rest of these notes treat as fixed on the H100. Throughout we use the H100 SXM (see GPU Hardware) as the baseline. Figures below are B200 unless noted.

1 · Three changes that matter (and what each moves)

Ignore the marketing spec sheet for a moment. Almost everything ML-relevant about Blackwell reduces to three levers, each pulling on a number you already reason about elsewhere in these notes:

2 · The dual-die “one GPU” and HBM3e capacity

A single die is capped by the reticle limit — the largest pattern a lithography stepper can print. Hopper was already near it. Blackwell sidesteps the wall by putting two reticle-sized dies in one package, joined by the NV-HBI interconnect at 10 TB/s die-to-die, and exposing them to software as a single CUDA device with one unified memory space. You do not program two GPUs; you program one, and the runtime keeps the dies coherent.

The 10 TB/s link is the reason this works: it is roughly 5–6× the per-GPU NVLink bandwidth and over an order of magnitude above the network, so cross-die traffic stays cheap enough to hide. It sits at the very top of the bandwidth pyramid from the GPU Hardware page, just below on-die SRAM.

Why the capacity bump matters
192 GB (B200) / 288 GB (B300) of HBM3e vs. 80 GB on H100 — 2.4–3.6× the on-package memory.

Capacity is the quietly decisive number. It sets the KV-cache budget (longer context and bigger batches before you spill), and it shrinks the GPU count needed to hold a model’s weights — which in turn shrinks the parallelism degrees you need (fewer GPUs, less collective traffic). The bandwidth, ~8 TB/s vs. 3.35 TB/s, lifts the memory roof that decode lives under (see §3).

3 · FP4 & microscaling — the inference headline

The 2nd-generation Transformer Engine adds FP4 (and FP6) next to Hopper’s FP8. Four bits buys huge bandwidth and math savings — but a raw 4-bit float (E2M1: 1 sign, 2 exponent, 1 mantissa) has only 16 levels and almost no dynamic range, so naïve FP4 destroys accuracy. The fix is microscaling: split a tensor into small contiguous blocks and give each block its own scale factor, so the limited 4-bit range is re-centered on the local magnitude.

FormatElementBlockPer-block scaleNote
MXFP4 (OCP)E2M1 (4-bit)32 valuesE8M0 (power-of-two)open standard, coarse scale
NVFP4 (NVIDIA)E2M1 (4-bit)16 valuesFP8 E4M3 + per-tensor FP32finer blocks + higher-precision scale ⇒ better accuracy

The payoff splits along the same line as the rest of inference — the prefill/decode compute model:

  • Decode is memory-bandwidth-bound. Halving bytes per weight vs. FP8 roughly doubles the TPOT-limited throughput — you stream half the bytes per generated token.
  • Prefill and training matmuls are compute-bound. Blackwell’s FP4 tensor cores deliver on the order of 2× the FP8 math throughput, i.e. a higher compute ceiling.
FP4 ceiling (~2×) BF16 ceiling

FP4 raises the compute roof on the same HBM bandwidth diagonal. x = arithmetic intensity (FLOP/byte), y = achievable throughput (normalized). At decode-level intensity (far left) both curves sit on the shared diagonal — there FP4’s win is moving fewer bytes per token, not more FLOP/s. The raised ceiling pays off for the compute-bound matmuls on the right (prefill, training). FP8 sits between the two lines.

FP4 is not free accuracy. Activation outliers and sensitive layers (e.g. the LM head, some attention projections) are usually kept at FP8/BF16, and quality depends on calibration and block granularity. The numerics — how the block scales are chosen and why NVFP4 beats INT4 — live on the Quantization page.

4 · NVL72 — the rack as one NVLink domain

5th-generation NVLink doubles per-GPU bandwidth to 1.8 TB/s. The structural change is what it enables: in a GB200 NVL72 rack, NVLink Switch trays wire 72 GPUs into a single, all-to-all NVLink domain (~130 TB/s aggregate) — every GPU talks to every other at NVLink speed, not just the 8 in a box.

That breaks an assumption baked across these notes. On H100 the NVLink domain is the node — about 8 GPUs — so AllReduce-heavy tensor parallelism stops at one node, and collectives fall off a bandwidth cliff past it. NVL72 raises that ceiling from 8 to 72.

What a 72-GPU NVLink domain unlocks
  • Tensor parallelism past 8. TP can run 16- or 32-way on NVLink before the exposed AllReduce forces you onto a slower axis — the TP↔PP transition moves out.
  • Expert parallelism for a whole MoE. A large model’s experts can sit inside one NVLink domain, so the AlltoAll dispatch stays on NVLink instead of crossing the network — often the difference that makes wide-EP serving practical.
  • Fewer, bigger collectives. More of the parallelism plan fits on the fast fabric, so the cross-node network carries less of the critical path.
Re-read Tensor Parallelism §6 “Why TP stops at one node” with NVL72 in mind: the math is unchanged, but “one node” is now up to 72 GPUs, not 8.

5 · B200 vs. B300 (Blackwell Ultra)

B300 is a refresh of the same architecture, not a new one. The differences are capacity and low-precision throughput, both aimed at reasoning and long-context inference — where more KV-cache headroom and more FP4 for test-time compute pay off directly.

6 · What it changes in your decisions

Quantization

FP4 becomes the default for throughput-bound decode; revisit the FP8 recipe and add per-block scaling.

Parallelism

TP/EP are no longer capped at ~8 GPUs — replan the degree split for a 72-GPU NVLink domain.

Memory

192–288 GB/GPU means larger KV budgets and fewer GPUs to hold a model — and smaller parallelism degrees.

Roofline

A new FP4 compute ceiling sits above the FP8/BF16 lines on the same — now higher — HBM bandwidth roof.

GPU Interconnect

The two networks a training GPU lives in: NVLink into a non-blocking NVSwitch crossbar inside the node, and a rail-optimized InfiniBand/Ethernet fat-tree across nodes — with a ~10× bandwidth cliff at the boundary. What a crossbar really is, how NVSwitch builds one, why GPUDirect RDMA matters, and how the two tiers dictate where each parallelism axis runs.

Inside the chip, the bandwidth ladder ran from registers down to HBM. This page continues down the same ladder, off the package: the NVLink / NVSwitch crossbar that joins 8 GPUs into a node, the scale-out fabric that joins nodes into a cluster, and the mapping from both onto the \(T \approx V/W\) parallelism math they physically ground. The H100 HGX node stays the running example; how Blackwell’s NVL72 moves these boundaries is on GPU Generations.

1 · Inside a node: NVLink and the NVSwitch crossbar

Eight H100s in a server sit on a shared carrier board (the HGX/DGX baseboard) and are wired together not by a ring or a mesh but by a set of NVSwitch chips forming a non-blocking all-to-all crossbar. NVLink is NVIDIA’s point-to-point GPU link; each H100 drives 18 of them (generation 4) — 900 GB/s bidirectional (≈450 GB/s each way), fanned out across the 4 switch chips — and, crucially, every GPU can push that full bandwidth to every other GPU at the same time. No shared bottleneck, no notion of “distance” inside the node.

▸ An 8-GPU node: every GPU reaches every other at full bandwidth
NVSwitch is a crossbar, not a ring — there are no nearest neighbors.

This is the single most important fact for tensor parallelism: a TP group can shard a layer across these 8 GPUs and pay only the NVLink-speed AllReduce, with no penalty for which GPU talks to which. But “non-blocking crossbar” is doing a lot of work in that sentence — it’s worth unpacking.

What “crossbar” actually means

A crossbar is the simplest fabric that connects every input to every output. The mental model is a literal grid: inputs run horizontally, outputs run vertically, and at each intersection sits a switch (a crosspoint). To connect input \(i\) to output \(j\), you close crosspoint \((i,j)\). Two properties make it the gold standard:

  • Any-to-any, one hop. There is no notion of “neighbor” or “distance” — every pair is directly reachable. (Contrast a ring/torus, where reaching a far node costs multiple hops.)
  • Non-blocking. All \(N\) inputs can be connected to \(N\) outputs at once — any permutation — each at full link rate, because no two connections share any wire in the middle.

The only limit is at the endpoints: two inputs can’t both drive one output at full rate (an output-port conflict). So the fabric never blocks — but a single GPU’s own link bandwidth still caps how much can flow into it. Drag a new permutation and notice all four paths stay full-rate simultaneously:

How NVSwitch really builds one

A real NVSwitch is not a grid of physical crosspoints you close — it is a packet router that recreates the crossbar’s guarantees in logic. Traffic crosses the chip as NVLink packets, and every cycle the switch decides afresh which inputs connect to which outputs. Two mechanisms make that behave like the ideal grid above:

  • Per-destination queues at each input. A packet waiting on a busy output never blocks the packets queued behind it that are headed elsewhere — one congested destination can’t stall the rest.
  • A per-cycle arbiter. Each cycle it pairs waiting inputs with free outputs, one-to-one, so every cycle carries a full permutation — the “all \(N\) connections at once, full rate” property of the ideal crossbar, re-established millions of times per second.

Why the crossbar stops at the node

A crossbar costs \(O(N^2)\) crosspoints, so you cannot build a 10,000-port one — the silicon and wiring blow up. The universal fix is to compose small crossbars into a near-non-blocking fat-tree (Clos network), trading a little blocking and \(O(\log N)\) hops for \(O(N\log N)\) cost. Crossbars are the atom at every tier: one node is a single crossbar (NVSwitch); the InfiniBand network in §2 is a fat-tree of crossbar switch chips. Stepping from one crossbar to a fat-tree of them is exactly the bandwidth cliff at the node boundary — and the reason tensor parallelism is capped at one node.

2 · Across nodes: the scale-out network

A GPU lives in two networks, and “going across nodes” means dropping from the first to the second:

  • Scale-up — the NVLink domain: the fast, non-blocking NVSwitch crossbar. On a classic HGX H100 node that’s 8 GPUs; on a GB200 NVL72 rack it’s 72 GPUs. No cliff inside this domain — it’s all ~450 GB/s/GPU.
  • Scale-out — the data-center fabric (InfiniBand or Ethernet) connecting NVLink domains to each other. This is the slow tier, ~50 GB/s/GPU.

So the boundary that matters isn’t literally “the node” — it’s the edge of the NVLink domain. Cross it and you’re on the scale-out network, built from three things:

  • One NIC per GPU. The NIC (network interface card) is the endpoint that puts a GPU’s bytes onto the datacenter network. A DGX H100 carries eight ConnectX-7 NICs at 400 Gb/s (≈50 GB/s), one dedicated to each GPU — a ratio that holds on NVL72 too.
  • GPU and NIC under the same PCIe switch — the on-node PCIe hub both devices hang off — so the GPU reaches the wire without crossing the CPU.
  • A fabric of switches wiring all the NICs together (next).

The topology: a rail-optimized fat-tree

The switches form a fat-tree (Clos network) — the same crossbars-of-crossbars from §1: each switch (e.g. an NVIDIA Quantum-2, 64×400 Gb/s) is a crossbar, composed into a near-non-blocking tree with full bisection bandwidth (split the cluster into any two halves, and the tree can carry both halves talking to each other at full rate). Nodes attach to leaf switches; leaves connect through spine switches; the largest clusters add a third core tier.

The GPU-specific twist is rail optimization: with 8 GPUs/8 NICs per node, NIC \(i\) of every node connects to the same “rail” (leaf switch \(i\)) — so there are 8 parallel rails. GPU \(i\) on any node reaches GPU \(i\) on any other node by staying on its rail — one leaf-switch hop, never touching the contended spine. Cross-rail traffic either climbs to the spine or first hops over NVLink to the right-rail GPU inside the node; how NCCL schedules collectives around this is its own page.

The data path, and why GPUDirect RDMA matters

With the pieces named, trace one transfer end to end — GPU A on one node to GPU B on another:

GPU A HBM → PCIe switch → NIC A → optics → leaf switch → [spine] → leaf switch → NIC B → PCIe switch → GPU B HBM

What makes this fast is a pair of ideas. RDMA (remote direct memory access) is the transport primitive: the NICs themselves move the bytes from one machine’s memory into another’s — the sender posts a transfer, the receiving NIC writes the payload straight to its destination address, and neither side’s CPU or operating system touches the data path. GPUDirect RDMA extends the memory a NIC can reach from host RAM to GPU HBM: the NIC reads and writes GPU memory directly over PCIe — no CPU copy, no staging in system RAM. Without it, every inter-node byte would detour through host memory and the effective bandwidth would collapse. (The GPU↔NIC PCIe Gen5 ×16 link is ~64 GB/s, which is why a 50 GB/s NIC fits comfortably under it.)

InfiniBand vs Ethernet

Two transport flavors carry this: InfiniBand (NVIDIA Quantum) — lossless, RDMA-native, with in-network reduction (SHARP), the default in most large training clusters — and RoCE / Ethernet (NVIDIA Spectrum-X) — RDMA over Ethernet, needing careful tuning to stay lossless, favored by some hyperscalers. Either way the transport is RDMA: GPU memory to GPU memory without the CPUs in the loop.

The cliff at the NVLink-domain boundary
NVLink ~450 GB/s → network ~50 GB/s   ≈   ~9–10× less bandwidth per GPU

Everything about multi-node parallelism is organized around hiding this drop. The collectives that must cross it — the gradient AllReduce of data parallelism, the activation sends of pipeline parallelism — are chosen either to be rare (once per step) or to overlap with compute, so the slow fabric is never on the exposed critical path. The chatty, per-layer collectives stay inside the NVLink domain. And the modern trend — NVL72 growing that domain from 8 to 72 GPUs — is precisely about moving this cliff outward so more of your parallelism enjoys scale-up bandwidth.

4 · Summary

Crossbar in, cliff out

NVSwitch gives all-to-all ~450 GB/s within 8 GPUs; the network drops to ~50 GB/s across nodes.

Crossbar

Any-to-any in one hop, every permutation at full rate, no shared wire — the gold-standard fabric, at \(O(N^2)\) cost.

NVSwitch reality

Packet-switched: per-destination queues + a per-cycle arbiter emulate the crossbar; the SHARP ALU sums and multicasts — AllReduce in the switch.

GPUDirect RDMA

The NIC DMAs GPU HBM directly, no CPU or host-RAM staging — what makes ~50 GB/s/GPU actually reachable across nodes.

Rails

NIC \(i\) of every node hangs off leaf \(i\): same-rank traffic stays on its rail, one hop, off the contended spine.

Two-tier \(W\)

Chatty collectives (TP) stay on NVLink; rare or overlappable ones (DP, PP) cross the network — the layering behind every parallelism plan.

# Asynchronous RL for LLM Post-Training

Math, algorithms, and training-inference mismatch for async reinforcement learning. Companion to the [PPO training walkthrough](ppo_training_walkthrough.md) which covers the synchronous baseline.

---

## 1. The Synchronous Bottleneck

In synchronous on-policy RL, generation and training alternate in strict lockstep:

```
rollout → [wait for slowest trajectory] → compute rewards → train → repeat
```

The wait is expensive. Autoregressive generation is sequential — each token requires a full forward pass — so rollout latency scales with response length. A 1024-token response can take seconds; a training step on the resulting batch takes milliseconds. All GPUs idle at the generation↔training boundary.

For short responses this is tolerable. For long-horizon reasoning (multi-step tool use, code, math) where rollouts run 2000–8000 tokens, the sync wait dominates.

---

## 2. Async RL: Decoupled Loops

Async RL breaks the lockstep. Rollout workers and the trainer run as independent, continuously-executing services:

```
rollout pool:   [gen] → [gen] → [gen] → ...      (fills a sample buffer)
trainer:        [step] → [step] → [step] → ...   (drains the buffer)
```

The trainer never waits for generation to finish; the rollout pool never waits for a training step to complete. Measured speedup: **2–3× over synchronous pipelines** on real workloads (e.g., Inseek prototype: 2h24m → 1h24m).

The cost: by the time a rollout sample reaches the trainer, the policy has been updated $K$ times. The sample is now **off-policy** — it was generated by $\pi_{old}$ but will be used to update $\pi_{new}$.

---

## 3. Policy Lag and Steady State

### Definition

**Policy lag $K$**: the number of trainer steps by which the current training policy $\pi_{new}$ leads the rollout policy $\pi_{old}$ that generated a given sample.

- $K = 0$: on-policy (synchronous)
- $K = 1$: sample generated by the policy one step behind current
- $K = K_{max}$: the discard threshold — samples older than this are dropped

### Steady-State Lag

In a running async system, the lag stabilizes when the rollout pool's production rate matches the trainer's consumption rate. The steady-state lag is:

$$K_{steady} \approx \frac{T_{rollout}}{T_{train}} \times \frac{N_{rollout}}{N_{train}}$$

where $T_{rollout}$ and $T_{train}$ are the per-step latencies and $N_{rollout}$, $N_{train}$ are the number of workers in each pool.

Derivation: at steady state, the number of training steps completed while one rollout is in flight is $T_{rollout} / T_{train}$. With $N_{rollout}$ rollout workers running in parallel, the effective lag per sample scales by $N_{rollout} / N_{train}$.

### Two Regimes

| Condition | Effect |
|---|---|
| $K_{max} < K_{steady}$ | Trainer stalls — rollout can't produce fast enough to keep the buffer full |
| $K_{max} \geq K_{steady}$ | Trainer never waits; lag accumulates toward $K_{max}$ — stability depends on IS correction at that lag |

The throughput gain from async is maximized by raising $K_{max}$. The stability loss from doing so is the central problem.

## 4. Off-Policy Correction via Importance Sampling

### The IS Objective

The on-policy policy gradient objective is:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\bigl[\sum_t A_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\bigr]$$

When $\tau$ was generated by a behavior policy $\pi_b \neq \pi_\theta$, reweight by the importance ratio to remain unbiased:

$$J_{IS}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_b}\bigl[w(\tau) \sum_t A_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\bigr]$$

In async RL, $\pi_b = \pi_{old}$ (the rollout policy at generation time) and $\pi_\theta = \pi_{new}$ (current training policy).

### Token-Level IS

Apply IS correction independently per token:

$$w_{tok}(\tau) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\pi_{new}(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)}$$

This is the standard PPO formulation — the clipped surrogate loss bounds each per-token ratio $r_t = \pi_{new}(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)$ individually.

**Problem at high lag:** each factor deviates slightly from 1; the product of $T$ factors (e.g., $T = 512$) has variance that grows exponentially with sequence length. Even small per-token deviations compound into extreme trajectory-level weights. Clipping/masking (§5) controls the damage but introduces bias that **grows with compute scale** — it cannot be fixed by increasing batch size.

### Sequence-Level IS

Treat the entire trajectory as the unit of reweighting:

$$w_{seq}(\tau) = \frac{\pi_{new}(\tau)}{\pi_{old}(\tau)} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} \frac{\pi_{new}(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)}$$

The formula is mathematically identical to $w_{tok}$, but it is applied as a **single scalar weight on the trajectory-level objective** rather than per-token:

$$J_{seq}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_b}\bigl[w_{seq}(\tau) \cdot \hat{A}(\tau) \cdot \sum_t \log \pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)\bigr]$$

**Why it's more robust:** unbiased under any lag (no clipping distorts the correction); immune to the token-level product's structural bias. The cost is high variance — $w_{seq}$ has even wider distribution than any individual $r_t$. Mitigations:

- **Length normalization**: use $\exp(\log w_{seq} / T)$ in the loss — equivalent to the geometric mean per-token ratio; keeps the weight in a tractable range without introducing bias
- **Large batch**: variance of $w_{seq}$ decreases as $1/N$ over batch size $N$ — the sequence IS estimator is consistent but needs larger $N$ to dominate

This is what GSPO (Qwen) and VeRL's `rollout_is=sequence` implement.

### Key Comparison

| | Token-level IS | Sequence-level IS |
|---|---|---|
| Bias under lag | Structurally biased (clipping) — grows with scale | Unbiased (no clipping) |
| Variance | High per-token but product has extreme tails | High trajectory-level; controlled by batch size |
| Stability at high $K$ | Collapses; can't be fixed by batch size | Holds longer; needs larger batch to dominate variance |
| Common use | PPO `ratio * advantage` per token | GSPO, `rollout_is=sequence` in VeRL |

**Low-bias compute-scaling hypothesis** (Luke Huang): token-level IS is faster at small scale but hits a ceiling; sequence-level IS is slower to start but improves with scale. Long-horizon tasks raise the batch-size requirement for sequence IS, making the problem worse.

## 5. IS Stabilization Algorithms

All of these trade bias for variance to prevent gradient explosions from large IS ratios. All fail at very high policy lag — they delay collapse, not prevent it.

### TIS / CISPO — Truncated IS

Clip per-token ratios to a bounded range:

$$\tilde{r}_t = \min\bigl(r_t,\, c\bigr) \quad \text{where } r_t = \pi_{new}(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)$$

Equivalently (one-sided): cut off outliers above a threshold $c$. This is the same operation as PPO's upper clip on the ratio, applied in the IS reweighting context rather than the surrogate loss.

VeRL exposes this via `rollout_is=token` with the clip threshold. Also studied as CISPO (Conservative IS Policy Optimization).

### MIS / IcePop — Masked IS

Drop entire samples whose trajectory-level IS ratio exceeds a threshold rather than clipping:

$$w = w_{seq}(\tau) \cdot \mathbf{1}[w_{seq}(\tau) \leq c]$$

Cleaner than clipping (no gradient from extreme samples at all) but wastes data. IcePop is the DeepSeek / Minimax variant.

### M2PO — Masked Multi-step PO

Iteratively mask individual tokens (not full trajectories) until the remaining sample's variance satisfies a constraint. Finer-grained than MIS — keeps most of the sample, drops the specific high-ratio tokens.

### DeepSeek Masking

A heuristic variant: mask out negative-advantage tokens with large log-ratios $\log \pi_{new} / \pi_{old}$. Motivated by the observation that large negative-advantage updates with big ratios produce destabilizing large-magnitude gradients. Implemented in DeepSeek's async RL system.

## 6. Training-Inference Mismatch (TIM)

Even without policy lag, a subtler source of off-policy data exists: the rollout engine (vLLM/SGLang) and the training engine (FSDP/Megatron) **produce different log-probabilities on identical weights and tokens**. This silently converts "on-policy" into off-policy without any lag at all.

The importance ratio $\pi_{new}(a_t|s_t) / \pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)$ is supposed to be 1 for on-policy data. TIM makes it deviate from 1 by construction.

### Root Causes

**1. Batch-size-dependent accumulation order.** Standard GPU CUDA matmul and reduction operations accumulate floating-point additions in an order that depends on the batch size and thread layout. Different batch sizes → different rounding → different log-probs on identical weights.

**2. TP-size-dependent accumulation.** The training engine typically uses FSDP (data-parallel, no tensor parallelism within a node) while the rollout engine uses tensor parallelism across GPUs. All-reduce operations in TP introduce rounding in a different order → log-prob divergence even for the same batch size.

**3. MoE routing instability.** For MoE models, two separate forward passes (one in rollout, one in training) can route tokens to different experts — especially early in training when routing is unstable. Different experts → completely different log-probs.

**4. Tokenization drift (TITO).** If the rollout and training engines apply slightly different token preprocessing, the sequences diverge before any computation.

### Diagnosing TIM

Standard aggregate KL $\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_{old} || \pi_{new})$ **hides** early-stage TIM failure — the aggregate averages over both aligned and misaligned tokens, masking local failures. Better signals:

- **Sign-imbalanced loss distortion**: after PPO clipping, if the ratio is wrong in a systematic direction, losses are distorted in a way that's visible as one-sided bias in the clipped-surrogate gradient
- **`pg_clipfrac`** (PPO policy-gradient clip fraction): the fraction of tokens whose ratio $r_t$ hits the PPO clip bound. Should be low when on-policy; TIM pushes it up even without lag
- **Pearson correlation** between rollout log-probs and training log-probs across tokens: near 1.0 = aligned; falling correlation = engines diverging

### Three Fix Families

**Eliminate — make engines bitwise-identical:**
- **Batch-invariant kernels**: rewrite RMSNorm, matmul, log_softmax to accumulate in a deterministic order regardless of batch size (Thinking Machines; DeepSeek V4)
- **TP-size determinism (TBIK)**: make the training engine produce identical results whether using TP=1 or TP=N (the open part — FSDP vs multi-GPU TP all-reduce still diverges at scale)
- **Routing replay (R3)**: record expert assignments during rollout and force the training forward pass to use the same routing
- **TITO**: align tokenization preprocessing between engines

**Correct — algorithmic IS patch (accepts the mismatch, reweights):**
- **Token-level TIS** (VeRL `rollout_correction`): one-sided ratio clip to bound the per-token mismatch
- **Sequence-level IS (GSPO)**: immune to MoE expert-activation volatility; removes the need for routing replay for MoE models; generalizes `rollout_is=sequence`
- **Decoupled PPO**: split the proximal policy (used for clipping) from the behavior policy (used for IS), so the clip bound and IS correction are no longer conflated
- **Bypass old-logprobs**: use rollout log-probs directly (skip the recompute pass that introduces the mismatch); on-policy only

**Precision — reduce the gap:**
- **FP16 over BF16**: BF16 has 7 mantissa bits vs FP16's 10. Over a 512-token autoregressive decode, rounding errors accumulate per step — FP16 cuts the accumulated mismatch ~24× and outperforms the best IS patch (~99% vs ~95% on MATH500). Cheapest thing to try first.
- **FP32 LM head**: older trick; superseded by FP16 for the full model
- **FP8 rollout with IS correction**: quantized rollout cuts latency but widens the gap — pair with TIS

### MILES / slime: the Eliminate Camp in Practice

MILES (the approach in slime and the SGLang deterministic inference work) commits fully to the eliminate family: make SGLang and FSDP/Megatron bitwise-identical for the full forward pass. IS ratio ≡ 1 by construction — no correction needed. IS is retained only as a fallback for edge cases where bitwise identity can't be guaranteed (e.g., TP-size boundary).

The differentiating claim: eliminating the mismatch is strictly better than correcting for it, because IS correction itself introduces variance and potential bias. The open question is whether bitwise identity can be guaranteed at scale across all TP configurations.

## 7. Async VeRL: Three Knobs

VeRL's fully-async mode controls the staleness tradeoff with three config parameters:

| Knob | What it controls |
|---|---|
| `trigger_parameter_sync_step` | How often the trainer pushes fresh weights to rollout workers (every $N$ steps). Smaller $N$ → fresher policy, more sync overhead. |
| `staleness_threshold` | Max age (in policy versions) before a sample is discarded. `0` ≈ synchronous; higher → tolerate more off-policy data. |
| `partial_rollout` | Whether unfinished rollouts are saved and resumed across a weight update (vs. thrown away). Saves long in-flight rollouts; the resumed half is from the older policy. |

**`pg_clipfrac` as a staleness gauge**: as the rollout policy falls behind the training policy (increasing $K$), more IS ratios fall outside the PPO clip range → `pg_clipfrac` rises. A rising clipfrac is a signal the sync cadence or staleness threshold is too permissive.

**Pearson correlation as a staleness metric**: measures alignment between $\log \pi_{old}(a_t|s_t)$ and $\log \pi_{current}(a_t|s_t)$ across all tokens in a batch. Correlation near 1.0 = policies still aligned (IS ratios near 1); falling correlation = diverging. Scale-invariant: captures relative ordering of log-prob mass across tokens, which is what actually keeps IS ratios bounded.
